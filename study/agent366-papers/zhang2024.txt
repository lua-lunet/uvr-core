   25  Abstract
   26  SSD failures have an increasing impact on storage reliability and performance in data centers. Some manufacturers
   27  have customized fine-grained Telemetry attributes to analyze
   28  and identify SSD failures. Based on Telemetry data, this paper proposes the mutation similarity based failure rating and
   29  diagnosis (MSFRD) scheme to predict failures in dynamic
   30  environment of data centers and improve failure handling
   31  efficiency. MSFRD dynamically detects the internal mutations of SSDs in real time and measures their similarity to
   32  the mutations of historical failed SSDs and healthy SSDs for
   33  failure prediction and early rating. Based on the rating, unavailable SSDs with serious failures are handled immediately,
   34  while available SSDs with less serious failures will be continuously tracked and diagnosed. The MSFRD is evaluated
   35  on real Telemetry datasets collected from large-scale SSDs in
   36  data centers. Compared with the existing schemes, MSFRD
   37  improves precision by 23.8% and recall by 38.9% on average
   38  for failure prediction. The results also show the effectiveness
   39  of MSFRD on failure rating and progressive diagnosis.

   40  1 Introduction

   41  Nowadays, with the development of the Internet, the scale of
   42  data is growing exponentially and storage plays a more and
   43  more crucial role in computer systems. NAND flash-based
   44  solid state drives (SSDs) have better performance and lower
   45  power consumption than hard disk drives (HDDs), and are
   46  increasingly used in data centers. However, as the storage
   47  density of SSDs increases, the durability and reliability are
   48  decreasing [18,21], which poses a challenge to the storage
   49  reliability of large data centers with even millions of SSDs.
   50  SSD failures have received more and more attention due to
   51  the following two impacts. First, although passive failure tolerance mechanisms (such as replication [33] and RAID [26])
   52  are used to avoid data loss, SSD failures would cause instability in online services, such as jitter performance and long-tail
   53  latency. Second, SSD failures bring additional maintenance
   54  costs, and failure handling may be inaccurate and result in
   55  more recovery costs. Therefore, SSD failure prediction, as a
   56  proactive failure tolerance mechanism, is a powerful supplement to passive failure tolerance mechanisms. The industry

   62  expects to reduce the impact and cost of SSD failures through
   63  early failure detection and handling (such as service migration
   64  and SSD replacement).
   65  Traditional failure prediction is mainly based on Self-
   66  Monitoring, Analysis, and Reporting Technology (SMART)
   67  originated from HDDs. Based on SMART logs, the existing HDD and SSD failure prediction schemes adopt classification or anomaly detection algorithms to distinguish
   68  failed disks from healthy disks. Specifically, classification
   69  algorithms such as random forest learn existing patterns of
   70  failed disks and healthy disks to perform binary classification
   71  [6,16,20,22,35,38,44]. Anomaly detection algorithms such
   72  as autoencoder learn the pattern of healthy disks, and identify
   73  a failed disk when its SMART data are very different from
   74  those of most healthy disks [4,7,42]. Based on these algorithms and SMART logs, HDD failure prediction has achieved
   75  high prediction accuracy [20], but SSD failure prediction has
   76  not [6]. Unlike mechanical-based HDDs, the internal mechanism of flash-based SSDs is more complicated. The SSD
   77  has more components and processes that require fine-grained
   78  monitoring. Moreover, it has various error tolerance mechanisms [15], which makes it difficult to distinguish between
   79  real failures and tolerable errors. SSD monitoring and failure
   80  prediction are still facing great challenges in industry.
   81  To enhance SSD monitoring and failure warning, some
   82  manufacturers (e.g., Samsung) have customized comprehensive Telemetry logs with more attributes to monitor SSD's
   83  internal mechanisms and components in detail. However, we
   84  find that the existing schemes face three challenges and are
   85  not fully suitable for complicated Telemetry information and
   86  dynamic environment in practice. To this end, we propose
   87  the mutation similarity based failure rating and diagnosis
   88  (MSFRD) scheme. The existing challenges and the main
   89  ideas of our solution are summarized as follows and introduced in detail in Section 3.
   90  • Changes in data importance from model training to
   91  online prediction. The existing schemes design feature engineering based on training data to obtain key information
   92  from data. For example, feature selection is usually used
   93  to select important monitoring attributes for failure prediction [4,6,22,34,38,42]. However, in practice, the training
   94  data are historical data, but actual failure prediction is per-

  147  3. We propose failure rating to predict and distinguish the

  151  2 A Look at Field Data

  152  We have two large-scale Telemetry datasets collected from the
  153  data centers of large Internet companies such as ByteDance.
  154  The Telemetry logs are the snapshots of SSD internal attributes on a regular basis (e.g., one Telemetry log per day for
  155  each SSD). The first dataset has over 41 million Telemetry
  156  logs collected from more than 120,000 SSDs over a year and
  157  a half, called the 41-M dataset in this paper. The SSDs carry
  158  various businesses and workloads. The second dataset from
  159  another company contains over 10 million Telemetry logs
  160  collected within seven months from more than 35,000 SSDs,
  161  called the 10-M dataset in this paper. These data are collected from Samsung's current data center-level PM9A3 SSD,
  162  since Samsung has customized up to 85 Telemetry attributes
  163  for PM9A3. These attributes monitor the fine-grained status
  164  and mechanisms inside the SSD. In particular, there are various error tolerant mechanisms inside SSDs to enhance their
  165  reliability. In addition to common read/write and temperaturerelated attributes, error tolerance and uncorrectable situations
  166  are also recorded. Some key Telemetry attributes are shown
  167  in Table 1.
  168  Besides Telemetry data and corresponding SSD information (serial number, model, firmware, etc.), the failure lists
  169  are also collected by the operators, including serial number

  171  handling measures. There are 1,126 and 318 records in the

  173  Many of the failed SSDs have been confirmed as unavailable

  175  resulted in online problems (e.g., performance degradation or

  242  an evaluation period is required before the model goes online. As workloads change and SSDs wear out, the patterns of
  243  monitoring data will change and some unseen data patterns

  245  will also change. Accordingly, the features selected based on

  311  Ideas. To focus on actual important attributes and abnormal
  312  changes in online data, we suggest dynamically extracting
  313  mutation information in Telemetry attributes to reflect the
  314  failure symptoms and patterns. Compared with normal slow
  315  changes, mutations are rare, sudden, and unexpected changes.
  316  If we predict future attribute trend as the expected trend based
  317  on historical trend, the prediction error (i.e., the difference
  318  between predicted trend and actual trend) for mutations will
  319  be much larger than normal changes. Therefore, we adopt the
  320  prediction errors of attribute trends as the mutation features to
  321  represent the degree of mutations. For normal slow changes,
  322  the prediction errors will be very small and the feature values
  323  tend to be zero, so they are implicitly eliminated. For mutations, there will be larger prediction errors, and the feature
  324  values will also be larger and more significant. Meanwhile,
  325  since common mutations on healthy SSDs are less important, we also recommend estimating the rarity of mutations to
  326  reflect their importance.

  327  3.2 Prediction Model

  328  Background. There are mainly two types of machine learning models for failure prediction: classification models and
  329  anomaly detection models. Most previous schemes [1,6,17,
  330  22,38, 40] treat failure prediction as a binary classification
  331  problem and use classification models such as random forest
  332  to classify healthy and failed SSDs. The classification models
  333  learn the patterns of healthy and failed SSDs at the same time,
  334  and classify them by their differences, so these models are
  335  good at distinguishing patterns seen in historical training data.

  377  be exploited to further distinguish outlier patterns.

  379  Ideas. In addition to identifying the patterns that have been
  380  seen in historical training data, unseen patterns and their tendency also need to be perceived. We adopt the idea of similarity measurement that is applicable to both classification
  381  and anomaly detection. First, the patterns that have been seen
  382  in historical training data can be effectively distinguished in
  383  prediction by measuring their similarity with historical health
  384  and failure patterns. Second, the similarity measure can capture unseen patterns which tend to be outlier, and is helpful
  385  for estimating their health/failure tendency.

  386  3.3 Failure Alarm and Handling

  387  Background. The existing schemes predict failures and take
  388  measures (e.g., disk replacement) in advance to reduce the
  389  impact of failures on online services. When failures are reported, some healthy SSDs may also be wrongly reported as
  390  failed ones. To reduce false alarms, some schemes [4,22,40]
  391  suggest using scrub technology to perform a full scan on the
  392  alarmed SSD to confirm whether it is failed. A scrubber is
  393  a background process provided by commercial storage systems, RAID, or file systems, and it detects data integrity errors
  394  through full disk scanning [22].
  395  Most previous schemes use a unified mechanism to alert
  396  and handle all failures. In practice, however, there are various
  397  failures [19,39] and the measures are also different (for example, the failure list includes the cases of replacing the SSD
  398  and not replacing the SSD). In particular, there are diverse
  399  error-tolerant mechanisms inside the SSD, which increase
  400  the reliability of the SSD, but also make it difficult to distinguish whether the SSD is really failed. Some error-tolerant
  401  mechanisms such as read retry may cause long tail latency in
  402  I/O or timeout error, thereby affecting service performance
  403  [25]. These SSDs are available but suffer performance degradation or transient error, and may also be reported in failure
  404  list. Some researchers call this event gray failure [14,39] or
  405  fail-slow [10,19]. Such failures cannot even be verified with
  406  scrub technology as they do not have data integrity issues. In
  407  general, coarse-grained failure prediction and handling cannot
  408  fully adapt to practical scenarios with various failures.
  409  Motivation and analysis. SSD failures involve various phenomena and degrees, and it is meaningful to identify finegrained failure status and take corresponding measures. Unavailable SSDs with serious failures should be predicted in

  458  4 Methodology

  459  The architecture of mutation similarity based failure rating
  460  and diagnosis (MSFRD) scheme is shown in Figure 4. Based
  461  on our motivations and ideas, the MSFRD scheme is divided
  462  into the following three parts. 1) To characterize key failure
  463  symptoms, the mutations in attribute trends are dynamically
  464  extracted. As shown in Section 3.1, the mutation means that
  465  the data trend deviates significantly from expectations, so
  466  it is less predictable than the normal trend. Therefore, we

  467  874 2024 USENIX Annual Technical Conference

  468  Figure 4: Overall architecture of MSFRD.

  469  predict the subsequent attribute trend, and calculate the error
  470  between predicted trend and actual trend to reflect the degree
  471  of mutation. Considering that failures and the corresponding
  472  mutations are generally rare, we also introduce self-learned
  473  weights in the prediction model to measure the rarity of mutations. 2) Based on the idea in Section 3.2, we adopt similarity
  474  measurement to capture both seen and unseen failure patterns.
  475  To identify the fine-grained failure status and degree, we divide historical SSDs into four levels from serious failure to
  476  perfect health, i.e., level 1 to level 4, based on the analysis
  477  in Section 3.3. For a new mutation of an SSD to be identified, the failure level of the SSD is estimated by measuring
  478  the similarity of this mutation to the mutations of failed and
  479  healthy SSDs of these four levels. 3) Measures are provided
  480  based on the failure level to reduce operation and maintenance
  481  overhead. Serious failures can be directly handled, and less
  482  serious failures would be gradually diagnosed and processed
  483  according to their similarity trend with historical SSDs, the
  484  development of mutations, and the inspection results of corresponding tools (i.e., scrubber and latency monitor). Next, we
  485  will introduce these three parts in detail.

  486  4.1 Mutation Feature Extraction

  487  We recommend dynamic mutation feature extraction due to
  488  its two benefits. 1) By dynamically extracting important mutations from data in real time instead of static feature engineering, we can adapt to the changes of data importance over time.
  489  2) By capturing the detailed mutation status of each Telemetry
  490  attribute of an SSD, we can focus on the abnormal changes
  491  that truly reflect the failure status and avoid the interference
  492  caused by normal changes.
  493  Since mutations are rare, sudden, rapid changes that deviate more from expectations than normal slow trends, the
  494  errors between predicted trends (i.e., expectations) and actual
  495  trends are adopted as mutation features. We adopt Informer
  496  model [43] to predict data trends, since it is an accurate time
  497  series prediction model derived from the widely used Transformer model [31]. First, after data preprocessing by min-max
  498  normalization, we train an Informer model with the timeseries data of large-scale historical healthy SSDs (i.e., healthy
  499  SSDs in the training set). In this way, the normal trends in

  552  4.2 Mutation Similarity based Failure Rating

  553  Based on the extracted mutation features, a machine learning
  554  based prediction model is needed to identify the detailed
  555  status of SSDs. As analyzed in Section 3.2, the unseen failure
  556  patterns would appear in future prediction in practice, and
  557  existing classification models and anomaly detection models
  558  have their own strengths and weaknesses in catching seen
  559  and unseen failure patterns. We adopt the idea of similarity
  560  measurement (i.e., k-nearest neighbor [30, 41]) that exist
  561  in these two types of models to take their advantages. The
  562  patterns seen in historical data are distinguished based on
  563  the nearest historical neighbors, and the outlier degree is also
  564  considered to make the health/failure tendency of unseen
  565  patterns more apparent.
  566  It is an option to only distinguish failed SSDs and healthy
  567  SSDs by the similarity measure of mutations. However, failed
  568  SSDs have different failure status and degrees, and available
  569  SSDs with less serious failures may not need to be replaced
  570  in practice. Therefore, failure rating is meaningful to reduce
  571  unnecessary maintenance overhead and additional impact on
  572  online services. Based on the ideas in Section 3.3, historical
  573  SSDs are automatically divided into four levels from failure
  574  (levels 1 and 2) to health (levels 3 and 4), which is reflected by
  575  SSDs' error, failure and replacement status. The four levels
  576  are defined as follows.
  577  • Level 1: serious failure, such as unavailability or data integrity failure. The failed SSDs (i.e., those in failure list)
  578  that are replaced or have uncorrectable errors are in this
  579  level.
  580  • Level 2: gray failure, such as performance degradation. The
  581  failed SSDs that are not replaced and have no uncorrectable
  582  errors are usually available SSDs with gray failures, so they
  583  are placed in this level.
  584  • Level 3: problematic health, with self warning or data issues
  585  that have no or unnoticed impact. This level includes healthy
  586  SSDs (i.e., those not in failure list) with critical warnings [8]
  587  or uncorrectable errors.
  588  • Level 4: perfect health, without any issues. The healthy
  589  SSDs without critical warnings and uncorrectable errors are
  590  in this level.

  673  4.3 Progressive Diagnosis and Processing

  674  After failure rating, the SSDs judged to be level 1 are about
  675  to face a serious failure and need to be replaced immediately,

  676  USENIX Association
=== PAGE 10 ===

  677  while the SSDs of levels 2 and 3 which would face gray failures or problems require tracking and diagnosis to confirm
  678  their impact and future status. For these SSDs, automatic diagnosis is first performed based on their subsequent similarity
  679  trend and mutation development. Based on the mutation similarity, if the SSD's level-1 score increases further, it is more
  680  likely to face serious failure. Moreover, mutations represent
  681  abnormal changes that may lead to failure. Therefore, we focus on the attribute with the largest mutation, which reflects
  682  the biggest anomaly inside the SSD. If the attribute value
  683  with the largest mutation further increases or decreases in
  684  the direction of mutation, it means that the mutation is still
  685  ongoing and the issue is getting more serious. Based on these
  686  two points, for an SSD rated at level 2 or 3, when the confidence score of level 1 further increases and the attribute value
  687  with the largest mutation continuously changes, it is further
  688  diagnosed as level 1 and needs to be processed immediately.
  689  In this way, we can catch the issues that may develop into
  690  serious failures and reduce unnecessary handling for SSDs
  691  that are no longer bad.
  692  In addition to automatic trend-based diagnosis above, we
  693  also recommend using some tools for diagnosis and treatment.
  694  Most SSDs rated at level 2 are available but may face performance issues. Therefore, the impact of SSD performance
  695  issues on online services needs to be evaluated to decide what
  696  measures to take. Since I/O latencies reflect the quality of
  697  services, and many mainstream SSDs for data centers (e.g.,
  698  Samsung PM9A3) support device-side latency tracing, monitoring latency is a good choice. By continuously monitoring
  699  the occurrence of SSD's long-tail latency, we can find SSD
  700  performance degradation in time and confirm whether it is
  701  acceptable or needs to be fixed. For SSDs rated at level 3, the
  702  main problem is data integrity, and the scrub technology with
  703  full scan is helpful for monitoring data integrity errors. In
  704  particular, the addresses of data integrity errors can be found
  705  to confirm their impact. If these errors occur accidentally at
  706  non-critical addresses and will not occur again, they may not
  707  need to be handled. Otherwise, data migration and SSD replacement are necessary. By using relevant tools to diagnose
  708  SSDs at different levels, we can clarify the fine-grained SSD
  709  status and suggest corresponding measures.

  868  5.3 Discussion on MSFRD Modules

  869  In this section, we shall discuss the effectiveness of each module or process in the proposed scheme. The following seven
  870  methods are compared on the 41-M dataset, from the baseline
  871  random forest method, to using partial MSFRD and finally
  872  the whole MSFRD. 1) Raw data + RF: The raw data are input
  873  into the trained random forest model for failure/health classification. 2) Feature selection + RF: The data after static feature
  874  selection are input into the trained random forest model for
  875  failure/health classification. It is the same as RF in Section 5.1.
  876  3) Mutation feature + RF: The dynamic mutation features are
  877  input into the trained random forest model for failure/health
  878  classification. 4) Mutation feature + similarity based failure
  879  rating (SFR): Based on the mutation feature, failed SSDs and
  880  their levels are identified based on their similarities to historical failed and healthy SSDs of four levels. 5) Mutation
  881  feature with rarity (Mutation(rarity)) + SFR: the difference
  882  from mutation feature + SFR is that the self-learned rarity
  883  weights (Equation 1 in Section 4.1) are used in similarity
  884  measure (Equation 2 in Section 4.2). 6) Mutation(rarity) +
  885  SFR with fine-tuned score (SFR(tuned)): the difference from
  886  Mutation(rarity) + SFR is that the fine-tuned confidence score
  887  (Equation 4 of Section 4.2) is used instead of base confidence
  888  score for failure prediction and rating. 7) MSFRD: coupled
  889  with automatic diagnosis (see Section 4.3) on Mutation(rarity)
  890  + SFR(tuned), the whole MSFRD is used to predict failures.

  982  6 Related Work

  983  Numerous studies have investigated and analyzed the impact
  984  of disk errors and failures on large data centers [11,12,27,28,
  985  32,36,37]. To take proactive measures before failures occur,
  986  disk failure prediction has received extensive attention. There
  987  are many studies on HDD failure prediction, since HDDs
  988  have been widely used for a long time. Most researchers use
  989  machine learning to predict HDD failures [5, 7, 16, 20, 29,
  990  35, 44], as machine learning is more accurate and flexible.
  991  Some of them have achieved high accuracy for HDD failure
  992  prediction. However, since flash-based SSDs have different
  993  storage technologies and more error-tolerant mechanisms,
  994  these previous results do not apply to SSDs [1,4].
  995  In recent years, with the large-scale use of SSDs in data
  996  centers, SSD failure prediction has received increasing attention. Most of existing studies [1,13,17,22,38,40] focus on
  997  predicting failures with classification algorithms. Based on
  998  random forest classifier, Xu et al. [38] explored the impact
  999  of different feature selection methods on prediction results.
 1000  They also combined the rankings of multiple feature selection
 1001  methods to obtain the mean ranking for better performance.
 1002  Chen et al. [6] trained multiple decision tree-based classification models with different hyper-parameters and boosting
 1003  methods, and combined the outputs of different models to
 1004  obtain the final prediction results. Zhang et al. [40] extracted

 1005  USENIX Association
=== PAGE 14 ===

 1006  time-series related features to capture the failure symptoms in
 1007  long-term data trends, and achieved better prediction accuracy.
 1008  Besides failure prediction, they also introduced failure type
 1009  classification and lifespan estimation. It should be pointed out
 1010  that the failure type definitions are not completely objective,
 1011  and there are some differences in the definitions from different companies. The failure ratings in this paper are based on
 1012  more objective SSD error, failure, and replacement status, so
 1013  they are more universal.
 1014  In addition to classification algorithms, Chandranil et al. [4]
 1015  adopted anomaly detection algorithms to capture failed SSDs
 1016  which are considered far away from most healthy SSDs, and
 1017  thus they can perceive unseen outlier patterns. They compared the isolation forest and autoencoder algorithms, and
 1018  autoencoder has higher accuracy for failure prediction.

 1019  7 Conclusion

 1020  In this paper, we propose MSFRD for failure prediction, early
 1021  rating and progressive diagnosis. MSFRD first dynamically
 1022  detects data mutations through the prediction error of timeseries Telemetry data and estimates the importance of mutations. Then, failed SSDs are predicted and rated based on
 1023  mutation similarity with historical failed SSDs and healthy
 1024  SSDs. Finally, failures are handled incrementally to minimize
 1025  the impact on available SSDs without serious failures. The
 1026  evaluations on multiple real datasets show that MSFRD significantly improves the accuracy of failure prediction, rating
 1027  and handling.
