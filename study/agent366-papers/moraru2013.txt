abstract: >-
  This paper describes the design and implementation of Egalitarian Paxos (EPaxos), a new distributed consensus algorithm based on Paxos. EPaxos achieves three goals: (1) optimal commit latency in the wide-area when tolerating one and two failures, under realistic conditions; (2) uniform load balancing across all replicas (thus achieving high throughput); and (3) graceful performance degradation when replicas are slow or crash. Egalitarian Paxos is to our knowledge the first protocol to achieve the previously stated goals efficiently---that is, requiring only a simple majority of replicas to be non-faulty, using a number of messages linear in the number of replicas to choose a command, and committing commands after just one communication round (one round trip) in the common case or after at most two rounds in any case.    23  1 Introduction

   24  Distributed computing places two main demands on repli-

   25  cation protocols: (1) high throughput for replication in-

   26  side a computing cluster; and (2) low latency for repli-

   27  cation across data centers. Today's clusters use fault-

   28  tolerant coordination engines such as Chubby [4], Box-

   29  wood [22], or ZooKeeper [12] for activities including

   30  operation sequencing, coordination, leader election, and

   31  resource discovery. Many databases are accessed si-

   32  multaneously from different continents, requiring geo-

   33  replication [2, 8].

   34  An important limitation on these systems is that dur-

   35  ing efficient, failure-free operation, all clients communi-

   36  cate with a single master (or leader) server at all times.

   52  This algorithmic limitation has several important con-

   53  sequences. First, it impairs scalability by placing a dis-

   54  proportionately high load on the master, which must pro-

   55  cess more messages than the other replicas [23]. Second,

   56  when performing geo-replication, clients incur additional

   57  latency for communicating with a remote master. Third,

   60  network delays that increase latency at the master. Fi-

   63  until a new master is elected. Previously proposed so-

   65  undesirable because they restrict the type of operations

   66  the cluster can perform. For example, a partitioned clus-

   67  ter cannot perform atomic operations across partitions

   68  without using additional techniques.

   69  Egalitarian Paxos (EPaxos) has no designated leader

   70  process. Instead, clients can choose at every step which

   71  replica to submit a command to, and in most cases the

   72  command is committed without interfering with other

   73  concurrent commands. This allows the system to evenly

   74  distribute the load to all replicas, eliminating the first bot-

   75  tleneck identified above (i.e., having one server that must

   76  be on the critical path for all communication). EPaxos's

   77  flexible load distribution better handles permanently or

   78  transiently slow nodes, as well as the latency heterogene-

   79  ity caused by geographical distribution of replicas; this

   80  substantially reduces both the median and tail commit la-

   81  tency. Finally, the system can provide higher availability

   82  and higher performance under failures because there is

   83  no transient interruption caused by leader election: there

   84  is no leader, and hence, no need for leader election, as

   85  long as more than half of the replicas are available.

  105  2 Overview

  106  We begin by briefly describing the classic Paxos algo-

  107  rithm, followed by an overview of Egalitarian Paxos.

  108  2.1 Paxos Background

  109  State machine replication aims to make a set of possibly

  110  faulty distributed processors (the replicas) execute the

  111  same commands in the same order. Because each proces-

  112  sor is a state machine with no other inputs, all non-faulty

  113  processors will transition through the same sequence of

  114  states. Given a particular position in the command se-

  115  quence, running the Paxos algorithm guarantees that, if

  116  and when termination is reached, all non-faulty replicas

  117  agree on a single command to be assigned that position.

  118  To be able to make progress, at most a minority of the

  119  replicas can be faulty—if N is the total number of repli-

  120  cas, at least bN/2c+ 1 must be non-faulty for Paxos to

  121  make progress. Paxos, EPaxos, and other common Paxos

  122  variants handle only non-Byzantine failures: a replica

  123  may crash, or it may fail to respond to messages from

  124  other replicas indefinitely; it cannot, however, respond in

  125  a way that does not conform to the protocol.

  126  The execution of a replicated state machine that uses

  127  Paxos proceeds as a series of pre-ordered instances,

  128  where the outcome of each instance is the agreement

  129  on a single command. The voting process for one in-

  130  stance may happen concurrently with voting processes

  131  for other instances, but does not interfere with them.

  132  Upon receiving a command request from a client, a

  133  replica will try to become the leader of a not-yet-used

  134  instance by sending Prepare messages to at least a ma-

  135  jority of replicas (possibly including itself). A reply to a

  136  Prepare contains the command that the replying replica

  137  believes may have already been chosen in this instance

  138  (in which case the new leader will have to use that com-

  139  mand instead of the newly proposed one), and also con-

  140  stitutes a promise not to acknowledge older messages

  141  from previous leaders. If the aspiring leader receives at

  142  least bN/2c+1 acknowledgements in this prepare phase,

  143  it will proceed to propose its command by sending it to a

  144  majority of peers in the form of Accept messages; if these

  145  messages are also acknowledged by a majority, the leader

  146  commits the command locally, and then asynchronously

  147  notifies all its peers and the client.

  148  Because this canonical mode of operation requires at

  149  least two rounds of communication (two round trips)

  150  to commit a command—and more rounds in the case

  151  of dueling leaders—the widely used "Multi-Paxos" op-

  152  timization designates a replica to be the stable leader

  153  (or distinguished proposer). A replica becomes a stable

  154  leader by running the prepare phase for a large (possibly

  155  infinite) number of instances at the same time, thus taking

  156  ownership of all of them. In steady state, clients send

  157  commands only to the stable leader, which directly pro-

  158  poses them in the instances it already owns (i.e., without

  159  running the prepare phase). When a non-leader replica

  160  suspects the leader has failed, it tries to become the new

  161  leader by taking ownership of the instances for which it

  162  believes commands have not yet been chosen.

  163  Section 3 discusses several Paxos variants that improve

  164  upon this basic protocol.

  165  2.2 Egalitarian Paxos: Contributions and

  166  Intuition

  167  The main goals when designing EPaxos were: (1) optimal

  168  commit latency in the wide area, (2) optimal load balanc-

  169  ing across all replicas, to achieve high throughput, and

  170  (3) graceful performance degradation when some replicas

  171  become slow or crash. To achieve these goals, EPaxos

  172  must allow all replicas to act as proposers (or command

  173  leaders) simultaneously, for clients not to waste round

  174  trips to remote sites, and functionality to be well bal-

  175  anced across replicas. Furthermore, each proposer must

  176  be able to commit a command after communicating with

  177  the smallest possible number of remote replicas (i.e., quo-

  178  rums must be as small as possible). Finally, the quorum

  179  composition must be flexible, so that command leaders

  180  can easily avoid slow or unresponsive replicas.

  181  EPaxos achieves all this due to the novel way in which

  182  it orders commands. Previous algorithms ordered com-

  183  mands either by having a single stable leader choose the

  184  order (as in Multi-Paxos and Generalized Paxos), or by

  185  assigning them to instances (i.e., command slots) in a

  186  pre-ordered instance space (as in canonical Paxos and

  187  Mencius) whereby the order of the commands is the pre-

  188  established order of their respective slots. In contrast,

  189  EPaxos orders the instances dynamically and in a decen-

  190  tralized fashion: in the process of choosing (i.e., voting) a

  191  command in an instance, each participant attaches order-

  192  ing constraints to that command. EPaxos guarantees that

  193  all non-faulty replicas will commit the same command

  194  with the same constraints, so every replica can use these

  195  constraints to independently reach the same ordering.

  210  This ordering approach is the source of the benefits

  211  EPaxos has over previous algorithms. First, committing

  212  a command is contingent upon the input of any majority

  213  of replicas (unlike in Multi-Paxos where the stable leader

  214  must be part of every decision, or in Mencius, where

  215  information from all replicas is required)—this benefits

  216  wide-area commit latency, availability, and also improves

  217  performance robustness, because it decouples the perfor-

  218  mance of the fastest replicas from that of the slowest.

  219  Second, any replica can propose a command, not just a

  220  distinguished proposer, or leader—this allows for load

  221  balancing, which increases throughput.

  222  In taking this ordering approach, EPaxos must main-

  223  tain safety and provide a linearizable ordering of com-

  224  mands, while minimizing both the number of replicas

  225  that must participate in voting for each command and

  226  the number of messages exchanged between them. One

  227  observation that makes this task easier—by substantially

  228  reducing the number of ordering constraints in the com-

  229  mon case—was made by generic broadcast algorithms

  230  and Generalized Paxos before us: it is not necessary to

  231  enforce a consistent ordering for the common case of

  232  commands that do not interfere with each other.

  263  3 Comparison with Related Work

  264  Multi-Paxos [15, 16] makes efficient forward progress by

  265  relying on a stable leader replica that brokers communica-

  266  tion with clients and other replicas. With N replicas, for

  267  each command, the leader handles Θ(N) messages, and

  268  non-leader replicas handle only O(1). Thus, the leader

  269  can become a bottleneck, as practical implementations

  270  of Paxos have observed [4]. When the leader fails, the

  271  state machine becomes temporarily unavailable until a

  272  new leader is elected. This problem is not easily solved:

  273  aggressive leader re-election can cause stalls if multiple

  274  replicas believe they are the leader. Chubby [4] and Box-

  275  wood [22] use Multi-Paxos, while ZooKeeper [12] relies

  276  on a stable leader protocol similar to Multi-Paxos.

  277  Mencius [23] distributes load evenly across replicas

  278  by rotating the Paxos leader for every command. The in-

  279  stance space is pre-partitioned among all replicas: replica
  280  Rid owns every instance i where (i mod N) = Rid. The

  281  drawback of this approach is that every replica must hear

  282  from all other replicas before committing a command A,

  283  because otherwise another command B that depends on

  284  A may be committed in an instance ordered before the

  285  current instance (the other replicas reply either that they

  286  are also committing commands for their instances, or that

  287  they are skipping their turn). This has two consequences:

  288  (1) the replicated state machine runs at the speed of the

  289  slowest replica, and (2) Mencius can exhibit worse avail-

  290  ability than Multi-Paxos, because if any replica fails to

  291  respond, no other replica can make progress until a fail-

  292  ure is suspected and another replica commits no-ops on

  293  behalf of the possibly failed replica.

  294  Fast Paxos [18] reduces the number of message delays

  325  latency and the overall number of messages exchanged,

  326  because a replica must contact fewer of its closest peers

  331  trips in Generalized Paxos. (3) For three-site replication,

  336  good fit for MDCC [14], which uses Generalized Paxos

  380  4 Design

  381  In this section we describe Egalitarian Paxos in detail,

  382  state its properties and sketch informal proofs of those

  383  properties. Formal proofs and a TLA+ specification of the

  384  protocol can be found in a technical report accompanying

  385  this paper [25]. We begin by stating assumptions and

  386  definitions, and by introducing our notation.

  387  4.1 Preliminaries

  388  Messages exchanged by processes (clients and replicas)

  389  are asynchronous. Failures are non-Byzantine (a ma-

  390  chine can fail by stopping to respond for an indefinite

  391  amount of time). The replicated state machine comprises

  392  N = 2F +1 replicas, where F is the maximum number

  393  of tolerated failures. For every replica R there is an un-

  394  bounded sequence of numbered instances R.1, R.2, R.3, ...

  395  that replica R is said to own. The complete state of each

  396  replica comprises all the instances owned by every replica

  397  in the system (i.e., for N replicas, the state of each replica

  398  can be regarded as a two-dimensional array with N rows

  399  and an unbounded number of columns). At most one
=== PAGE 5 ===

  400  command will be chosen in an instance. The ordering

  401  of the instances is not pre-determined—it is determined

  402  dynamically by the protocol, as commands are chosen.

  403  It is important to understand that committing and exe-

  404  cuting commands are different actions, and that the com-

  405  mit and execution orders are not necessarily the same.

  406  To modify the replicated state, a client sends Re-

  407  quest(command) to a replica of its choice. A RequestRe-

  408  ply from that replica will notify the client that the com-

  409  mand has been committed. However, the client has no

  410  information about whether the command has been exe-

  411  cuted or not. Only when the client reads the replicated

  412  state updated by its previously committed commands is

  413  it necessary for the system to execute those commands.

  414  To read (part of) the state, clients send Read(objectIDs)

  415  messages and wait for ReadReplies. Read is a no-op com-

  416  mand that interferes with updates to the objects it is read-

  417  ing. Clients can also use RequestAndRead(γ, objectIDs)

  418  to propose command γ and atomically read the machine

  419  state immediately after γ is executed—Read(objectIDs)

  420  is equivalent to RequestAndRead(no-op, objectIDs).

  421  Before describing EPaxos in detail, we must define

  422  command interference: Two commands γ and δ inter-

  423  fere if there exists a sequence of commands Σ such that

  424  the serial execution Σ, γ, δ is not equivalent to Σ, δ, γ

  425  (i.e., they result in different machine states and/or differ-

  426  ent values returned by the reads within these sequences).

  427  4.2 Protocol Guarantees

  428  The formal guarantees that EPaxos offers clients are sim-

  429  ilar to those provided by other Paxos variants:

  430  Nontriviality: Any command committed by any

  431  replica must have been proposed by a client.

  432  Stability: For any replica, the set of committed com-

  433  mands at any time is a subset of the committed commands
  434  at any later time. Furthermore, if at time t1 a replica R

  435  has command γ committed at some instance Q.i, then R
  436  will have γ committed in Q.i at any later time t2 > t1.

  437  Consistency: Two replicas can never have different

  438  commands committed for the same instance.

  439  Execution consistency: If two interfering commands

  440  γ and δ are successfully committed (by any replicas) they

  441  will be executed in the same order by every replica.

  442  Execution linearizability: If two interfering com-

  443  mands γ and δ are serialized by clients (i.e., δ is pro-

  444  posed only after γ is committed by any replica), then

  445  every replica will execute γ before δ.

  446  Liveness (w/ high probability): Commands will even-

  447  tually be committed by every non-faulty replica, as long

  448  as fewer than half the replicas are faulty and messages
  449  eventually go through before recipients time out.2

  452  4.3 The Basic Protocol

  453  For clarity, we first describe the basic Egalitarian Paxos,

  454  and improve on it in the next section. This basic EPaxos

  455  uses a simplified procedure to recover from failures, and
  456  as a consequence, its fast-path quorum3 is 2F (out of

  457  the total of N = 2F + 1 replicas). The fully optimized
  458  EPaxos reduces this quorum to only F + F +1 replicas.
  459  The slow-path quorum size is always F + 1. 2

  460  4.3.1 The Commit Protocol

  461  As mentioned earlier, committing and executing com-

  462  mands are separate. Accordingly, EPaxos comprises (1)

  463  the protocol for choosing (committing) commands and

  464  determining their ordering attributes; and (2) the algo-

  465  rithm for executing commands based on these attributes.

  466  Figure 2 shows the pseudocode of the basic protocol

  467  for choosing commands. Each replica's state is repre-

  468  sented by its private cmds log that records all commands

  469  seen (but not necessarily committed) by the replica.

  470  We split the description of the commit protocol into

  471  multiple phases. Not all phases are executed for every

  472  command: a command committed after Phase 1 and Com-

  473  mit was committed on the fast path. The slow path in-

  474  volves the additional Phase 2 (the Paxos-Accept phase).

  475  Explicit Prepare (Figure 3) is run only on failure recovery.

  476  Phase 1 starts when a replica L receives a request for a

  477  command γ from a client and becomes a command leader.

  478  L begins the process of choosing γ in the next available

  479  instance of its instance sub-space. It also attaches what it

  480  believes are the correct attributes for that command:

  481  deps is the list of all instances that contain commands

  482  (not necessarily committed) that interfere with γ;

  483  we say that γ depends on those instances and their

  484  corresponding commands;

  485  seq is a sequence number used to break dependency cy-

  486  cles during the execution algorithm; seq is updated

  487  to be larger than the seq of all interfering commands

  488  in deps.

  489  The command leader forwards the command and the

  490  initial attributes to at least a fast-path quorum of replicas

  491  as a PreAccept message. Each replica, upon receiving the

  492  PreAccept, updates γ's deps and seq attributes according

  493  to the contents of its cmds log, records γ and the new

  494  attributes in the log, and replies to the command leader.

  495  If the command leader receives replies from enough

  496  replicas to constitute a fast-path quorum, and all the up-

  497  dated attributes are the same, it commits the command.

  498  If it does not receive enough replies, or the attributes in

  499  some replies have been updated differently than in others,

  500  then the command leader updates the attributes based

  645  4.3.3 Informal Proof of Properties

  646  Together, the commit protocol and execution algorithm

  647  guarantee the properties stated in Section 4.2. We prove

  648  this formally in a technical report [25], but give informal

  649  proofs here to convey the intuition of our design choices.

  650  Nontriviality is straightforward: Phase 1 is only exe-

  651  cuted for commands proposed by clients.

  652  To prove stability and consistency, we first prove:

  653  Proposition 1. If replica R commits command γ at in-

  654  stance Q.i (with R and Q not necessarily distinct), then
  655  for any replica R0 that commits command γ0 at Q.i it must
  656  hold that γ and γ0 are the same command.

  657  Proof sketch. Command γ is committed at instance Q.i

  658  only if replica Q has started Phase 1 for γ at instance Q.i.

  659  Q cannot start Phase 1 for different commands at the same

  660  instance, because (1) Q increments its instance number

  661  for every new command, and (2) if Q fails and restarts, it

  662  will be given a new, unused identifier (Section 4.7).

  663  Proposition 1 implies consistency. Furthermore, be-

  664  cause commands can be forgotten only if a replica

  665  crashes, this also implies stability if the cmds log is

  666  maintained on persistent storage. Execution consistency

  667  also requires stability and consistency for the command

  668  attributes.

  670  deps , we say that the tuple ( seq deps ) is safeγ at

  715  By the execution algorithm, a command is executed

  716  only after all the commands in its dependency graph have

  717  been committed. There are three possible scenarios:

  718  Case 1: Both commands are in each other's depen-

  719  dency graph. By the way the graphs are constructed, this

  720  implies: (1) the dependency graphs are identical; and

  721  (2) γ and δ are in the same strongly connected compo-

  722  nent. Therefore, when executing one command, the other

  723  is also executed, and they are executed in the order of

  724  their sequence numbers (with arbitrary criteria to break

  725  ties). By Proposition 2 the attributes of all committed

  726  commands are stable and consistent across replicas, so

  727  all replicas build the same dependency graph and execute

  728  γ and δ in the same order.

  729  Case 2: γ is in δ's dependency graph but δ is not in

  730  γ's. There is a path from δ to γ in δ's dependency graph,

  731  but there is no path from γ to δ. Therefore, γ and δ

  732  are in different strongly connected components, and γ's

  733  component will come before δ's in inverse topological

  734  order. By the execution algorithm, γ will be executed

  735  before δ. This is consistent with the situation when γ had

  736  been executed on some replicas before δ was committed

  737  (which is possible, because γ does not depend on δ).

  738  Case 3: Just like case 2, with γ and δ reversed.

  739  Lemma 2 (Execution linearizability). If two interfering

  740  commands γ and δ are serialized by clients (i.e., δ is

  741  proposed only after γ is committed by any replica), then

  742  every replica will execute γ before δ.

  743  Proof sketch. Because δ is proposed after γ was com-

  744  mitted, γ's sequence number is stable and consistent by

  745  the time any replica receives PreAccept messages for

  746  δ. Because a tuple containing γ and its final sequence

  748  sequence number will be updated to be larger than γ's,

  749  and δ will contain γ in its dependencies. Therefore, when

  752  number will be higher), or in a component ordered be-

  756  replicas are non-faulty. A client keeps retrying a com-

  757  mand until a replica gets a majority to accept it.

  758  4.4 Optimized Egalitarian Paxos

  759  We have described the core concepts of our protocol in

  760  the previous section. We now describe modifications that

  761  allow EPaxos to use a smaller fast-path quorum—only
  762  F + F+1 replicas, including the command leader. This
  763  is an important optimization because, by decreasing the2

  764  number of replicas that must be contacted, EPaxos has

  765  lower latency (especially in the wide area) and higher

  766  throughput, because replicas process fewer messages for

  767  each command. For three and five replicas, this fast path

  768  quorum is optimal (two and three replicas respectively).

  770  Phase) changes substantially, starting with line 32 in our

  771  pseudocode description. The new command leader Q
  772  looks for only F+1 replicas that have pre-accepted a

  779  with other commands in the replica's log—i.e., an inter-

  833  4.5 Keeping the Dependency List Small

  834  Instead of including all interfering instances, we include

  835  only N dependencies in each list: the instance number R.i

  836  with the highest i for which the current replica has seen

  837  an interfering command (not necessarily committed). If

  838  interference is transitive (usually the case in practice) the

  839  most recent interfering command suffices, because its

  840  dependency graph will contain all interfering instances

  841  R. j, with j < i. Otherwise, every replica must assume

  842  that any unexecuted commands in previous instances

  843  R. j (j < i) are possible dependencies and independently

  844  check them at execute time. This is a fast operation when

  845  commands are executed soon after commit.

  846  4.6 Recovering from Failures

  847  A replica may need to learn the decision for an instance

  848  because it has to execute commands that depend on that

  849  instance. If a replica times out waiting for the commit

  850  for an instance, the replica will try to take ownership of

  851  that instance by running Explicit Prepare, at the end of

  852  which it will either learn what command was proposed

  853  in this problem instance (and then finalize committing it),

  854  or, if no other replica has seen a command, will commit

  855  a no-op to finalize the instance.

  856  If clients are allowed to time-out and re-issue com-

  857  mands to a different replica, the replicas must be able to

  858  recognize duplicates and execute the command only once.

  859  This situation affects any replication protocol, and stan-

  860  dard solutions are applicable, such as unique command

  861  IDs or ensuring that commands are idempotent.

  862  4.7 Reconfiguring the Replica Set

  863  Reconfiguring a replicated state machine is an extensive

  864  topic [19, 20, 21]. In EPaxos, ordering ballots by their

  865  epoch prefix enables a solution that resembles Vertical

  866  Paxos [19] with majority read quorums: A new replica, or

  867  one that recovers without its memory, must receive a new

  868  ID and a new (higher) epoch number, e.g., from a config-

  869  uration service or a human. It then sends Join messages

  870  to at least F +1 live replicas that are not themselves in the

  871  process of joining. Upon receiving a Join, a live replica

  872  updates its membership information and the epoch part of

  873  each ballot number it uses or expects to receive for new

  874  instances. It will thus no longer acknowledge messages

  875  for instances initiated in older epochs (instances that it

  876  was not already aware of). The live replica will then

  877  send the joining replica the list of committed or ongoing

  878  instances that the live replica is aware of. The joining

  879  replica becomes live (i.e., it proposes commands and par-

  880  ticipates in voting the proposals of other replicas) only

  881  after receiving commits for all instances included in the

  882  replies to at least F +1 Join messages. Production imple-

  883  mentations optimize this process using snapshots [7].

  884  4.8 Read Leases

  885  As in any other state machine replication protocol, a Read

  886  must be committed as a command that interferes with

  887  updates to the objects it is reading to avoid reading stale

  888  data. However, Paxos-based systems are often optimized

  889  for read-heavy scenarios in one of two ways: assume the

  890  clients can handle stale data and perform reads locally

  891  at any replica, as in ZooKeeper [12]; or grant a read

  892  lease to the stable leader so that it can respond without

  893  committing an operation [7]. EPaxos can use read leases

  894  just as easily, with the understanding that a (infrequent)

  895  write to the leased object must be channeled through

  896  the node holding the lease. In wide-area replication, the

  897  leaderless design of EPaxos and Mencius allows different

  898  sites to hold leases for different objects simultaneously

  899  (e.g., based on the observed demand for each object).

  900  4.9 Avoiding Execution Livelock

  901  With a fast stream of interfering proposals, command

  902  execution could livelock: command γ will acquire depen-

  903  dencies on newer commands proposed between sending

  904  and receiving the PreAccept(γ). These new commands

  905  in turn gain dependencies on even newer commands. To

  906  prevent this, we prioritize completing old commands

  907  over proposing new commands. Even without this op-

  908  timization, however, long dependency chains increase

  909  only execution latency, not commit latency. They also

  910  negligibly affect throughput, because executing a batch

  911  of n inter-dependent commands at once adds only modest

  912  computational overhead: finding the strongly connected

  913  components has linear time complexity (the number of

  914  dependencies for each command is usually constant—

  915  Section 4.5), and sorting the commands by their sequence

  916  attribute adds only an O(logn) factor.

  917  5 Practical Considerations

  918  Command interference. For EPaxos to function ef-

  919  ficiently, the implementation must be able to decide

  920  whether two commands interfere before executing them

  921  (it can, however, conservatively assume interference if un-

  922  certain). Although there are many approaches that could

  923  work, one that seems likely is to use explicitly-specified

  924  dependency keys as in Google's High Replication Datas-

  925  tore [10] and Megastore [2]. Interference can easily be

  926  inferred for NoSQL key-value stores where all (or most)

  927  operations identify the keys they are targeting. Even for

  928  relational databases, the transactions that usually con-

  929  stitute the bulk of the workload are simple and can be

  930  examined before execution to determine which rows they

  931  will update (e.g. the New-Order transaction in the TPC-C

  932  benchmark [28]). For other transactions it will be diffi-

  933  cult to predict what exact state they will modify, but it is

  934  safe to assume they interfere with any other transaction.

  935  Consistency guarantees. EPaxos guarantees per-object

  936  linearizability. As shown by Herlihy and Wing [11], lin-

  937  earizability is a local property, meaning that "a system

  938  is linearizable if each individual object is linearizable".

  939  This only applies to operations that target single objects,

  940  or more generally, to operations for which interference

  941  is transitive. The equivalent property for multi-object

  942  operations is strict serializability. If interference is not

  943  transitive, EPaxos maintains per-object linearizability,

  944  but does not guarantee strict serializability without a sim-

  945  ple modification: the commit notification for a command

  946  is sent to clients only after every instance in the com-

  947  mand's graph of dependencies has been committed (the

  948  proof is in the associated tech report [25]). This has the

  949  added benefit that it simplifies the protocol: approximate

  950  sequence numbers are no longer necessary (commands

  951  within the same strongly connected component are sorted

  952  by an arbitrary criterion), which makes for a simplified

  953  recovery procedure. The drawback of this version of

  954  EPaxos is increased perceived latency at the client for

  955  operations that conflict with other concurrent operations—

  956  for the common case of non-interfering operations the

  957  latency remains the same as in canonical EPaxos.

  958  6 Implementation

  959  We implemented EPaxos, Multi-Paxos, Mencius, and

  960  Generalized Paxos in Go, version 1.0.2.

  961  6.1 Language-specific details

  962  Behind our choice of Go was the goal of comparing the

  963  performance of the four Paxos variants within a common

  964  framework in which the protocols share as much code

  965  as possible to reduce implementation-related differences.

  966  While subjective, we believe we achieved this, applying

  967  roughly equal implementation optimization to each; we

  968  are releasing our implementations for others to perform

  969  comparisons or further optimization [24].

  970  Go presented two challenges: First, the garbage collec-

  971  tion that eased implementation of four complete Paxos

  972  variants adds performance variability; and second, its

  973  RPC implementation is slow. We solved the latter by

  974  implementing our own RPC stub generator. We have

  975  not fully mitigated the GC penalty, but EPaxos is more

  976  affected than the other protocols because its attribute-

  977  containing messages are larger, so our results are fair to

  978  the other protocols.

  979  6.2 Thrifty Operation

  980  For all protocols except Mencius, we used an optimiza-

  981  tion that we call thrifty. In thrifty, a replica in charge of a

  982  command (the command leader in EPaxos, or the stable

  983  leader in Multi-Paxos) sends Accept and PreAccept mes-

  984  sages to only a quorum of replicas, including itself, not

  985  the full set. This reduces message traffic and improves

  986  throughput. The drawback is that if an acceptor fails

  987  to reply quickly, there is no quick fall-back to another

  988  reply. However, thrifty can aggressively send messages

  989  to additional acceptors when a reply is not received after

  990  a short wait time; doing so does not affect safety and only

  991  slightly reduces throughput. Mencius cannot be thrifty be-

  992  cause the replies to Accept messages contain information

  993  necessary to commit the current instance (i.e., whether
  994  previous instances were skipped or not).4

 1043  7.2 Latency In Wide Area Replication

 1044  We validate empirically that EPaxos has optimal median

 1045  commit latency in the wide area with three replicas (tol-

 1046  erating one failure) and five replicas (tolerating two fail-

 1047  ures). The replicas are located in Amazon EC2 datacen-

 1048  ters in California (CA), Virginia (VA) and Ireland (EU),

 1049  plus Oregon (OR) and Japan (JP) for the five-replica

 1050  experiment. At each location there are also ten clients

 1051  co-located with each replica (fifty in total). They gen-

 1052  erate requests simultaneously, and measure the commit

 1053  and execute latency for each request. Figure 4 shows

 1054  the median and 99%ile latency for EPaxos, Multi-Paxos,

 1055  Mencius and Generalized Paxos.

 1056  With three replicas, an EPaxos replica can always com-

 1057  mit after one round trip to its nearest peer even if that

 1058  command interferes with other concurrent commands.

 1059  In contrast, Generalized Paxos's fast quorum size when

 1060  N = 3 is three. Its latency is therefore determined by a

 1061  round-trip to the farthest replica. The high 99%ile la-

 1062  tency experienced by Generalized Paxos is caused by

 1063  checkpoint commits. Furthermore, conflicts cause two

 1064  additional round trips in Generalized Paxos (for any num-

 1065  ber of replicas). Thus, in this experiment, EPaxos is

 1066  not affected by conflicts, but Generalized Paxos experi-

 1067  ences median latencies of 341 ms with 100% command

 1068  interference.

 1069  With five replicas, EPaxos avoids the two most distant

 1070  replicas, while Generalized Paxos avoids only the most

 1071  distant one. Thus, EPaxos has optimal commit latency

 1072  for the common case of non-interfering concurrent com-

 1073  mands, with both three and five replicas. For five replicas,

 1074  interfering commands cause one extra round trip to the

 1075  closest two replicas for EPaxos, but up to two additional

 1076  round trips for Generalized Paxos.

 1077  Mencius performs relatively well with multiple clients

 1078  at every location and all locations generating commands

 1079  at the same aggregate rate. Imbalances force Mencius to

 1080  wait for more replies to Accept messages. In the worst

 1081  case, with active clients at only one location at a time,

 1082  Mencius experiences latency corresponding to the round

 1083  trip time to the replica that is farthest away from the

 1084  client, for any number of replicas.

 1085  Multi-Paxos has high latency because the local replica

 1086  must forward all commands to the stable leader.

 1087  The results in Figure 4 refer to commit latency. For

 1088  EPaxos, execution latency differs from commit latency

 1089  only for high conflict rates because a replica must delay

 1090  executing a command until it receives commit confir-

 1091  mations for the command's dependencies. With 100%

 1092  interference rate (i.e., worst case), three-replicas EPaxos

 1093  experiences median execution latencies of 125 ms to

 1094  139 ms (depending on the site), whereas for five repli-

 1095  cas, median execution latencies range from 304 ms to

 1096  319 ms (compared to 274 ms to 296 ms for Mencius, and

 1097  unchanged latencies for Multi-Paxos and Generalized

 1098  Paxos 100%). As explained in the previous section, this

 1099  worst case scenario is highly unlikely to occur in prac-

 1100  tice. Furthermore, commit latency is the only one that
 1101  matters for writes6, while for reads, which have a lower

 1102  chance of generating conflicts, there is a high likelihood

 1103  that commit and execution latency are the same. Fur-

 1104  thermore, reads will also benefit from read leases, which

 1105  allow reads to be serviced locally.

 1106  However, if high command interference is common,

 1107  there is a wide range of techniques that we can use to

 1108  reduce latency, but we leave for future work: e.g., for-

 1109  warding PreAccepts among fast quorum members to re-

 1110  duce slow-path commit latency by one message delay,

 1111  or reverting to a partitioned Multi-Paxos mode, where

 1112  the same site acts as command leader for all commands

 1113  in a certain group (thus eliminating conflicts among the

 1114  commands within that group).

 1202  7.4 Logging Messages Persistently

 1203  To resume immediately after a crash, a replica must pre-

 1204  serve the contents of its memory intact, otherwise it may

 1205  break safety (for all of the protocols we evaluate). This

 1206  implies persistently logging every state change before

 1207  acting upon or replying to any message. The preceding

 1208  experiments did not include this overhead, because it is

 1209  avoidable in some circumstances: if power failure of all

 1210  replicas is not a threat, replicas can recover from failures

 1211  as presented in Section 4.7; in addition, persistent mem-

 1212  ory technologies keep improving, and battery-backed

 1213  memory is sometimes feasible. We nevertheless wanted

 1214  to evaluate whether EPaxos is fundamentally more I/O

 1215  intensive than Multi-Paxos or Mencius.

 1216  For the experiments in this section we used Amazon

 1217  EC2 High-I/O instances equipped with high-performance

 1218  solid state drives. Every replica logs its state changes

 1219  synchronously to an SSD-backed file, for all protocols.

 1220  Here (Figure 7), all protocols are I/O bound, but Multi-

 1221  Paxos places a higher I/O load on the stable leader than

 1222  on non-leader replicas, making it slower. EPaxos outper-

 1223  forms Mencius due to the thrifty optimization: in EPaxos,

 1224  unlike in Mencius, it is sufficient to send (pre-)accept

 1225  messages to only a quorum of replicas, and therefore

 1226  EPaxos requires fewer logging operations per command

 1227  than Mencius. However, we make the (novel, to our

 1228  knowledge) observation that while every Mencius accep-

 1229  tor must reply to accept messages, not all acceptors must

 1230  log their replies synchronously—it is sufficient that a quo-

 1231  rum of acceptors log synchronously before responding,

 1232  and the command leader commits only after receiving

 1233  their replies. "Mencius min-log" (in Figure 7), needs only

 1234  slightly more synchronous logging than EPaxos (every

 1235  min-log replica must still log its own skipped instances

 1236  synchronously).

 1340  7.7 Service Availability under Failures

 1341  Figure 10 shows the evolution of the commit throughput

 1342  in a three-replica setup that experiences the failure of

 1343  one replica. A client sends requests in an open loop, at

 1344  the same rate for every system—approximately 10,000

 1345  requests per second (a rate at which none of the systems

 1346  is close to saturation, hence the steady throughput).

 1347  With Multi-Paxos, or any variant that relies on a stable

 1348  leader, a leader failure prevents the system from process-

 1349  ing client requests until a new leader is elected. Although

 1350  clients could direct their requests to another replica (after

 1351  they time out), a replica will usually not try to become the

 1352  new leader immediately. False suspicions can degrade

 1353  performance by causing stalls, so the fail-over time will

 1354  usually be on the order of seconds [4, 22]. The failure of

 1355  a non-leader replica (a situation not depicted in Figure 10)

 1356  does not affect the availability of the system.

 1357  In contrast, any replica failure disrupts Mencius: a

 1358  replica cannot finalize an instance before knowing the

 1359  outcome of (or at least which commands are being pro-

 1360  posed in) all instances that precede it, and instances are

 1361  pre-assigned to replicas round-robin. Unlike in Multi-

 1362  Paxos, clients can continue to send requests to the remain-

 1363  ing replicas; they will be processed up to the point where

 1364  they are ready to be committed. Eventually, a live replica

 1365  will time out and commit no-ops on behalf of the failed

 1366  replica, thus freeing the instances waiting on them. At

 1367  this point, the delayed commands are committed and ac-

 1368  knowledged, which causes the throughput spike depicted

 1369  in Figure 10. Live replicas commit no-ops periodically

 1370  until the failed replica recovers, or until a reconfiguration.

 1371  Both in Multi-Paxos and Mencius, the timeout duration

 1372  is a trade-off between the availability of the service and

 1373  the impact that acting too frequently on false positives has

 1374  on throughput and latency. EPaxos avoids this dilemma

 1375  because it can operate uninterrupted by the crash of a

 1376  minority of replicas. Clients with commands outstanding

 1377  at a failed replica will time out and retry those requests

 1378  at another replica. Although live replicas will commit

 1379  commands unhindered, some of these commands may

 1380  have acquired dependencies on commands proposed by

 1381  the failed replica. Executing the former (as opposed to

 1382  committing them) will therefore be delayed until another

 1383  replica finalizes committing the latter. Unlike in Mencius,

 1384  this occurs only once: an inactive replica cannot continue

 1385  to generate dependencies. Moreover, it occurs rarely for

 1386  workloads with low conflict rates.
