   24  1 Introduction
   25  One of the potential benefits of distributed systems is their use in
   26  providing highly-available services, that is, services that are likely to
   27  be up and accessible when needed. Availability is essential to many
   28  computer-based services; for example, in airline reservation systems
   29  the failure of a single computer can prevent ticket sales for a
   30  considerable time, causing a loss of revenue and passenger
   31  goodwill.

   32  Availability is achieved through replication. By having more than
   33  one copy of important information, the service continues to be usable
   34  even when some copies are inaccessible, for example, because of a
   35  crash of the computer where a copy was stored. Various replication
   36  algorithms have been proposed to achieve availability
   37  [2, 4, 9, 11, 12, 16, 21,351. This paper presents a new replication
   38  algorithm that has desirable performance properties.

  109  2 Overview of our Method
  110  The method replicates individual modules to obtain module
  111  groups. A module group consists of several copies of the module,
  112  called cohods, which behave as a single, logical entity; the program
  113  can indicate the number of cohorts when the group is created. The
  114  set of cohorts is the group's configuration. Each cohort has a unique
  115  name called an mid; the group as a whole bears a unique groupid.
  116  We expect a small number of cohorts per group, on the order of
  117  three or five.

  118  One cohort is designated as the primary; it executes procedure
  119  calls, and participates in two-phase commit.

  120  The remaining cohorts are backups, which are essentially passive
  121  and merely receive state information from the primary.

  122  Failures and recoveries are masked when they are noticed. Over
  123  time the communication capability within a group may change as
  124  cohorts or communication links fail and recover. To reflect this
  125  changing situation, each cohort runs in a view. A view is a set of
  126  cohorts that are (or were) capable of communicating with each other,
  127  together with an indication of which cohort is the primary; it is a
  128  subset of the configur&ion and must contain a majority of group
  129  members.

  130  A group switches to a new view by executing a view change
  131  protocol; our protocol is a simpfiffcation and modification of the virtual
  132  partitions protocol [12]. Each view is identified by a unique viewid;
  133  we guarantee that viewids are totally ordered. The view change
  134  protocol generates a new view and viewid. If a majority of cohorts
  135  accept the new view, cohorts switch to the new, active view;
  136  otherwise, they remain in their old views, but the views become
  137  inactive. Transactions are processed only in active views.

  138  Views and viewids reflect the current communication patterns, but
  139  not the information about committed and active transactions that
  140  have run at the group. This additional information is obtained by
  141  using fimestamps. Timestamps are unique within a view and form a
  142  total order: they are generated by the primary and are easy to
  143  produce, for example, by incrementing a local counter. The primary
  144  generates a new timestamp each time it needs to communicate
  145  information to its backups; we refer to each such occurrence as an
  146  event. Examples of events are the completion of processing of a
  147  remote calf or of a prepare or commit message. Each event is
  148  assigned a unique timestamp, and later events receive later
  149  timestamps. Instead of checkpointing events directly to the backups,
  150  the primary maintains a communication buffer (similar to a fifo
  151  queue) to which it writes event records. An event record identifies
  152  the type of the event, and contains other relevant information about
  153  the event. Information in the buffer is sent to the backups in
  154  timestamp order. The buffer implementation provides reliable
  155  delivery of event records to all backups in the primary's view; if it fails
  156  to defiver a message, then a crash or communication failure has
  157  occurred that willcause a view change.

  158  We use timestamps as an inexpensive way of determining what

  159  information survives a view change. Because a timestamp is
  160  meaningful only within a view, we introduce viewstamps. A
  161  viewstamp is simply a timestamp concatenated with the viewid of the
  162  view In which the timestamp was generated; we refer to the parts of
  163  viewstamp Y as v.id and v.ts. Each cohort maintains a history.
  164  consisting of a sequence of viewstamps. each with a different viewfd.
  165  We guarantee that for each viewstamp v in its history, the cohort's
  166  state reflects event e from view v.id iff e's timestamp is less than or
  167  equal to v.ts.

  168  The correctness of our algorithm depends on the interaction of
  169  transaction processing and the view change algorithm. Transaction
  170  processing guarantees that transactions are serialized properly. In
  171  addition, it guarantees that a transaction can commit only if all its
  172  events are known to at least a majority of cohorts. The view change
  173  algorithm guarantees that events known to a majority of cohorts
  174  survive into subsequent views. Thus, events of committed
  175  transactions will survive view changes. Not all events survive view
  176  changes, however; for example, the processing of a particular
  177  remote calf may be lost. We use the hisfory plus some information
  178  that arrives in the prepare message to ensure that the transaction
  179  will be forced to abort in such a case. On the other hand, if the
  180  history and the information in the prepare message indicate that all
  181  the events associated with the transaction survived the view change,
  182  the transaction can commit.

  486  The algorithms above assumed that both the client and the server

  489  however, may not be worthwhile.

  499  participants know who it is. In answering a query about a transaction

  502  3.6 Nested Transactlons
  503  The protocol discussed above is quite permisstve about when a
  504  transaction can prepare, but much less permissive when a client
  505  sends a message to a cohort that does not respond. A lack of
  506  response causes the entire transaction to abort. Such an abort can
  507  cause lots of work to be lost.

  508  Obviously, there are ways to reduce the number of situations in
  509  which the abort happens. For example, we couM force a special
  510  "start call" record to the backups before making a nested remote call.
  511  It would be safe to run the call at the new primary lf there were no
  512  such record, since even if the call ran before the view change, its
  513  effects were bcal lo this group and therefore have been undone by
  514  the view change. Atternatively, the client could do a probe before
  515  making the call to determine the current primary. However, neither
  516  of these techniques is satisfactory, since they delay normal
  517  processing.

  518  A better approach is to use nested transactions [lo, 28.301.
  519  Nested transactions have two desirable properties. First, they allow

  520  concurrency within a transaction in a way that allows the concurrent
  521  activities to be serialized. Second, they provide a checkpointing
  522  mechanism: if some part of a transaction cannot complete, we can
  523  avoid aborting the entire transaction by running that part as a
  524  subactiin.

  525  Checkpointing is what allows us to minimize the effects of view
  526  changes. If the call is made as a subaction, we need not abort the
  527  entire transaction if there is no reply. Instead. we can abort just the
  528  subaction, and then do the call again as a new subaction. An
  529  algorithm for our ,nethod in a system with nested transactions is
  530  described in[32]: lt is based on the implementation of nested
  531  transactions in Argus (26, 281.

  532  Subactions are an economical way to cope with view changes.
  533  They are not expensive to implement [27j; they are much cheaper
  534  than either of the alternatives for avoiding aborts sketched above.
  535  Furthermore, we need to abort and redo a call subaction Only when
  536  the view changes; thus we do extra work only when the problem
  537  arises.

  538  3.7 Dlscusslon
  539  There is a one-to-one correspondence between event records and
  540  information written to stable storage by a coniventional transaction
  541  system and therefore our system works because a conventional one
  542  does. The "completed-call" records are equivalent to the data
  543  records that must be forced to stable storage before preparing, and
  544  the "commit" and "abort" records are the same as their stable
  545  storage counterparts. The only difference is our treatment of
  546  prepares, since we have no prepare record. In a conventional
  547  system, the prepare record tells the participant #after a crash whether
  548  a transaction that ran there before a crash is ablle to commit. We do
  549  not need the prepare record because we use the primary's history
  550  and the psef in the prepare message to determine what to do.

  551  Even when a transaction only has read locks, we must force the
  552  "completed-call" records to the backups when preparing to ensure
  553  that read locks are held across aview change. A view change may
  554  have happened without this primary being aware of it. and there may
  555  be a new primary already processing user requests in the other view.
  556  Furthermore, the preparing transaction's read-locks may not be
  557  known in the new view, so the new primary may allow other
  558  transactions to obtain conflicting locks. Forcing the buffer
  559  guarantees that the prepare can succeed only if the transaction's
  560  locks survived the view change. Without the force, the prepare could
  561  succeed at the old primary even though the locks did not survive. fn
  562  essence, not doing the force is equivalent to not sending the prepare
  563  message to a read-only participant; such prepare messages are
  564  needed to prevent violations of two-phase locking.

  565  We believe that our method will perform better than a nonreplicated system. Remote calls in our system run only at the

  581  4 Changing Views
  582  Transaction processing depends upon forcing information to
  583  backups so that a majority of cohorts know about particular events.
  584  The job of the view change algorithm is to ensure that events known
  585  to a majority of cohorts survive into subsequent views. It does this
  586  by ensuring that every view contains at least a majority of cohorts
  587  and by starting up the new view in the latest possible state.

  588  If every view has at least a majority of cohorts, then it contains at
  589  least one cohort that knows about any event that was forced to a
  590  majority of cohorts. Thus we need only make sure that the state of
  591  the new view includes what that cohort knows. This is done using
  592  viewstamps: the state of the cohort with the highest viewstamp for
  593  the previous view is used to initialize the state in the new view. This
  594  scheme works because event records are sent to the backups in
  595  timestamp order, and therefore a cohort with a later viewstamp for
  596  some view knows everything known to a cohort with an earlier
  597  viewstamp for that view.

  764  4.1 Discussion
  765  When failures or recoveries are detected by the system, the view
  766  change protocol runs in each affected module group. The protocol
  767  requires relatively little message-passing in the simple case of no
  768  additional failures and no concurrent view managers. One round of

  769  messages is all that is needed when the manager is also the primary
  770  in the last active view; otherwise, one round plus one message is
  771  needed.

  772  The system performs correctly even if there are several active
  773  primaries. This situation could arise when them is a panlion and the
  774  old primary is slow to notice the need for a view change and
  775  continues to respond to client requests even after the new view is
  776  formed. The old primary will not be able to prepare and commit user
  777  transactions, however, since it Cannot force their effects to the
  778  backups.

  779  If the same cohort is the primary both befo,re and after the view
  780  change, then no user work is lost in the change. Otherwise, we
  781  guarantee the following: Transactions that prepared in the okl view
  782  will be able to commit, and those that committed will still be
  783  committed. Transactions that had not yet prepared before the
  784  change may be able to prepare afterwards, depending on whether
  785  the completion events of the remote calls are known in the new view.
  786  Aborts of transactions may have been forgotten, but delivery of abort
  787  messages is not guaranteed in any case: recovery from lost
  788  messages is done by using queries (see Section 3.4). To minimize
  789  disruption while a view change is happening, or when there is no
  790  active view, queries can be answered by any cohort that knows the
  791  answer.

  792  The algorithm is tolerant to several cohorts simultaneously acting
  793  as managers; the one that chooses the higher viewid will ultimately
  794  succeed. Having several managers will slow things down, since
  795  there will be more message traffic, but the slow down will be slight.
  796  Furthermore, we can avoid concurrent managers to some extent by
  797  various policies. For example, the cohorts could be ordered, and a
  798  cohort would become a manager only if all higher-priority cohorts
  799  appear to be inaccessible.

  800  However, the algofhm is not tolerant of lost messages and slow
  801  responses. For example, suppose a manager waits only until it
  802  hears from a sub-majority even though there are other cohorts that
  803  could respond. This would result in those other cohorts being
  804  excluded from the new view, which in turn will mean another round of
  805  view changing will occur shortly. If that next view change also
  806  excludes some potential members, that will lead to another view
  807  change, and so on.

  808  To avoid such a situation, a manager should use a fairly long
  809  timeout while it waits to hear from all cohorts8 that the "I'm alive"
  810  messages indicate should reply. Similarly, an underling should use a
  811  fairly long timeout before it becomes a manager. In addition, it is
  812  worthwhile to mask lost messages by sending duplicates, so that a
  813  lost message won't trigger another view change.

  814  A final point is that not all view changes descrfbed above really
  815  need to be done. One special case is wheln an active primary
  816  notices that it cannot communicate wfth a backup, but it still has a
  817  sub-majority of other backups. In this case, the primary can
  818  unilaterally exclude the inaccessible backup from the view. Similarly,
  819  an acttte primary can unilaterally add a backup to its view. View
  820  changes are really needed only when the primary is lost, or when a
  821  current active view loses enough members that it is no longer a
  822  majority. In the latter case, we need not do a view change either; we
  823  make the primary inactive since this stops it from working on
  824  transactions when it wilt not be able to commit them.

  826  In our algorithm we assumed that most of a cohort's state was
  827  volatile. Such an assumption means that if a majority of cohorts are
  828  crashed "simultaneously," we may lose information about the module
  829  group's state. Here we view a cohort as crashed if either it is really
  830  crashed, or if it has recovered from a crash, but its up-to-dare

  831  variable is false. Note that a catastrophe does not cause a group to
  832  enter a new view missing some needed information. Rather, it
  833  causes the algorithm to never again form a new view.

  834  Whether it is worthwhile to worry about catastrophes depends on
  835  the likelihood of occurrence and the importance of the information in
  836  the group state. The considerations here are similar to decisions
  837  about when it is necessary to store information in stable storage in a

  840  catastrophe smaller to begin with.

  844  universal power supply and have them write information to
  845  nonvolatile storage in the background.

  846  5 Related Work
  847  In this section we discuss the relationship of our approach to other
  848  work on replication and view changes.

  849  The best known replication technique is voting [16,21]. With
  850  voting, write operations are usually performed at all Cohorts, and
  851  reads are performed at only one cohort, but in general writes can be
  852  performed at a majority of cohorts and reads at enough cohorts that
  853  each read will intersect each write at at least one cohort. The write
  854  ail/read one choice is preferred when reads are much more common
  855  than writes.

  856  Our method is faster than voting for write operations since we
  857  require fewer messages. Also, we avoid the deadlocks that can
  858  arise if messages for concurrent updates arrive at the cohorts in
  859  different orders. Our method will also be faster for read operations if
  860  these take place at several cohorts. If reads take place at just one
  861  cohorl, voting may outperform our method because reading can
  862  occur at any cohort, while reading in our scheme must happen at the
  863  primary, which could become a performance bottleneck. On the
  864  other hand, the real source of a bottlenedc is a node, not a cohort,
  865  and we can organize our system so that primaries of different groups
  866  usually run on different nodes. Furthermore, the system can be
  867  Configured to place primaries at more powerful nodes most of the
  868  time. This organization Could lead to better performance than voting.

  869  Voting allows operations to continue running as long as the
  870  needed number of cohorts are up and accessible. However, when
  871  writes must happen at all Cohorts, the lost of a single cohort can
  872  cause writes to become unavailable. The virtual partitions
  873  pro~ocol[l2, 131 was invented to solve this problem. Our view
  874  change protocol is a simplification and modification of this protocol
  875  and has better performance. The virtual partitions protocol requires
  876  three phases. The first round establishes the new view, the second
  877  informs the cohorts of the new view, and in the third, the Cohorts all
  878  communicate with one another to find out the current state. We
  879  avoid extra work by using viewstamps in phase 1 (the first round) to
  880  determine what each cohort knows. Our technique can be used in
  881  conjunction with voting when writes are done at all members of a
  882  view. Just as we use viewstamps, in such a system timestamps
  883  assigned when transactions commit could be used to determine
  884  which replica has the most information about transaction Commits
  885  (the timestamps would not contain information about the state of
  886  active transactions). Systems in which writes only go to a majority
  887  are more difficult to optimize in this way since there is usually no
  888  cohort whose state Contains at least as much information as the
  889  state of any other cohort.

  890  Virtual partitions force transactions that were active across a view
  891  change to abort. For example, a transaction that did a remote call in
  892  the old view will not be able to prepare in the new view. We use
  893  viewstamps to avoid the abort and we rely on the fact that knowledge

  894  of later events implies knowledge of earlier ones. A total order on
  895  viewstamps would be costly to implement with voting, since there iS
  896  no single place (like our primary) to generate the viewstamp. it might
  897  be possible to use multipart viewstamps (23, 291, however. This is a
  898  matter for future research.

  899  A different approach to replication is taken in Isis [4, 51. Isis works
  900  only in a local area network because its view change protocol does
  901  not tolerate partitions. In Isis, calls are sent to a single cohort. If the
  902  called procedure is a read, the cohort acquires a read lock IOCally
  903  and performs the operation locally. If the procedure is a write, the
  904  cohort acquires write locks at all cohorts before doing the Call. (Write
  905  locks are acquired using a two-phase algorithm that prevents
  906  deadlocks in the case of concurrent writes.) Then the cohoR
  907  performs the call. In either case, the Cohort communicates the
  908  effects of reads3 and writes to other cohorts in background 'mode,
  909  and piggybacks them on reply messages. This piggybacked
  910  information accompanies all future client messages, including calls to
  911  other servers as well as prepare and commit messages. This
  912  means, for example, that if the prepare message is sent to a different
  913  cohort from the one that performed the call, the information about the
  914  effect of the call wilt be present at the cohort doing the prepare, SO
  915  there will be no need for that cohort to wait for the background
  916  message to arrive, and no possibility that it would need to reject the
  917  prepare. Unlike our pset, however, piggybacked information in Isis
  918  cannot be discarded when transactions commit. A disadvantage of
  919  Isis is the large amount of extra information flowing on every
  920  message, and the difficulty in garbage collecting that information.

  921  Our method avoids these problems at the Cost of a possible delay
  922  at prepare time (to force the buffer) and of an occasional abort when
  923  there is a view change. The viewstamps in our method represent the
  924  information flowing in Isis. However, since the viewstamps only
  925  indicate that certain events have occurred, but not what these events
  926  are, we must sometimes wait for information about its events to
  927  arrive in buffer messages. Also, we must sometimes abort a
  928  transaction because information about its events is lost in a view
  929  change.

  930  In Cooper's replicated remote procedure calls [9], each procedure
  931  call is replicated and executed at every cohort of a server. This
  932  technique has high overhead during normal system operation: il
  933  requires lots of messages, is wasteful of computation, and requires
  934  that programs be deterministic. The advantage of the method is that
  935  recovery is inexpensive.

  936  Finally, Tandem's Nonstop systemI2, 7,8] and Ihe Auragen
  937  system [6] are primary copy methods but there is just one backup, so
  938  they can survive onfy a single failure. Furlhermore, the
  939  primary/backup pair must reside at a single node (containing multiple
  940  processors). If these constraints are acceptable, these methods are
  941  efficient. Ours is more general.

  942  6 Conclusions
  943  This paper has described a new replication method for providing
  944  high availability. The method performs well in the normal case, does
  945  view changes efficiently, and loses little information in a view
  946  change. We expect the performance of our method to be
  947  comparable to that of a system in which modules are not replicated
  948  and better than most other replication methods. At present we are
  949  implementing our method; we will be able to run experiments about
  950  system performance when our implementation is complete.

  951  Our view change algorithm is highly likely not to lose work in a
  952  view change. If a transaction's effects are known at the new primary,

  953  3The effect of a read is that a read lock has been acquired.

  954  the transaction can commit. Our notion of viewstamps allows us to
  955  determine inexpensively how much each cohort knows and whether
  956  a transaction can be committed. Our policy of choosing the primary
  957  of the last active view to be the new primary whenever possible
  958  avoids losing work altogether; even remote calls that were running
  959  before the view change can continue to run afterwards. Note that
  960  the probability of aborts can be decreased further if desired. There is
  961  a tradeoff here between loss of information in view changes and
  962  speed of processing calls. For example, if "completed call" records
  963  were forced to the backups before the call returned, there would be
  964  no aborts due to view changes, but calls would be processed more
  965  slowly.

  966  Choosing the primary of the old view to be the new primary
  967  minimizes information loss and makes the view change protocol run
  968  quickly. On the other hand, we could modify the protocol to always
  969  choose a particular cohort to be the primary if possible. Such a
  970  policy matches the needs of some applications. The policy would
  971  not cause loss of information: if the old primary is a member of the
  972  new view, all its events will survive into the new view. However,
  973  work in progress at the old primary would be lost in the change
  974  (unless some additional mechanism is included); this includes
  975  aborting transactions for which the primary is the coordinator. In
  976  addition, a few extra messages will sometimes be needed in the view
  977  change protocol.

  978  We presented our algorithm in a system with one-level
  979  transactions. However, as noted earlier, such a system can lead to
  980  aborts in which a substantial amount of work can be lost. The
  981  problem arises when a client gets no reply for a remote call; the
  982  transaction must be aborted to avoid running a call more than once.
  983  Nested transactions prevent the abort of the top level transaction,
  984  and, furthermore, do so efficiently.

  985  In defining our algorithm, we chose to avoid the use of stable
  986  storage as much as possible because we were interested in
  987  understanding the extent to which having several replicas eliminated
  988  the need for stable storage. We found that catastrophes (loss of a
  989  group's state) that would not happen if events were recorded on
  990  stable storage could sometimes occur in our system. The probability
  991  of a catastrophe depends on the configuration, e.g., on whether the
  992  cohort's nodes are failure independent. The algorithm can be
  993  modified in various ways to reduce the probability of catastrophe if it
  994  is considered to be too high.

  995  The use of viewstamps is an interesting compromise between loss
  996  of work in failures and extra information. Isis represents one
  997  extreme here: no work is lost when there is a failure but large
  998  amounts of information must flow around the system. Other systems
  999  have no information like viewstamps and must abort all transactions
 1000  affected by a failure.

 1001  Viewstamps may also be worthwhile in a nonreplicated System. In
 1002  such a system, records containing the effects of Calls could be
 1003  written to stable storage in background mode; the records, like event
 1004  records, would contain viewstamps. When the prepare message
 1005  arrives, it would only be necessaty to force the records; no delay
 1006  would be encountered if the records had already been Written. A
 1007  crash would not cause active transactions to abort aUtOmatiCally;
 1008  instead, queries would be sent to coordinators to determine the
 1009  outcomes. The result would be a system that is more tolerant Of
 1010  crashes (by avoiding aborts) and also faster at prepare time.
