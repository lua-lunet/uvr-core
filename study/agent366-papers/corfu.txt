    6  CORFU is a global log which clients can append-to and read-from over a network. Internally, CORFU is
    7  distributed over a cluster of machines in such a way that there is no single I/O bottleneck to either appends
    8  or reads. Data is fully replicated for fault tolerance, and a modest cluster of about 16–32 machines with SSD
    9  drives can sustain 1 million 4-KByte operations per second.
   10  The CORFU log enabled the construction of a variety of distributed applications that require strong
   11  consistency at high speeds, such as databases, transactional key-value stores, replicated state machines,
   12  and metadata services.

   21  1. INTRODUCTION
   22  Traditionally, system designers have been forced to believe that the only way to scale up
   23  reliable data stores, whether on-premise or cloud-hosted, is to shard the database. In
   24  this manner, recent systems like Percolator [Peng and Dabek 2010]; Megastore [Baker
   25  et al. 2011]; WAS [Calder et al. 2011]; and Spanner [Corbett et al. 2012] are able
   26  to drive parallel IO across enormous fleets of storage machines. Unfortunately, these
   27  designs defer to costly mechanisms like two-phase locking or centralized concurrency
   28  managers in order to provide strong consistency across partitions.
   29  At the same time, consensus protocols like Paxos [Lamport 1998] have been used as
   30  a building block for reliable distributed systems, typically deployed on a small number
   31  (between three to seven) servers, to provide a lever for a variety of consistent services:
   32  virtual block devices [Lee and Thekkath 1996]; replicated storage systems [Liskov et al.
   33  1991; Thekkath et al. 1997]; lock services [Burrows 2006]; coordination facilities [Hunt
   34  et al. 2010]; configuration management utilities [MacCormick et al. 2004]; and transaction coordinators [Peng and Dabek 2010; Baker et al. 2011; Corbett et al. 2012]. As
   35  these successful designs have become pillars of today's data centers and cloud backends, there is a growing recognition of the need for these systems to scale in number
   36  of machines, storage-volume, and bandwidth.

   50  In this article we present CORFU, a shared global log design which alters this
   51  performance-safety tradeoff: On one hand, CORFU is designed to scale to hundreds of
   52  thousands of concurrent client operations per second. On the other hand, a shared log
   53  is a powerful and versatile primitive for ensuring strong consistency in the presence of
   54  failures and asynchrony.
   55  Internally, CORFU is distributed over a cluster of machines and is fully replicated for
   56  fault-tolerance, without sharding data or sacrificing global consistency. CORFU allows
   57  hundreds of concurrent client machines in a large data-center to append to the tail of
   58  a shared log and to read from it over a network. Nevertheless, there is no single I/O
   59  bottleneck to either appends or reads, and the aggregated cluster throughput may be
   60  utilized by clients accessing the log.
   61  Historically, shared log designs have appeared in a diverse array of systems.
   62  QuickSilver [Haskin et al. 1988; Schmuck and Wylie 1991] and Camelot [Spector et al.
   63  1985] used shared logs for failure atomicity and node recovery. LBRM [Holbrook et al.
   64  1995] uses shared logs for recovery from multicast packet loss. Shared logs are also
   65  used in distributed storage systems for consistent remote mirroring [Ji et al. 2003]. In
   66  such systems, CORFU fits the shared log role perfectly, pooling together the aggregate
   67  cluster resources for higher throughput and lower latency.
   68  Moreover, a shared log is panacea for replicated transactional systems. For instance,
   69  Hyder [Bernstein et al. 2011] is a recently proposed high-performance database designed around a shared log, where servers speculatively execute transactions by appending them to the shared log and then use the log order to decide commit/abort
   70  status. In fact, Hyder was the original motivating application for CORFU and has been
   71  fully implemented over our code base.
   72  Interestingly, a shared log can also be used as a consensus engine, providing functionality identical to consensus protocols such as Paxos (geographically speaking, Corfu
   73  and Paxos are neighboring Greek islands). Used in this manner, CORFU provides a
   74  fast, fault-tolerant service for imposing and durably storing a total order on events in
   75  a distributed system. From this perspective, CORFU can be used as a drop-in replacement for existing Paxos implementations, with far better performance than previous
   76  solutions.

   77  Design. So far, we have argued the power, and hence the desirability, of a shared log.
   78  The key to its success is high performance, which we realize through a paradigm shift
   79  from existing cluster storage designs. In CORFU, each position in the shared log is
   80  projected onto a set of storage pages on different storage units. The projection map is
   81  maintained—consistently and compactly—at the clients. To read a particular position
   82  in the shared log, a client uses its local copy of the map to determine a corresponding
   83  physical storage page, and then directly issues a read to the storage unit storing
   84  that page. To append data, a client first determines the next available position in the
   85  shared log—using a sequencer node as an optimization for avoiding contention with
   86  other appending clients—and then writes data directly to the set of physical storage
   87  pages mapped to that position.
   88  In this way, the log in its entirety is managed without a leader, and CORFU circumvents the throughput cap of any single storage node. Instead, we can append data to
   89  the log at the aggregate bandwidth of the cluster, limited only by the speed at which
   90  the sequencer can assign them 64-bit tokens, that is, new positions in the log. The evaluation for this article was done with a user-space sequencer serving 200K tokens/s,
   91  whereas our more recent user-space sequencer is capable of 500K tokens/s. Moreover,
   92  we can support reads at the aggregate cluster bandwidth. Essentially, CORFU's design
   93  decouples ordering from I/O, extracting parallelism from the cluster for all IO while
   94  providing single-copy semantics for the shared log.

   97  Naturally, the real throughput that clients obtain may depend on application workloads. However, we argue that CORFU provides excellent throughput in many realistic
   98  scenarios. CORFU's append protocol generates nearly perfect sequential write-load,
   99  which can be turned into a high throughput, purely sequential access pattern at the
  100  server units with very little buffering effort. Meanwhile, reads can be served from a
  101  memory cache [Rosenblum and Ousterhout 1991; Seltzer et al. 1995], or from from a
  102  cold standby as in Junqueira [2012]. This leaves nonsequential accesses, which may
  103  result from log compaction procedures. Because we design for large clusters, compaction need not be performed during normal operations, and may be fulfilled by
  104  switching between sets of active storage drives. Finally, as we originally argued in
  105  Balakrishnan et al. [2012] for the use of nonvolatile flash memory as CORFU storage units, which alleviates altogether any performance degradation due to randomaccesses. Indeed, our present evaluation of CORFU is carried on servers equipped with
  106  SSD drives.
  107  The last matter we need to address is efficient failure handling. When storage units
  108  fail, clients must move consistently to a new projection from log positions to storage
  109  pages. CORFU achieves this via a reconfiguration mechanism (patterned after Vertical
  110  Paxos [Lamport et al. 2009] and Virtually Synchronous Reconfiguration [Birman et al.
  111  2010]) capable of restoring availability within tens of milliseconds on drive failures.
  112  A challenging failure mode peculiar to a client-centric design involves "holes" in the
  113  log; a client can obtain a log position from the sequencer for an append and then
  114  crash without completing the write to that position. To handle such situations, CORFU
  115  provides a fast hole-filling primitive that allows other clients to complete an unfinished
  116  append (or mark the log position as junk) within a millisecond.
  117  Applications. Applications can make use of the high throughput shared log design
  118  that CORFU offers in two fundamental ways. One is as a journal of commands which
  119  are applied in sequence to replicate a service, such as a replicated database. The other
  120  is as a log of data updates, to which clients dump revisions over the network, and
  121  CORFU durably stores data versions without overwriting in place. We exemplify both
  122  modes through two general-purpose service applications.
  123  (1) CORFU-Store. This is a key-value store that supports atomic multikey puts and
  124  gets, fast consistent checkpointing, and low-latency geo-distribution; these are
  125  properties that are difficult to achieve on conventional partitioned key-value stores.
  126  (2) CORFU-SMR. This is a State Machine Replication (SMR) library where replicas
  127  propose commands by appending to the log and execute commands by playing the
  128  log.
  129  The evaluation section reports on our experience with both of these services. In addition to these, we have also built a runtime called Tango that implements highly
  130  available, persistent data structures over CORFU, described in an accompanying paper
  131  [Balakrishnan et al. 2013].
  132  Evaluation. We evaluate a CORFU implementation on a cluster of 32 Intel X25-V
  133  drives attached to servers, showing that it saturates the aggregate storage bandwidth
  134  of the cluster at speeds of 400K 4KB reads per second and nearly 200K 4KB appends
  135  per second over the network. We also evaluate CORFU running over an FPGA-based
  136  network-attached storage unit, showing that it performs end-to-end reads under 0.5 ms
  137  and cross-rack mirrored appends under 1 ms. We show that CORFU is capable of
  138  recovering from holes within a millisecond and from crashed drives within 30 ms.
  139  Finally, we show that CORFU-Store provides atomic operations at the speed of the
  140  log (40K 10-key multigets/s and 20K 10-key multiputs/s, with 4KB values), and that
  141  CORFU-SMR runs at 70K 512-byte commands/s with 10 state machine replicas.

  146  Our design places most CORFU functionality at the clients, which reduces the complexity, cost, latency, and power consumption of the storage units. In fact, CORFU
  147  can operate over SSDs that are attached directly to the network, eliminating generalpurpose storage servers from the critical path. In a parallel effort outside the scope of
  148  this paper [Wei et al. 2013], we have prototyped a network-attached storage unit on

  151  less power and providing 33% lower latency on reads. Over a cluster of such storage
  152  units, CORFU's logging design acts as a distributed SSD, implementing functionality
  153  found inside conventional SSDs—such as wear-leveling—at cluster scale.

  154  Summary. To summarize the contributions of this article, we propose a complete
  155  design and implementation of a shared log abstraction over a storage cluster. This
  156  design is characterized by the property that maximum append throughput is not a
  157  function of any single node's I/O bandwidth. We describe low-latency fault-tolerance
  158  mechanisms for recovering from storage unit crashes and holes in the log. We present
  159  designs for a strongly consistent key-value store and a state machine replication library
  160  that use CORFU. Finally, we evaluate CORFU throughput, latency, fault-tolerance, and
  161  application performance on a 32-drive cluster of server-attached SSDs as well as an
  162  FPGA-based network-attached SSD.

  163  2. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION
  164  The setting for CORFU is a data center with a large number of application servers
  165  (which we call clients) and a cluster of storage units (see Figure 1). Our goal is to
  166  provide applications running on the clients with a shared log abstraction implemented
  167  over the storage cluster.
  168  Our design for this shared log abstraction aims to drive appends and random-reads
  169  to/from the log at the aggregate throughput that the storage cluster provides, while
  170  avoiding bottlenecks in the distributed software design. We strive to keep the server
  171  units' functionality as simple as possible, so that it can be realized by a standard host

  181  The entire solution is given succinctly in Figure 4. On the left-hand side are the

  184  complements it to provide for reliable replication and efficient space management.
  185  The client API includes the following operations. The append interface adds an entry
  186  to the log and returns its position. If the append operation encounters a problem, an
  187  error-code is returned, indicating that there is no certainty that the entry has been
  188  appended. The read interface accepts a position in the log and returns the entry at that
  189  position. If no entry exists at that position, an error code is returned. The application
  190  can perform garbage collection using trim, which indicates to CORFU that no valid
  191  data exists at a specific log position. Lastly, the application can fill a position in the log,
  192  which may be needed to fill gaps in the log with valid content. The semantics provided
  193  for read/write operations is linearizability [Herlihy and Wing 1990], which for a log
  194  means that once a log entry is fully written by a client or has been read, any future
  195  read will see the same content (unless it is reclaimed via a trim operation).
  196  CORFU's task of implementing a shared log abstraction with this API over a cluster
  197  of storage units—each of which exposes a separate address space—is described in the
  198  remainder of this section. We walk through the algorithms in detail below, starting with
  199  the storage-server, then moving to the way log entries are projected onto storage pages,
  200  the append functionality, and replication. We finish the description with mechanisms
  201  for reconfiguration and for garbage collection.

  202  2.1. Storage Unit Functionality
  203  The storage unit functionality is depicted in Figure 4 on the right-hand side. The most
  204  basic requirement of a storage unit is that it support READs and WRITEs on an infinite,
  205  logical address space of fixed-size pages. We use the term "storage page" to refer to
  206  a page on this logical address space; logical pages are mapped internally to physical
  207  pages.
  208  To support a specially-tailored replication protocol we have devised for the shared
  209  log, CORFU requires "write-once" semantics on the storage unit's address space. Reads
  210  on pages that have not yet been written should return an error code (err unwritten).
  211  Writes on pages that have already been written should also return an error code

  214  (err written). In addition to reads and writes, storage units are also required to expose
  215  a DELETE command, allowing clients to indicate that the storage page is not in use
  216  anymore. Reading a previously deleted page should return an err deleted error code.
  217  Implementing the infinite address space entails keeping a hash-map from 64-bit
  218  virtual addresses to the physical address space of the storage. With respect to writeonce semantics, an address is considered unwritten if it does not exist in the hash-map.
  219  When an address is written to the first time, an entry is created in the hash-map
  220  pointing the virtual address to a valid physical address. Each hash-map entry also has
  221  a bit indicating whether the address has been deleted or not, which is set by the DELETE
  222  command.
  223  Accordingly, a read succeeds if the address exists in the hash-map and does not have
  224  the deleted bit set. It returns err deleted if the deleted bit is set, and err unwritten if
  225  the address does not exist in the hash-map. A write to an address that exists in the
  226  hash-map returns err deleted if the deleted bit is set and err written if it is not. If the
  227  address is not in the hash-map, the write succeeds.
  228  To eventually remove deleted addresses from the hash-map, the storage unit also
  229  maintains a watermark before which no unwritten addresses exist, and removes
  230  deleted addresses from the hash-map that are lower than the watermark. Reads
  231  and writes to addresses before the watermark that are not in the hash-map return
  232  err deleted immediately.
  233  The storage unit also efficiently supports fill operations that write junk by treating such writes differently from first-class writes. Junk writes are initially treated as
  234  conventional writes, either succeeding or returning err written or err deleted as appropriate. However, instead of writing to the block device, the storage unit points the
  235  hash-map entry to a special address reserved for junk; this ensures that storage pages
  236  are not wasted. Also, once the hash-map is updated, the entry is immediately deleted
  237  in the scope of the same operation. This removes the need for clients to explicitly track
  238  and trim junk in the log.
  239  In addition, storage units are required to support a SEAL command. Each incoming
  240  message to a storage unit is tagged with an epoch number. When a particular epoch
  241  number is sealed at a storage unit, it must reject all subsequent messages sent with
  242  an epoch equal or lower to the sealed epoch. In addition, the storage unit is expected
  243  to send back an acknowledgment for the seal command to the sealing entity, including
  244  the highest logical page address that has been written on its address space thus far.
  245  The seal capability is simple: the storage unit maintains an epoch number s epoch
  246  and rejects all messages tagged with equal or lower epochs, sending back an err sealed
  247  error code. When a seal command arrives with a new epoch to seal, the storage unit
  248  first flushes all ongoing operations and then updates its s epoch. It then responds to
  249  the seal command with highaddr, the highest address written in the address space it
  250  exposes; this is required for reconfiguration, as explained below.

  284  2.3. Appending without Replication

  286  of storage pages. This allows clients to directly access the page(s) associated with any
  287  position in a logical address space. Supporting reads is immediate from this. However,

  293  It is possible to allow clients to contend for positions in an unconstrained manner so

  298  case, when a CORFU instance is started, every client that wishes to append data will
  299  try to concurrently write to position 0. One client will win, while the rest fail; these
  300  clients then try again on position 1, and so on. This approach provides linearizable log
  301  semantics, i.e., writes complete successfully with the guarantee that any subsequent
  302  read on the position returns the value written, until the position is trimmed.
  303  However, it is clear that such an approach will result in poor performance when
  304  there are hundreds of clients concurrently attempting appends to the log. To eliminate
  305  such contention at the tail of the log, CORFU uses a dedicated sequencer that assigns
  306  clients "tokens," corresponding to empty log positions. The sequencer can be thought
  307  of as a simple networked counter. To append data, a client first goes to the sequencer,
  308  which returns its current value and increments itself. The client has now reserved a
  309  position in the log and can write to it without contention from other clients.
  310  Importantly, the sequencer does not represent a single point of failure; it is merely an
  311  optimization to reduce contention in the system and is not required for either safety or
  312  progress. For fast recovery from sequencer failure, we store the identity of the current
  313  sequencer in the projection and use reconfiguration to change sequencers. The starting
  314  counter of a new sequencer is determined using the highest page written on each
  315  storage unit, which is returned by the storage unit in response to the seal command
  316  during reconfiguration.
  317  However, CORFU's sequencer-based approach does introduce a new failure mode,
  318  since "holes" can appear in the log when clients obtain tokens and then fail to use them
  319  immediately due to crashes or slowdowns. Holes can cripple applications that consume
  320  the log in strict order, such as state machine replication or transaction processing,
  321  since no progress can be made until the status of the hole is resolved. Given that a
  322  large system is likely to have a few malfunctioning clients at any given time, holes can
  323  severely disrupt application performance. A simple solution is to have other clients fill
  324  holes aggressively with a reserved junk value. To prevent aggressive hole-filling from
  325  burning up storage pages and network bandwidth, storage units can be junk-aware,
  326  simply updating internal metadata to mark a logical address as filled with junk instead
  327  of writing an actual value to the storage.
  328  Note that filling holes reintroduces contention for log positions: if a client is merely
  329  late in using a reserved token and has not really crashed, it could end up competing
  330  with another client trying to fill the position with junk, which is equivalent to two
  331  clients concurrently writing to the same position. In other words, the sequencer is an
  332  optimization that removes contention for log positions in the common case, but does
  333  not eliminate it entirely.

  334  2.4. Appending with Replication
  335  Naturally, the single-unit solution above does not provide fault tolerance. We achieve
  336  fault tolerance by projecting each position to a replica set of storage pages in the
  337  cluster using the current projection. The full append protocol is more intricate due to
  338  replication, as we now need to establish agreement on content among its replica set.
  339  Although any replication protocol solves this correctly, several aspects of our settings
  340  guided us to devise a novel variant. First, since deploying a storage unit for data has
  341  nonmarginal cost, we require a consensus solution that tolerates f failures with just
  342  f +1 replicas. This means that we do not deploy any majority-based algorithm. Second,
  343  the CORFU design poses minimal functionality requisites on storage units, hence we

  344  ACM Transactions on Computer Systems, Vol. 31, No. 4, Article 10, Publication date: December 2013.
=== PAGE 10 ===

  345  10:10 M. Balakrishnan et al.

  346  require replicas to not communicate with one another. Finally, we need to support fast
  347  reads that do not require a client to go to more than one replica to retrieve data.
  348  To address these needs, CORFU uses a simple chaining protocol, which is in essence
  349  a client-driven variant of Chain Replication [van Renesse and Schneider 2004]. When
  350  a client wants to write to a replica set of storage pages (after having obtained a token
  351  for this position), it updates them in a deterministic order, waiting for each storage
  352  unit to respond before moving to the next one. The write is successfully completed
  353  when the last storage unit in the chain is updated. As a result, if two clients attempt
  354  to concurrently update the same replica set of storage pages, one of them will arrive
  355  second at the first unit of the chain and receive an err written.
  356  To read from the replica set, clients have two options. If the reading client already
  357  knows that the log position has been successfully written to (for example, via an out-ofband notification by the writing client), it can go to any replica in the chain for better
  358  read performance. Alternatively, a reader may probe the last unit of the chain to check
  359  if a write has completed. If indeed a write has completed on that log position, the last
  360  unit can return the content. If the last unit has not yet been updated, it will return an
  361  err unwritten.
  362  We need to address three kinds of failures. First, a client failure midway through an
  363  append can result in a replica chain partially or fully empty. Chained appends allow a
  364  client to rapidly fill such holes. To fill holes, the client starts by checking the first unit of
  365  the chain to determine if a valid value exists in the prefix of the chain. If such a value
  366  exists, the client walks down the chain to find the first unwritten replica, and then
  367  "completes" the append by copying over the value to the remaining unwritten replicas
  368  in chain order. Alternatively, if the first unit of the chain is unwritten, the client writes
  369  the junk value to all the replicas in chain order. CORFU exposes this fast hole-filling
  370  functionality to applications via a fill interface. Applications can use this primitive as
  371  aggressively as required, depending on their sensitivity to holes in the shared log.
  372  Second, storage units may fail (or, they may require an upgrade, or become filled up
  373  and need replacement). We address configuration changes in the next section.
  374  Third, due to reconfiguration, a client might fail to read a position even though
  375  a write on it has completed. This may happen as follows. Say that a projection is
  376  changed to remove a working unit, for example, due to a network disruption that
  377  causes the unit to temporarily detach. Some clients may remain uninformed about the
  378  reconfiguration, perhaps having detached from the main partition of the system due to
  379  the same network disruption. If this unit stored the last page in a replica set for some
  380  position in the previous projection, this client might continue to probe this unit for the
  381  status of this log position. Then the client may obtain an err unwritten error code, even
  382  after the position has been completely written in the new configuration.
  383  There are several ways to address this problem. Even with this somewhat rare failure
  384  scenario, the system upholds serializability of reads, which may suffice. Alternatively,
  385  we may disable fast reads altogether, and require reads to go to all replicas, unless they
  386  already know an entry has been filled. Another solution is to assign leases to storage
  387  units, which automatically expire unless the unit periodically renews them. Some
  388  entity in the system, for example, the sequencer, must manage these leases, which is
  389  not a heavy burden, as renewals are performed at fairly coarse intervals (typically in
  390  the order of seconds). However, a cost associated with this approach is that we need to
  391  wait for a lease expiration before we can remove a unit from the configuration.

  397  2.5. Changing Projections
  398  When some event occurs that necessitates a change in a projection—for example, when
  399  a storage unit fails, or when the tail of the log moves past the current active range—we
  400  switch to a new projection. The succession of projections forms a sequence of epochs,
  401  and clients can read and write the storage units directly so long as their projection is
  402  up-to-date. When we change the projection, we invoke a seal request on all relevant
  403  storage units, so that clients with obsolete copies of a projection will be prevented from
  404  continuing to access them. All messages from clients to storage units are tagged with
  405  the epoch number, so messages from sealed epochs can be aborted.
  406  In this sense, a projection serves as a view of the current configuration. However, a
  407  projection change does not necessarily ditch an old configuration and replace it with
  408  a new one. Rather, it may link a new range in a succession of ranges, keeping the old
  409  ones intact, and letting clients continue reading log entries which have already been
  410  filled. Likewise, it may affect the configuration of some past ranges and not others. So
  411  over time the log evolves in disjoint ranges, each one using its own projection onto a
  412  set of storage extents. Care must be taken to ensure that any clients operating in the
  413  context of previous epochs and clients in the new epoch read the log consistently.

  466  2.6. Garbage Collection
  467  CORFU provides the abstraction of an infinitely growing log to applications. The application does not have to move data around in the address space of the log to free
  468  up space. All it is required to do is to use the trim interface to inform CORFU when
  469  individual log positions are no longer in use. As a result, CORFU makes it easy for
  470  developers to build applications over the shared log without worrying about garbage
  471  collection strategies. An implication of this approach is that as the application appends
  472  to the log and trims positions selectively, the address space of the log can become
  473  increasingly sparse.

  474  ACM Transactions on Computer Systems, Vol. 31, No. 4, Article 10, Publication date: December 2013.

  476  Accordingly, CORFU has to efficiently support a sparse address space for the shared
  477  log. The solution is a two-level mapping. As described before, CORFU uses projections
  478  to map from a single infinite address space to individual extents on each storage unit.
  479  Each storage-unit then maps a sparse 64-bit address space, broken into extents, onto
  480  the physical set of pages. The storage unit has to maintain a hash map from 64-bit
  481  addresses to the physical address space of the storage.
  482  Another place where system information might grow large is the succession of projections. Each projection by itself is a range-to-range mapping, and hence it is quite
  483  concise. However, it is possible that an adversarial workload can result in bloated projections; for instance, if each range in the projection has a single valid entry that is
  484  never trimmed, the mapping for that range has to be retained in the projection for
  485  perpetuity.
  486  Even for such adversarial workloads, it is easy to bound the size of the projection
  487  tightly by introducing a small amount of proactive data movement across storage units
  488  in order to merge consecutive ranges in the projection. For instance, we estimate that
  489  adding 0.1% writes to the system can keep the projection under 25 MB on a 1 TB
  490  cluster for an adversarial workload. In practice, we do not expect projections to exceed
  491  10s of KBs for conventional workloads; this is borne out by our experience in building
  492  applications over CORFU. In any case, handling multi-MB projections is not difficult,
  493  since they are static data structures that can be indexed efficiently. Additionally, new
  494  projections can be written as deltas to the auxiliary share.

  495  3. CORFU APPLICATIONS
  496  There are two fundamental modes in which distributed applications can cooperate
  497  over a shared log, one as a log structured store, and another as a totally ordered
  498  reliable broadcast channel. We explain both modes and exemplify their use through
  499  two general-purpose services, one for each mode, which we built over CORFU.
  500  The first usage mode is a log-structured store. It holds a universe of data items which
  501  are updated over time by appending new versions to the log. This has several benefits:
  502  It automatically maintains a journal of versions and updates. It creates a sequential
  503  update workload instead of writing to random places. It can easily support multiitem
  504  operation atomicity. Last, it can easily support snapshot and mirroring operations by
  505  capturing and copying growing prefixes of the log.
  506  We have prototyped a general-purpose service using this mode, named CORFU-Store,
  507  a key-value store. This is a key-value store that supports a number of properties that
  508  are difficult to achieve on partitioned stores, including atomic multikey puts and gets,
  509  distributed snapshots, geo-distribution and distributed rollback/replay. In CORFU-
  510  Store, a map-service (which can be replicated) maintains a mapping from keys to
  511  shared log offsets. To atomically put multiple keys, clients must first append the keyvalue pairs to the CORFU log, append a commit record, and then send the commit
  512  record to the map-service. To atomically get multiple keys, clients must query the
  513  map-service for the latest key-offset mappings, and then perform CORFU reads on the
  514  returned offsets. This protocol ensures linearizability for single-key puts and gets, as
  515  well as atomicity for multikey puts and gets.
  516  CORFU-Store's shared log design makes it easy to take consistent point-in-time
  517  snapshots across the entire key space, simply by playing the shared log up to some
  518  position. The key-value store can be geo-distributed asynchronously by copying the log,

  519  ACM Transactions on Computer Systems, Vol. 31, No. 4, Article 10, Publication date: December 2013.
=== PAGE 14 ===

  520  10:14 M. Balakrishnan et al.

  521  ensuring that the mirror is always at some prior snapshot of the system, with bounded
  522  lag. Additionally, CORFU-Store ensures even wear-out across the cluster despite highly
  523  skewed write workloads.
  524  The evaluation section reports more on our experience with CORFU-Store.
  525  The second use case of CORFU is as a reliable, totally ordered broadcast channel.
  526  This is a quintessential tool for consistent replication, identical to the manner in which
  527  Paxos is used for implementing replicated state machines. Each SMR server simply
  528  plays the log forward to receive the next command to execute. It proposes new commands into the state machine by appending them to the log. Here, the log serves both
  529  as the authoritative source of ordering in the system and as a durable store; an SMR
  530  state can be soft, and may always be recovered by playing the log. In this capacity,
  531  CORFU is an enabler for a class of high throughput, in-memory replicated services.
  532  For example, we have implemented the Hyder database [Bernstein et al. 2011], which
  533  was our primary initial motivating use case, over CORFU.
  534  We also built and evaluated an SMR library over CORFU, which deposits opaque
  535  commands in the log and uploads commands from the log for execution by the SMR
  536  servers. In our current implementation, each SMR server plays the log forward by
  537  issuing reads to the log. We present some performance numbers regarding this SMR
  538  library in the evaluation section.
  539  Many of the research issues involved with CORFU applications are beyond the scope
  540  of this article, and will be reported separately. Briefly, when used as a log-structured
  541  store, the challenges concentrate around two dimensions. One is to maintain a mapping from data items to their most recent location; and to implement the distributed
  542  mapping, so that it doesn't become a bottleneck for users. Another is to compact and
  543  clean-up the log so that it does not grow sparse with old entries left at the tail of the
  544  log. When used for replication, one of the main challenges is to be able to keep up with
  545  the log by the replicated state-machines. We are investigating different approaches to
  546  playing the log forward, perhaps by having dedicated machines multicast the contents
  547  of the log out to all SMR servers. In both use modes, common challenges are to provide
  548  for checkpointing application state, so that recovering from a failure with the help
  549  of the log becomes fast, and to allow selective recovery from the log for a variety of
  550  purposes.
  551  There are other potential applications. One application in the works is a shared block
  552  device that exposes a conventional fixed-size address space; this can then be used to
  553  expose multiple, independent virtual disks to client VMs [Meyer et al. 2008]. Another
  554  application is reliable multicast, where senders append to a single, channel-specific
  555  log before multicasting. A receiver can detect lost packets by observing gaps in the
  556  sequence of log positions and retrieve them from the log.

  701  of metadata; accordingly, seven commands can fit into a single CORFU log entry with
  702  batching. In the experiment, each client generates 7K commands/sec; as we add clients
  703  on the x-axis, the total rate of commands injected into the system increases by 7K.
  704  On the y-axis, we plot the average rate (with error bars signifying the min and max
  705  across replicas) at which commands are executed by each replica. While the system has

  707  system matches the rate at which they are injected. Beyond ten replicas, we overload
  708  the ability of a client to execute incoming commands, and hence throughput flatlines;
  709  this means that clients are now lagging behind while playing the state machine.

  710  5. RELATED WORK
  711  At its base, CORFU derives its success by scaling Paxos to data center settings and
  712  providing a durable engine for totally ordering client requests at tens of Gigabits per
  713  second throughput. The introduction already mentions a variety of real-life uses of
  714  Paxos for which CORFU provides a high-performance drop-in replacement, and we do
  715  not repeat them here.
  716  But how far is CORFU from Paxos? A CORFU log implements a fault-tolerant total
  717  ordering service to which clients propose to append values (page-payloads) and receive
  718  them back in a unique sequence order. This service is at the core of the State-Machine-
  719  Replication (SMR [Schneider 1990]) approach, which forms a total ordering on client
  720  requests and delivers as output an ordered sequence of requests. Covering the multitude of existing SMR solutions is beyond our scope, and we refer the reader to an
  721  excellent survey by DeFago et al. [2003]. Our design borrows bits and pieces from various existing methods, yet we invented a new solution in order to scale SMR to large

  722  ACM Transactions on Computer Systems, Vol. 31, No. 4, Article 10, Publication date: December 2013.
=== PAGE 20 ===

  723  10:20 M. Balakrishnan et al.

  724  cluster scales and deliver aggregate cluster throughput to hundreds of clients. This
  725  was made possible via two principles.
  726  —First, we have many small replica-sets, typically each one with 2–5 servers, each
  727  responsible for one or more log entries. These entries form an abstract log, and we
  728  can write and read different entries in the log in parallel, thus utilizing the total
  729  bandwidth available in the cluster.
  730  —Second, we make writing to the log fast by avoiding contention on any entry.
  731  Contention-free appends are managed using the CORFU-sequencer, which guides
  732  clients smoothly from one offset to the next. It is important to emphasize that the
  733  CORFU-sequencer does not have a precise analogy in traditional consensus protocols.
  734  The sequencer might look like a leader, but the big difference is that the sequencer
  735  does not process or store client requests, nor interact with data replicas; it only
  736  handles sequence-number "reservations". This crucial distinction lets the CORFU
  737  sequencer operate at extremely high rates, free of communication with data replicas.
  738  Putting these two principles together, the replication protocol we devised for CORFU
  739  avoids any centralized IO bottleneck without partitioning data.
  740  Stitching many small replica sets into a global order has also been the goal in a number of recent works, notably Mencius [Mao et al. 2008]; Kapritsos and Junqueira [2010];
  741  and Calvin [Thomson et al. 2012]. Mencius rotates the tail of the log among the sets in
  742  a round-robin manner, requiring each set to declare a proposal or an empty announcement in its turn. In the latter two, replicas advance in lock-step from one global wave
  743  after another, essentially emulating Lamport's total ordering protocol [Lamport 1978].
  744  The consensus protocol we use for replicating each log-entry is itself a somewhat
  745  novel consensus variant. The protocol works similarly to chain replication [van Renesse
  746  and Schneider 2004], with passive data-stores instead of servers at each link, and
  747  with the client copying the value from link to link instead of servers forwarding it
  748  to one another. Each replica enforces write-once semantics. Clients reach consensus
  749  by attempting to write to the head of the chain, and copying the head to the rest of
  750  the chain. Client-driven consensus protocols exist already [Gafni and Lamport 2000;
  751  Chockler and Malkhi 2005], but these previous approaches do no scale well with the
  752  number of clients.
  753  Working with our architecture brought additional challenges, which were met with
  754  more deviations from standard Paxos. First, our design creates a new failure mode,
  755  a "hole," which happens when a client reserves a sequence offset and fails without
  756  completing a consensus decision on it. We fill such a hole by contending for the consensus decision with a competing "junk" mark. This leverages the power of consensus for
  757  lightweight fault-handling, without requiring system reconfiguration. Second, when
  758  reconfiguration is needed, we employ an auxiliary configuration manager, which lets
  759  us architect CORFU with only F+1 data replicas for each log-entry (rather than 2F+1,
  760  as in Paxos). This design makes sense because the auxiliary is not a contention point,
  761  and participates only in low-volume operations, hence it can be shared throughout the
  762  cluster and itself be hardened using replication. The foundations for this approach have
  763  been formalized in Lamport et al. [2010]; Birman et al. [2010]; Lamport et al. [2009].
  764  The broader context in which CORFU lies is back-end infrastructure systems for
  765  web applications that are characterized by the need to strike a balance between performance and consistency at unprecedented magnitudes. In this arena, the dominance

  781  semantics were needed after all. Percolator [Peng and Dabek 2010] and ReTSO

  783  a centralized serialization authority to manage concurrency. More recently, systems
  784  designed for large-scale data services which provide support for transactions were designed from bare bones (e.g., Megastore [Baker et al. 2011]; WAS [Calder et al. 2011];
  785  Walter [Sovran et al. 2011]; and Spanner [Corbett et al. 2012]). These systems employ costly concurrency control mechanisms like two-phase locking for cross-partition
  786  transaction support. A very recent distributed transactional system, Calvin, avoids
  787  two-phase locking using the idea of deterministic databases [Thomson et al. 2012],
  788  already mentioned above. Calvin leverages a common source of ordering for lock acquisition, which is implemented via a real-time distributed sequencer; CORFU can
  789  provide an asynchronous replacement for Calvin's sequencer.

  791  high throughput in-memory lock-free transactional service over a shared log using optimistic concurrency control. Indeed, we have a fully working prototype of Hyder on top
  792  of CORFU. With CORFU operating an a shared log driving Hyder's optimistic concurrency control, we can sustain hundreds of thousands of transactions per second. In fact,
  793  Hyder was the motivating application that initially inspired the CORFU construction,
  794  although CORFU departs extensively from the Hyder-Log proposal in the mechanisms
  795  it employs, such as projections, sequencer, chain replication, the hole-filling protocol,
  796  and write-once units with infinite address spaces.
  797  Finally, there is a strong connection between CORFU and log-structured file-systems
  798  (LFS) [Rosenblum and Ousterhout 1991]. Our rekindled interest in this approach is
  799  partly due to emerging solid-state memory technologies, although not limited to it.

  804  a distributed setting, striping logs across a collection of storage servers. Like these systems, CORFU stripes updates across drives in a log-structured manner. Unlike them,

  809  6. CONCLUSION
  810  Despite almost forty years of research into replicated storage schemes, the only approach so far to scale up capacity and throughput has been to shard data and trade
  811  consistency for performance. In this article, we presented the CORFU system, which
  812  breaks this seeming tradeoff by organizing a cluster of drives as a single, shared log.
  813  CORFU offers single-copy semantics at cluster-scale speeds, providing a scalable source
  814  of atomicity and durability for distributed systems. CORFU's novel client-centric design eliminates any single I/O bottleneck between numerous clients and the cluster,
  815  allowing data to stream to and from the cluster in parallel.
