   76  Conflict-free Replicated Data Types 3

   77  1 Introduction

   78  Replication and consistency are essential features of any large distributed system, such as
   79  the WWW, peer-to-peer, or cloud computing platforms. The standard "strong consistency"
   80  approach serialises updates in a global total order [10]. This constitutes a performance
   81  and scalability bottleneck. Furthermore, strong consistency conflicts with availability and
   82  partition-tolerance [8].
   83  When network delays are large or partitioning is an issue, as in delay-tolerant networks,
   84  disconnected operation, cloud computing, or P2P systems, eventual consistency promises
   85  better availability and performance [17, 20]. An update executes at some replica, without
   86  synchronisation; later, it is sent to the other replicas. All updates eventually take effect
   87  at all replicas, asynchronously and possibly in different orders. Concurrent updates may
   88  conflict; conflict arbitration may require a consensus and a roll-back.1
   89  This weaker consistency is considered acceptable for some classes of applications. However, conflict resolution is hard. The literature offers little guidance on designing a correct
   90  optimistic system. Ad-hoc approaches are brittle and error-prone; witness for instance the
   91  concurrency anomalies of the Amazon Shopping Cart [3].
   92  We propose a simple, theoretically-sound approach to eventual consistency. Our system
   93  model, Strong Eventual Consistency or SEC, avoids the complexity of conflict resolution and
   94  of roll-back. Conflict-freedom ensures safety and liveness despite any number of failures. It
   95  leverages simple mathematical properties that ensure absence of conflict, i.e., monotonicity
   96  in a semi-lattice and/or commutativity. A trivial example is a replicated counter, which (assuming no overflow) converges because its increment and decrement operations commute.
   97  In our conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs), an update does not require synchronisation, and CRDT replicas provably converge to a correct common state. CRDTs remain
   98  responsive, available and scalable despite high network latency, faults, or disconnection.
   99  Non-trivial CRDTs are known to exist: for instance, we previously published Treedoc, a
  100  sequence CRDT for co-operative text editing [14]. Our aim here is to expand our knowledge
  101  of the principles and practice of CRDTs. We claim the following contributions for this paper:
  102  – A solution to the CAP problem, Strong Eventual Consistency (SEC).
  103  – Formal definitions of Strong Eventual Consistency (SEC) and of CRDTs.
  104  – Two sufficient conditions for SEC.
  105  – A strong equivalence between the two conditions.
  106  – We show that SEC is incomparable to sequential consistency.
  107  – Description of basic CRDTs, including integer vectors and counters.
  108  – More advanced CRDTs, including sets and graphs.
  109  We refer the interested reader to a separate technical report [18] for a comprehensive
  110  portfolio of CRDT designs.
  111  1. A conflict is a combination of concurrent updates, which may be individually correct, but that, taken
  112  together, would violate some invariant.

  125  2 System model

  126  We consider a system of processes interconnected by an asynchronous network. The
  127  network can partition and recover. We assume a finite set Π = {p0, . . . , pn−1} of nonbyzantine processes. Processes in Π may crash silently; a crashed process may remain
  128  crashed forever, or may recover with its memory intact. A non-crashed process is said
  129  correct.

  130  2.1 State-based object
  131  In this section we specify replicated objects in the so-called state-based style. The intuition is illustrated in Figure 1. Executing an update modifies the state of a single replica.
  132  Every replica occasionally sends its local state to some other replica, which merges the state
  133  thus received into its own state. In this way, every update eventually reaches every replica,
  134  either directly or indirectly.
  135  With no loss of generality, we consider a single object with one replica at each process.
  136  An object is a tuple (S, s0, q, u, m). The replica at process p has state s ∈ S, called its
  137  i i
  138  payload; the initial state is s0. A client of the object may read the state of the object via
  139  query method q and modify it via update method u. Method m serves to merge the state
  140  from a remote replica. A method (whether q, u or m) executes at a single replica.
  141  Systems that deliver every update to every replica eventually in a fault-tolerant manner
  142  are well-known in the literature, for instance gossip or anti-entropy approaches [5, 13]. For
  143  simplicity, we will assume hereafter a fully connected communication graph, where every arc
  144  is a fair-lossy channel. Infinitely often, the replica at pi sends (if it is correct) its current state
  145  to pj; replica pj (if it is correct) merges the received state into its local state by executing
  146  method m.
  147  A method whose precondition is satisfied is said enabled. We assume that an enabled
  148  method executes as soon as it is invoked. Method executions at some replica are numbered
  149  sequentially from 1. The kth method execution at replica i will be noted fk (a), where f
  150  i
  151  is either q, u or m, and a denotes the arguments. We note Ki(f) the ordinal of execution
  152  f at replica i, i.e., K (fk(a)) = k for i = j, and is undefined otherwise. (Abusing notai j

  184  2.2 Strong Eventual Consistency
  185  Informally, eventual consistency means that replicas eventually reach the same final value
  186  if clients stop submitting updates. We capture this intuition as follows:
  187  Definition 2.2 (Eventual Consistency (EC)). Eventual delivery: An update delivered at
  188  some correct replica is eventually delivered to all correct replicas: ∀i, j : f ∈ ci ⇒ ♦f ∈
  189  cj.
  190  Convergence: Correct replicas that have delivered the same updates eventually reach equivalent state: ∀i, j : c = c ⇒ ♦s ≡ s .
  191  i j i j
  192  Termination: All method executions terminate.
  193  Several EC systems will execute an update immediately, only to discover later that it
  194  conflicts with another, and to roll back to resolve this conflict [19]. This constitutes a waste
  195  of resources, and in general requires a consensus to ensure that all replicas arbitrate conflicts
  196  in the same way. To avoid this, we require a stronger condition:
  197  Definition 2.3 (Strong eventual consistency (SEC)). An object is Strongly Eventually Consistent if it is Eventually Consistent and:
  198  Strong Convergence: Correct replicas that have delivered the same updates have equivalent state: ∀i, j : c = c ⇒ s ≡ s .

  260  t is a side-effect-free prepare-update method and u is an effect-update method. (their arguments may differ, e.g., t(a) and u(a0 ) in Figure 2). The prepare-update executes at the
  261  single replica where the operation is invoked (its source). At the source, prepare-update
  262  method t is followed immediately by effect-update method u, i.e., fk−1 = t ⇒ fk = u. (If
  263  i i
  264  this were not true, there would be no causality between successive updates.)

  323  3 Some results

  324  3.1 Fault-tolerance and the CAP theorem
  325  The CAP theorem states that it is impossible to simultaneously ensure strong consistency
  326  (C), availability (A) and tolerate network partition (P) [8]. As, network faults unavoidably
  327  occur in a large-scale environment, a real system must sacrifice either consistency or availability. Availability is often the top priority in practice [3]: does this mean giving up all
  328  consistency guarantees?
  329  No: SEC provides a solution. A SEC replica is always available for both reads and writes,

  392  3.3 SEC is incomparable to sequential consistency
  393  A state-based replica executes a sequence of query, update, and merge methods. In
  394  addition to its sequential behaviour, a CRDT specifies concurrent behaviours that must
  395  satisfy the strong convergence property. As we show now, this permits executions that
  396  would be impossible in a sequentially-consistent system.
  397  Consider a Set CRDT S with operations add(e) and remove(e). Immediately after
  398  add(e), the state will satisfy e ∈ S; after remove(e) the state satisfies e /∈ S. In a sequential
  399  execution, the last update wins, e.g., after remove(e) → add(e) the state satisfies e ∈
  400  S. Concurrent adds or removes of different elements are independent, e.g., after add(e) k
  401  remove(e0) the state satisfies e ∈ S ∧ e0 ∈/ S.
  402  There is a choice of alternative semantics for concurrent updates of the same element.
  403  When concurrently adding and removing the same element, the add could win, or the remove
  404  could win, or the update of the replica with the highest IP address could win, or the state
  405  might be reset to a distinguished state ⊥, and so on. All these alternatives satisfy the strong
  406  convergence condition, and any of them may be reasonable for some application.
  407  Let us consider the add-wins alternative: after add(e) k remove(e) the state satisfies e ∈
  408  S. Now consider the following scenario. Replica p executes the sequence add(e); remove(e0).
  409  0
  410  Concurrently, replica p executes add(e0 );remove(e). Then, replica p merges the state
  411  1 3
  412  from p0 and p1. According to the concurrent specification, the final state at p3 satisfies

  418  Now consider the converse. In the absence of crashes, a sequentially-consistent object is

  420  all replicas must terminate with the same state. However, in the general case, sequential
  421  consistency requires consensus, which cannot be solved in the presence of n − 1 crashes.
  422  Therefore, SEC is incomparable with sequential consistency.

  423  4 Example CRDTs

  424  We now recall some basic CRDTs that are known in the existing literature, which we
  425  will later compose to build higher-level objects. We will use state- or op-based specifications
  426  as most convenient. Generally, we find the state-based style more compact and easier to
  427  reason about formally, whereas the op-based style is often convenient for implementation.

  428  4.1 Integer vectors and counters
  429  Consider the state-oriented specification of a vector-of-integers object: (Nn ,[0, . . . ,0],≤n
  430  ,[0, . . . ,0], value, inc, maxn). Vectors v, v0 ∈ Nn are (partially) ordered by v ≤n v0 ⇔ ∀j ∈
  431  [0..n − 1], v[i] ≤ v0 [i]. A query invocation value() returns a copy of the local payload. An
  432  update inc(i) increments the payload entry at index i, that is, s•inc(i) = [s0 [0], . . . , s0[n−1]]
  433  where s0[j] = s[j] + 1 if i = j and s0 [j] = s[j] otherwise. Merging two vectors takes the
  434  per-index maximum, i.e., s • maxn(s0) = [max(s[0], s0[0]), . . . ,max(s[n − 1], s0[n − 1])]. We
  435  omit the proof that it is a CRDT.
  436  If each process pi is restricted to incrementing its own index inc(i), this is the well-known
  437  vector clock [11].
  438  An increment-only integer counter is very similar; the only difference being that query
  439  invocation value() of a vector in state v returns |v| def= P v[j]. We construct an intej
  440  ger counter that can be both incremented and decremented, by basically associating two
  441  increment-only counters I and D, where incrementing increments I and decrementing increments D, whereas value() returns |I| − |D|. The ordering method ≤ is defined as
  442  (I, D) ≤ (I0, D0) def= I ≤n I0 ∧ D ≤n D0 .

  443  4.2 U-Set, map and log
  444  Another simple CRDT construct is an add-only set object (S, ⊆,∅,value, add(e),∪).
  445  The payload is any set; sets are ordered by inclusion. A query value() returns a copy of
  446  the local payload. Update add(e) adds element e to the set, i.e., s • add(e) = s ∪ {e}. It
  447  is well-known that sets ordered by ⊆ form a semi-lattice with ∪ as the LUB operator. It is
  448  clearly monotonic by the definition of add. Therefore, the add-only set is a CRDT.
  449  Wuu and Bernstein build further CRDTs by combination of these basic components [22].
  450  They propose a set with both add and remove operations by associating two add-only sets

  468  keeps the largest version. Then, a replica may discard a log entry as soon as its timestamp
  469  is less than all the remote vector clocks. This algorithm does not require a consensus, but
  470  it is live only if no process is crashed. However, this may be acceptable, since the liveness
  471  of garbage collection does not impact the correctness of the main algorithm.
  472  This algorithm may be adapted to other data types, for instance to discarding the A and
  473  R entries of a removed element in the U-Set.

  536  5.2 Design alternatives for arc removal
  537  A directed graph is a pair of sets (V, A), called vertices and arcs respectively, such
  538  that A ⊆ V × V . Updates must maintain the invariant that the head and tail vertices
  539  of an arc both exist. Therefore, adding an arc to A has the precondition that its two
  540  vertices are in V ; conversely, a vertex may be removed only if it supports no arc; these
  541  are preconditions to prepare-update. Furthermore, the system must ensure that concurrent
  542  addArc(v0, v00) k removeVertex(v0 ) do not violate the invariant. Several alternatives may be
  543  considered: (i) Give precedence to removeVertex(v0): all edges to or from v0 are removed as
  544  a side effect. This is easy to implement, by hiding any arc that includes a removed vertex.
  545  (ii) Give precedence to addArc(v0, v00): if either v0 or v00 has been removed, it is restored.
  546  This requires recreating nodes that have being explicitly deleted. (iii) removeVertex(v0) is
  547  delayed until all concurrent addArc operations have executed. This requires synchronisation
  548  which violates the goals of asynchrony and fault tolerance. There is no perfect choice.
  549  Hereafter, we choose Option (i) because it is adequate in our application scenario.

  555  has an unique internal identifier. If the same vertex is added twice, the two additions will
  556  be distinguished by their two unique identifiers. A lookup will mask the duplicates.
  557  To remove vertex v, the prepare-update computes the set R of pairs that contain v, i.e.,
  558  all copies known in the source replica; the effect-update method removes this same set R from
  559  the set of vertices in all replicas. As operations are delivered in causal order, when the effectupdate method executes in some replica, for each pair in R, the correspondent addVertex
  560  operations has already executed. Thus, unlike the state-based solution of Section 4.2, a set
  561  need not keep tombstones.
  562  If the same vertex is removed and added concurrently, the addVertex wins, as the new
  563  unique identifier is not included in the set computed by the remove's prepare-update. This
  564  approach is consistent with a sequential execution, as the a vertex can removed only if it is
  565  observed. The same approach is used for arcs.
  566  To remove a vertex, the source replica checks that the vertex is observed, and also that
  567  it is not the head of any existing arc. Conversely, to add an arc, its head node must exist,
  568  but there is no check for existence of the tail. The lookup method will mask the existence
  569  of such an arc. However, if the tail is added later, then the arc becomes visible. Similarly,
  570  concurrent updates may remove a vertex that is the head of an arc. However, the lookup
  571  method will mask such an arc.

  601  6 Comparison with previous work

  602  Eventual consistency has been an active topic of research in highly-available, large-scale
  603  asynchronous systems [17, 20]. Contrary to much previous work [3, for instance], we take a
  604  formal approach grounded in the theory of commutativity and semilattices.
  605  The state-based approach was invented for register-like objects, where the only update
  606  operation is assignment. It is in wide use in file systems such as NFS, AFS or Coda, and
  607  in key-value stores such as Dynamo [3] and Riak. Op-based approaches are used when the
  608  cost of transferring state is too high, e.g., databases, and when operation semantics are
  609  important, e.g., cooperative systems such as Bayou [13] or IceCube [15].
  610  Although the CRDT concept was identified only recently, related designs have been
  611  published before. Johnson and Thomas invented the LWW-Register [9]. They propose
  612  a database of registers that can be created, updated and deleted, using the last-writerwins (LWW) rule to arbitrate between concurrent changes. LWW ensures a total order of
  613  operations, at the cost of losing concurrent updates.
  614  Concurrent editing uses the related concept of Operational Transformation (OT), due
  615  to Ellis and Gibbs [7]. To ensure responsiveness, a local operation executes immediately.
  616  Operations are not designed to commute; however, a replica receiving an update transforms
  617  it against previously-executed concurrent updates to achieve a similar result. OT algorithms
  618  for a decentralised architecture have been proposed; Oster et al. show that most of them are
  619  incorrect [12]. We believe that designing for commutativity from the start is cleaner and
  620  simpler.
  621  The foundations of CvRDTs were introduced by Baquero and Moura [1]. We extend their
  622  work with CmRDTs and with a number of new results. The CRDT concept was invented by
  623  Shapiro and Preguiça on their work on Treedoc, a Sequence CRDT for concurrent editing
  624  [14]. Logoot is another Sequence CRDT that supports an undo mechanism based on a
  625  CRDT Counter [21].
  626  Roh et al. [16] independently developed the related concept of Replicated Abstract Data
  627  Type. They generalise LWW to a partial order of updates, which they leverage to build
  628  several LWW-style classes.
  629  Burckhardt and Leijen propose the Concurrent Revisions programming model for shared
  630  abstract data types, in which a forked revision runs in isolation until it joins again. Join is
  631  based on a three-way merge function [2]. They show that simple sequential merge functions
  632  exist for ADTs built upon Abelian groups. We have also demonstrated the relation between
  633  CRDTs and sequential consistency in a similar, but more loosely-coupled, replication model.
  634  Ducourthial et al. study algebraic structures with specific properties in order to solve
  635  self-stabilisation problems [6]. They propose the so-called r-operator for "silent" tasks [4].
  636  Strong convergence can be seen as as a silent task, given a limited number of disturbing updates. However, there are differences between the two approaches. Whereas a self-stabilising
  637  system must tolerate arbitrary memory corruption, a shared mutable object should change
  638  state durably only by executing update operations. Furthermore, whereas CvRDT states
  639  constitute a monotonic semi-lattice, the r-operator requries a total order.

  642  7 Conclusion

  643  We presented the concept of a CRDT, a replicated data type for which some simple
  644  mathematical properties guarantee eventual consistency. In the state-based style, the successive states of an object should form a monotonic semilattice, with merge computing a
  645  least upper bound. In the op-based style, concurrent operations should commute. Assuming only that the communication subsystem ensures eventual delivery (in causal order for
  646  op-based objects), CRDTs are guaranteed to converge towards a common, correct state,
  647  without requiring any synchronisation.
  648  We presented some simple CRDT examples, such as sets, and detailed how to create a
  649  directed Graph CRDT, which might be used in a large-scale web search engine. Our data
  650  types have a clean and deterministic semantics in the presence of concurrent updates.
  651  Eventual consistency is a critical technique in many large-scale distributed systems, including delay-tolerant networks, sensor networks, peer-to-peer networks, collaborative computing, cloud computing, and so on. However, work on eventual consistency was mostly
  652  ad-hoc so far. Although some of our CRDTs were known before in the literature or in the
  653  folklore, this is the first work to engage in a systematic study. We believe this is required if
  654  eventual consistency is to gain a solid theoretical and practical foundation.
  655  Future work is both theoretical and practical. On the theory side, this will include understanding the class of computations that can be accomplished by CRDTs, the complexity
  656  classes of CRDTs, the classes of invariants that can be supported by a CRDT, the relations
  657  between CRDTs and concepts such as self-stabilisation and aggregation, and so on. On the
  658  practical side, we plan to implement the data types specified herein as a library, to use them
  659  in practical applications, and to evaluate their performance analytically and experimentally.
  660  Another direction is to support support infrequent, non-critical synchronous operations,
  661  such as committing a state or performing a global reset. We will also look into stronger
  662  global invariants, possibly using probabilistic or heuristic techniques.
