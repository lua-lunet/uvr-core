   42  1 The Problem
   43  1.1 The Island of Paxos
   44  Early in this millennium, the Aegean island of Paxos was a thriving mercantile center.1 Wealth led to political sophistication, and the Paxons replaced their ancient
   45  theocracy with a parliamentary form of government. But trade came before civic
   46  duty, and no one in Paxos was willingto devote his life to Parliament. The Paxon
   47  Parliament had to function even though legislators continually wandered in and out
   48  of the parliamentary Chamber.
   49  The problem of governing with a part-time parliament bears a remarkable correspondence to the problem faced by today's fault-tolerant distributed systems, where
   50  legislators correspond to processes and leaving the Chamber corresponds to failing.
   51  The Paxons' solution may therefore be of some interest to computer scientists. I
   52  present here a short history of the Paxos Parliament's protocol, followed by an even
   53  shorter discussion of its relevance for distributed systems.
   54  Paxon civilization was destroyed by a foreign invasion, and archeologists have just
   55  recently begun to unearth its history. Our knowledge of the Paxon Parliament is
   56  therefore fragmentary. Although the basic protocols are known, we are ignorant of
   57  many details. Where such details are of interest, I will take the liberty of speculating
   58  on what the Paxons might have done.
   59  1.2 Requirements
   60  Parliament's primary task was to determine the law of the land, which was defined
   61  by the sequence of decrees it passed. A modern parliament will employ a secretary
   62  to record its actions, but no one in Paxos was willingto remain in the Chamber
   63  throughout the session to act as secretary. Instead, each Paxon legislator maintained a ledger in which he recorded the numbered sequence of decrees that were
   64  passed. For example, legislator Λ˘ινχ∂'s ledger had the entry

   65  155: The olive tax is 3 drachmas per ton

   66  if she believed that the 155th decree passed by Parliament set the tax on olives to 3
   67  drachmas per ton. Ledgers were written with indelible ink, and their entries could
   68  not be changed.
   69  The first requirement of the parliamentary protocol was the consistency of ledgers,
   70  meaning that no two ledgers could contain contradictory information. If legislator
   71  Φισ∂ρ had the entry

   72  132: Lamps must use only olive oil

   74  132. However, another legislator might have no entry in his ledger for decree 132 if
   75  he hadn't yet learned that the decree had been passed.
   76  Consistency of ledgers was not sufficient, since it could be trivially fulfilled by
   77  leaving all ledgers blank. Some requirement was needed to guarantee that decrees

  103  1.3 Assumptions
  104  The requirements of the parliamentary protocol could be achieved only by providing
  105  the legislators with the necessary resources. Each legislator received a sturdy ledger
  106  in which to record the decrees, a pen, and a supply of indelible ink. Legislators might
  107  forget what they had been doing if they left the Chamber,3 so they would write
  108  notes in the back of the ledgers to remind themselves of important parliamentary
  109  tasks. An entry in the list of decrees was never changed, but notes could be crossed
  110  out. Achieving the progress condition required that legislators be able to measure
  111  the passage of time, so they were given simple hourglass timers.
  112  Legislators carried their ledgers at all times, and could always read the list of
  113  decrees and any note that had not been crossed out. The ledgers were made of the
  114  finest parchment and were used for only the most important notes. A legislator
  115  would write other notes on a slip of paper, which he might (or might not) lose if
  116  he left the Chamber.
  117  The acoustics of the Chamber were poor, makingoratory impossible. Legislators
  118  could communicate only by messenger, and were provided with funds to hire as

  141  2 The Single-Decree Synod
  142  The Paxon Parliament evolved from an earlier ceremonial Synod of priests that
  143  was convened every 19 years to choose a single, symbolic decree. For centuries, the
  144  Synod had chosen the decree by a conventional procedure that required all priests
  145  to be present. But as commerce flourished, priests began wandering in and out of
  146  the Chamber while the Synod was in progress. Finally, the old protocol failed, and
  147  a Synod ended with no decree chosen. To prevent a repetition of this theological
  148  disaster, Paxon religious leaders asked mathematicians to formulate a protocol for
  149  choosingthe Synod's decree. The protocol's requirements and assumptions were essentially the same as those of the later Parliament except that instead of containing
  150  a sequence of decrees, a ledger would have at most one decree. The resulting Synod
  151  protocol is described here; the Parliamentary protocol is described in Section 3.
  152  Mathematicians derived the Synod protocol in a series of steps. First, they proved
  153  results showingthat a protocol satisfyingcertain constraints would guarantee consistency and allow progress. A preliminary protocol was then derived directly from
  154  these constraints. A restricted version of the preliminary protocol provided the
  155  basic protocol that guaranteed consistency, but not progress. The complete Synod
  156  protocol, satisfyingthe consistency and progress requirements, was obtained by
  157  restrictingthe basic protocol.4
  158  The mathematical results are described in Section 2.1, and the protocols are
  159  described informally in Sections 2.2–2.4. A more formal description and correctness
  160  proof of the basic protocol appears in the appendix.

  161  4The complete history of the Synod protocol's discovery is not known. Like modern computer
  162  scientists, Paxon mathematicians would describe elegant, logical derivations that bore no resemblance to how the algorithms were actually derived. However, it is known that the mathematical
  163  results (Theorems 1 and 2 of Section 2.1) really did precede the protocol. They were discovered
  164  when mathematicians, in response to the request for a protocol, were attempting to prove that a
  165  satisfactory protocol was impossible.

  381  2.2 The Preliminary Protocol
  382  The Paxons derived the preliminary protocol from the requirement that conditions
  383  B1(B)–B3(B) remain true, where B was the set of all ballots that had been or were
  384  beingconducted. The definition of the protocol specified how the set B changed,
  385  but the set was never explicitly calculated. The Paxons referred to B as a quantity
  386  observed only by the gods, since it might never be known to any mortal.
  387  Each ballot was initiated by a priest, who chose its number, decree, and quorum.
  388  Each priest in the quorum then decided whether or not to vote in the ballot. The
  389  rules determininghow the initiator chose a ballot's number, decree, and quorum,
  390  and how a priest decided whether or not to vote in a ballot were derived directly
  391  from the need to maintain B1(B)–B3(B).
  392  To maintain B1, each ballot had to receive a unique number. By remembering
  393  (with notes in his ledger) what ballots he had previously initiated, a priest could
  394  easily avoid initiatingtwo different ballots with the same number. To keep different
  395  priests from initiatingballots with the same number, the set of possible ballot
  396  numbers was partitioned amongthe priests. While it is not known how this was
  397  done, an obvious method would have been to let a ballot number be a pair consisting

  663  When Parliament was established, a protocol to satisfy its consistency and progress

  667  3.1 The Protocol
  668  Instead of passingjust one decree, the Paxon Parliament had to pass a series of
  669  numbered decrees. As in the Synod protocol, a president was elected. Anyone who
  670  wanted a decree passed would inform the president, who would assign a number
  671  to the decree and attempt to pass it. Logically, the parliamentary protocol used a
  672  separate instance of the complete Synod protocol for each decree number. However,
  673  a single president was selected for all these instances, and he performed the first
  674  two steps of the protocol just once.
  675  The key to derivingthe parliamentary protocol is the observation that, in the
  676  Synod protocol, the president does not choose the decree or the quorum until step 3.
  677  A newly elected president p can send to some set of legislators a single message that
  678  serves as the NextBallot(b) message for all instances of the Synod protocol. (There
  679  are an infinite number of instances—one for each decree number.) A legislator q
  680  can reply with a single message that serves as the LastVote messages for step 2 of
  681  all instances of the Synod protocol. This message contains only a finite amount of
  682  information, since q can have voted in only a finite number of instances.
  683  When the new president has received a reply from every member of a majority
  684  set, he is ready to perform step 3 for every instance of the Synod protocol. For
  685  some finite number of instances (decree numbers), the choice of decree in step 3 will
  686  be determined by B3. The president immediately performs step 3 for each of those
  687  instances to try passingthese decrees. Then, whenever he receives a request to pass
  688  a decree, he chooses the lowest-numbered decree that he is still free to choose, and
  689  he performs step 3 for that decree number (instance of the Synod protocol) to try
  690  to pass the decree.

  731  of the correspondinginstance of the Synod protocol. The decree was said to be
  732  passed when it was written for the first time in a ledger. Before a president could
  733  propose any new decrees, he had to learn from all the members of a majority set

  759  3.3 Further Developments
  760  Governingthe island turned out to be a more complex task than the Paxons realized.
  761  A number of problems arose whose solutions required changes to the protocol. The
  762  most important of these changes are described below.
  763  3.3.1 Picking a President The president of parliament was originally chosen
  764  by the method that had been used in the Synod, which was based purely on the
  765  alphabetical orderingof names. Thus, when legislator Ωκι returned from a sixmonth vacation, he was immediately made president—even though he had no idea
  766  what had happened in his absence. Parliamentary activity came to a halt while
  767  Ωκι, who was a slow writer, laboriously copied six months worth of decrees to bring
  768  his ledger up to date.
  769  This incident led to a debate about the best way to choose a president. Some
  770  Paxons urged that once a legislator became president, he should remain president
  771  until he left the Chamber. An influential group of citizens wanted the richest
  772  legislator in the Chamber to be president, since he could afford to hire more scribes
  773  and other servants to help him with the presidential duties. They argued that once
  774  a rich legislator had brought his ledger up to date, there was no reason for him
  775  not to assume the presidency. Others, however, argued that the most upstanding
  776  citizen should be made president, regardless of wealth. Upstanding probably meant
  777  less likely to be dishonest, although no Paxon would publicly admit the possibility

  933  3.3.5 Dishonest Legislators and Honest Mistakes Despite official assertions to
  934  the contrary, there must have been a few dishonest legislators in the history of
  935  Paxos. When caught, they were probably exiled. By sending contradictory messages, a malicious legislator could cause different legislators' ledgers to be inconsistent. Inconsistency could also result from a lapse of memory by an honest legislator
  936  or messenger.
  937  When inconsistencies were recognized, they could easily be corrected by passing
  938  decrees. For example, disagreement about the current olive tax could be eliminated
  939  by passinga new decree declaringthe tax to have a certain value. The difficult
  940  problem lay in correctinginconsistent ledgers even if no one was aware of the
  941  inconsistency.
  942  The existence of dishonesty or mistakes by legislators can be inferred from the
  943  redundant decrees that began appearing in ledgers several years after the founding
  944  of Parliament. For example, the decree

  945  2605: The olive tax is 9 drachmas per ton

  946  was passed even though decree 2155 had already set the olive tax to 9 drachmas
  947  per ton, and no interveningdecree had changed it. Parliament apparently cycled
  948  through its laws every six months so that even if legislators' ledgers were initially
  949  inconsistent, all legislators would agree on the current law of the land within six
  950  months. It is believed that by the use of these redundant decrees, the Paxons
  951  made their Parliament self-stabilizing. (Self-stabilizing is a modern term due to
  952  Dijkstra [Dijkstra 1974].)
  953  It is not clear precisely what self-stabilization meant in a Parliament with legislators comingand goingat will. The Paxons would not have been satisfied with
  954  a definition that required all legislators to be in the Chamber at one time before
  955  consistency could be guaranteed. However, achieving consistency required that if
  956  one legislator had an entry in his ledger for a certain decree number and another
  957  did not, then the second legislator would eventually fill in that entry.
  958  Unfortunately, we don't know exactly what sort of self-stabilization property
  959  the Paxon Parliament possessed or how it was achieved. Paxon mathematicians
  960  undoubtedly addressed the problem, but their work has not yet been found. I hope
  961  that future archaeological expeditions to Paxos will give high priority to the search
  962  for manuscripts on self-stabilization.

  965  3.3.6 Choosing New Legislators At first, membership in Parliament was hereditary, passingfrom parent to child. When the elder statesman Παρνας retired,
  966  he gave his ledger to his son, who carried on without interruption. It made no
  967  difference to other legislators which Παρνας they communicated with.
  968  As old families emigrated and new ones immigrated, this system had to change.
  969  The Paxons decided to add and remove members of Parliament by decree. This
  970  posed a circularity problem: membership in Parliament was determined by which
  971  decrees were passed, but passinga decree required knowingwhat constituted a
  972  majority set, which in turn depended upon who was a member of Parliament. The
  973  circularity was broken by lettingthe membership of Parliament used in passing
  974  decree n be specified by the law as of decree n − 3. A president could not try to
  975  pass decree 3255 until he knew all decrees through decree 3252. In practice, after
  976  passingthe decree
  977  3252: Στρωνγ is now a legislator
  978  the president would immediately pass the "olive-day" decree as decrees 3253 and
  979  3254.
  980  Changing the composition of Parliament in this way was dangerous and had to
  981  be done with care. The consistency and progress conditions would always hold.
  982  However, the progress condition guaranteed progress only if a majority set was in
  983  the Chamber; it did not guarantee that a majority set would ever be there. In fact,
  984  the mechanism for choosinglegislators led to the downfall of the Parliamentary
  985  system in Paxos. Because of a scribe's error, a decree that was supposed to honor
  986  sailors who had drowned in a shipwreck instead declared them to be the only
  987  members of Parliament. Its passage prevented any new decrees from being passed—
  988  includingthe decrees proposed to correct the mistake. Government in Paxos came
  989  to a halt. A general named Λαµπσων took advantage of the confusion to stage
  990  a coup, establishinga military dictatorship that ended centuries of progressive
  991  government. Paxos grew weak under a series of corrupt dictators, and was unable
  992  to repel an invasion from the east that led to the destruction of its civilization.

  995  Although Paxos's Parliament was destroyed many centuries ago, its protocol is still

 1001  currently assigned to a name, and a fast read, which is faster but might not reflect

 1021  This method of implementinga distributed database is an instance of the state
 1022  machine approach, first proposed in [Lamport 1978]. In this approach, one first
 1023  defines a state machine, which consists of a set of states, a set of commands, a set
 1024  of responses, and a function that assigns a response/state pair (a pair consisting of
 1025  a response and a state) to each command/state pair. Intuitively, a state machine
 1026  executes a command by producinga response and changingits state; the command
 1027  and the machine's current state determine its response and its new state. For the
 1028  distributed database, a state-machine state is just a database state. The statemachine commands and the function specifyingthe response and new state are
 1029  described in Figure 2.
 1030  In the state-machine approach, a system is implemented with a network of server
 1031  processes. The servers transform client requests into state machine commands,
 1032  execute the commands, and transform the state-machine responses into replies to
 1033  clients. A general algorithm ensures that all servers obtain the same sequence of
 1034  commands, thereby ensuringthat they all produce the same sequence of responses
 1035  and state changes—assuming they all start from the same initial state. In the
 1036  database example, a client request to perform a slow read or to change a value is
 1037  transformed into a state-machine read or update command. That command is
 1038  executed, and the state-machine response is transformed into a reply to the client,
 1039  which is sent to him by the server who received his request. Since all servers perform
 1040  the same sequence of state-machine commands, they all maintain consistent versions
 1041  of the database. However, at any time, some servers may have earlier versions than
 1042  others because a state-machine command need not be executed at the same time
 1043  by all servers. A server uses his current version of the state to reply to a fast read
 1044  request, without executinga state-machine command.
 1045  The functionality of the system is expressed by the state machine, which is just
 1046  a function from command/state pairs to response/state pairs. Problems of synchronization and fault-tolerance are handled by the general algorithm with which
 1047  servers obtain the sequence of commands. When designing a new system, only
 1048  the state machine is new. The servers obtain the state-machine commands by a
 1049  standard distributed algorithm that has already been proved correct. Functions are
 1050  much easier to design, and to get right, than distributed algorithms.
 1051  The first algorithm for implementing an arbitrary state machine appeared in
 1052  [Lamport 1978]. Later, algorithms were devised to tolerate up to any fixed number
 1053  f of arbitrary failures [Lamport 1984]. These algorithms guarantee that, if fewer

 1058  inconsistent copies of the state machine. Moreover, the inability of two servers to
 1059  communicate with each other is equivalent to the failure of one of them. For a

 1064  state machine. The legislators' law book corresponds to the machine state, and
 1065  passinga decree corresponds to executinga state-machine command. The resulting
 1066  algorithm is less robust and less expensive than the earlier algorithms. It does not
 1067  tolerate arbitrary, malicious failures, nor does it guarantee bounded-time response.
 1068  However, consistency is maintained despite the (benign) failure of any number of
 1069  processes and communication paths. The Paxon algorithm is suitable for systems
 1070  with modest reliability requirements that do not justify the expense of an extremely
 1071  fault-tolerant, real-time implementation.

 1084  4.2 Commit Protocols
 1085  The Paxon Synod protocol is similar to standard three-phase commit protocols
 1086  [Bernstein et al. 1987; Skeen 1982]. A Paxon ballot and a three-phase commit
 1087  protocol both involve the exchange of five messages between a coordinator (the
 1088  president) and the other quorum members (legislators). A commit protocol chooses
 1089  one of two values—commit or abort—while the Synod protocol chooses an arbitrary
 1090  decree. To convert a commit protocol to a Synod protocol, one sends the decree
 1091  in the initial round of messages. A commit decision means that this decree was
 1092  passed, and an abort decision means that the "olive-day" decree was passed.
 1093  The Synod protocol differs from a converted commit protocol because the decree
 1094  is not sent until the second phase. This allows the correspondingparliamentary
 1095  protocol to execute the first phase just once for all decrees, so the exchange of only
 1096  three messages is needed to pass each individual decree.

 1231  This algorithm is an abstract description of the real protocol performed by Paxon
 1232  priests. Do the algorithm's actions accurately model the actions of the real priests?
 1233  There were three kinds of actions that a priest could perform "atomically": receivinga message, writinga note or ledger entry, and sendinga message. Each of these
 1234  is represented by a single action of the algorithm, except that Receive actions both
 1235  receive a message and set a variable. We can pretend that the receipt of a message
 1236  occurred when a priest acted upon the message; if he left the Chamber before actingupon it, then we can pretend that the message was never received. Since this
 1237  pretense does not affect the consistency condition, we can infer the consistency of
 1238  the basic Synod protocol from the consistency of the algorithm.
 1239  A2 Proof of Consistency
 1240  To prove the consistency condition, it is necessary to show that whenever outcome[p]
 1241  and outcome[q] are both different from blank, they are equal. A rigorous correctness proof requires a complete description of the algorithm. The description given
 1242  above is almost complete. Missingis a variable M whose value is the multiset of
 1243  all messages in transit.15 Each Send action adds a message to this multiset and
 1244  each Receive action removes one. Also needed are actions to represent the loss
 1245  and duplication of messages, as well as a Forget action that represents a priest
 1246  losinghis slip of paper.
