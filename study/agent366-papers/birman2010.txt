    6  Abstract
    7  In designing and building distributed systems, it is common engineering practice to separate steady-state ("normal") operation from abnormal events such as recovery from failure. This way the normal case can be optimized
    8  extensively while recovery can be amortized. However, integrating the recovery procedure with the steady-state
    9  protocol is often far from obvious, and can present subtle difficulties. This issue comes to the forefront in modern data centers, where applications are often implemented as elastic sets of replicas that must reconfigure while
   10  continuing to provide service, and where it may be necessary to install new versions of active services as bugs
   11  are fixed or new functionality is introduced. Our paper explores this topic in the context of a dynamic reconfiguration model of our own design that unifies two widely popular prior approaches to the problem: virtual
   12  synchrony, a model and associated protocols for reliable group communication, and state machine replication
   13  (in particular, Paxos), a model and protocol for replicating some form of deterministic functionality specified as
   14  an event-driven state machine.

   18  1 Introduction

   19  Our work deals with a style of distributed services that uses elastic sets of replicas to achieve availability, scalability,
   20  and long term durability. These replica sets can vary widely over time: expanding as the service confronts load
   21  surges or replaces failed server instances, and contracting as load drops or members crash. Thus, the contemporary
   22  distributed systems developer, or service developer, is unavoidably faced with issues of dynamic service replication.
   23  Service replication and reconfiguration confront the developer with a series of tough choices. One can evade
   24  these choices by abandoning consistency, but there are many kinds of services for which consistency guarantees
   25  are important and must not be violated simply because the service replica set is elastic. For services of this type,
   26  reconfiguration remains a murky black art, error prone and associated with complex, subtle bugs. Moreover, among
   27  the correct, rigorously analyzed solutions there are all sorts of hidden tradeoffs, representing deliberate compromises between performance and complexity during steady-state operation of the service (when reconfiguration isn't
   28  happening) and the complexity of the reconfiguration event itself, which may also entail some period of service
   29  unavailability.
   30  Our treatment of this problem is tutorial in style, but aimed at a sophisticated audience. We suspect that
   31  any developer confronting this topic will have had a considerable amount of experience with distributed systems.
   32  Accordingly, we target a reader who knows how one creates distributed services, has some familiarity with the
   33  classical papers on asynchronous consensus and communication protocols and impossibility results such as the
   34  FLP theorem, and who may even have worked with the virtual synchrony [8] or the state machine replication
   35  methodologies [19, 32, 20]. Our premise is that even knowledgeable readers be surprised to learn of some of
   36  the pitfalls, anomalies and tradeoffs that the prevailing methodologies tacitly accept. Our work unifies what had
   37  previously been different models using a single overarching methodology and formalism which we name Dynamic
   38  Service Replication (DSR).
   39  Our goal will be to design services that can be reconfigured without disrupting correctness, up to some maximum tolerable rate of service membership changes, beyond which it becomes unavailable. We assume that reconfiguration is triggered by a reconfiguration command, issued by the system management layer, and either removing
   40  some members from a service, or adding some, or perhaps doing both at once. Reconfiguration could also change
   41  application parameters, or even be used to upgrade to a new version of an application or a protocol while keeping
   42  the service as available as possible.
   43  Building a reliable service using the DSR approach entails three fundamental steps.

   44  Safety. The first is to provide a service-oriented safety definition. A good specification must abstract away from
   45  implementation details, and describe the service using the methods clients can access, and expressing properties
   46  in terms of guarantees that those methods will offer. Two example services are interwoven throughout the body
   47  of our paper. The first example is a Reliable Multicast service, for which we give a service-oriented description.
   48  Our definition exposes a Get() API call that provide clients with a full, consistent history of messages that clients
   49  Add(). This definition lets us focus on the matter of preserving service state consistently while transitioning
   50  through configuration changes. Conversely, it is stripped of internal details such as clients joining and departing
   51  the service, delivery duplication, etc.; those can be added as various filters and add-ons, as discussed below. We
   52  use the well known State Machine Replication (SMR) problem [19, 32] as our second primary example. For
   53  completeness, in Section 8 we also briefly flesh out an Atomic Read/Write Storage service.

   54  Liveness. The second ingredient of our treatment is an appropriate failure model. In the distributed computing
   55  arena, reliability is often expressed as the requirement to tolerate a threshold t failures out of an initial system of n
   56  processes. This classical fault model ignores the ability of a dynamic system to out-live such an initial setting via
   57  administrative decrees, e.g., to deploy new processes, or to remove faulty ones from consideration. For example,
   58  an initial system configuration of four processes, {A, B, C, D}, may tolerate a single failure. However, through

   60  an administrative decree to reconfigure, say removing A after a failure, the remaining set {B, C, D} can tolerate
   61  an additional single failure. But as this example suggests, although we have increased the overall fault tolerance
   62  through dynamism, we can't simply say that we have a system of four processes, any two of which may crash at
   63  any time. We need a more subtle condition that gives the system "sufficient time" to reconfigure. In our example,
   64  two crashes can be tolerated over the total period the system is running, but during the early part, before the
   65  system had been reconfigured, only one of {A, B, C, D} may fail. This notion of a dynamically-defined majority
   66  appeared implicitly in various prior works, including [30, 31, 37, 24, 20, 26], but the conditions always involved
   67  solution-specific details. Here, we adopt the principles of the DynaStore liveness model [1], which gives an opaque
   68  Reconfig() handle for (administrative) clients. We then enhance this model to suit our more general framework.

   69  Reconfiguration recipe. The third component is an algorithmic foundation for solution. Briefly, a DSR epochby-epoch reconfiguration starts with a consensus decision by the current configuration (say, C1) on a new configuration (C2). The reconfiguration procedure proceeds with a transfer of control from C1 to C2, which entails (a) a
   70  decision to suspend C1; (b) a snapshot of completed and potentially-completed operations in C1 ; (c) a state transfer
   71  of the snapshot to C2. The reconfiguration completes with a decision to enable C2 for processing new operations,
   72  with the initial state of C2 determined by step b.
   73  In our methodology, the developer deals with one epoch at a time. An epoch terminates with a configuration
   74  change decision. The next configuration is uniquely determined in the current epoch, and transition to it is irreversible. When an epoch ends, a new epoch starts a new incarnation of the same algorithm (albeit with a non-empty
   75  initial service state), whose participants and messages do not mix with the current epoch. If an epoch starts in a
   76  configuration that already includes some failed nodes, it might make progress in that state, or it might initiate a
   77  further round of reconfiguration to remove those nodes; indeed, it can initiate reconfiguration as its first action.
   78  The new configuration operates completely separately from the current one; the messages in it are not confused
   79  with messages in the current configuration; and it can consist of an entirely new set of machine. Any number of
   80  reconfigurations can be chained in this manner.

   81  Solutions. While the general framework – decide, suspend, state transfer, resume – may appear obvious, there
   82  are numerous design choices and potential pitfalls when realizing any specific service. Underlying any choice of
   83  reconfiguration solution are inherent tradeoffs. One wants to maximize availability and performance, but without
   84  violating safety, and it isn't easy to accomplish all of these simultaneously. This does not mean that protocols need
   85  to be overly cumbersome. To the contrary, in six Figures, 4-10, we give succinct pseudo-code solutions for six
   86  service variants, each frame containing a entire solution including precise safety and liveness definitions.
   87  We'll see that we can trade steady-state simplicity with continuous availability: A fault-recovery approach
   88  utilizes servers in steady-state in an uncomplicated manner, disregarding the possibility of failure. It can be highly
   89  optimized, but requires reconfiguration to unblock the service in case of a failure. Figure 4 illustrates this methodology within the context of Reliable Multicast and Figure 8 does so for SMR. The alternative is a fault-masking
   90  methodology, which crafts steady-state protocols with built-in redundancy for high availability; it adds reconfiguration functionality for even greater agility in a long-lived system. Figures 5, 6, 9 and 10 demonstrate fault-masking
   91  for multicast, SMR and read/write storage, respectively.
   92  Reconfiguration itself presents another interesting design tradeoff. The reconfiguration procedure entails forming two consensus decisions – one on the next configuration and another on the closing state of the current. These
   93  may be obtained either among the group of servers themselves, or using a separate consensus engine. Even the latter case requires careful integration with the steady-state protocol, and we give the recipes for doing so in Figures 4
   94  and 8. Fully distributed reconfiguration protocols are detailed in Figures 6, 10 and 9.
   95  In general, any new members will need to catch up: for this we use the term state transfer; it entails packaging
   96  the state of the service of the service into some sort of external representation, copying that data to the new
   97  member(s), and them loading the state before starting to process new requests. The benefit of forming agreement

   99  on a closing state is explained below in Section 6, using a novel formulation of an old idea, virtual synchrony.
  100  Without agreement, we'll see that a service might exhibit various forms of unexpected behavior. For example, one
  101  standard approach leads to services that are correct, but in which unfinished operations can linger in a concealed
  102  form, suddenly becoming complete "retroactively" arbitrarily far in the future, as discussed in Section 9. An
  103  alternative approach to this problem was explored in systems like RAMBO [24] which seek to offer continuous
  104  availability, to do so they must keep both the old and the new configurations active for a potentially extended
  105  period until the new one can take over entirely. Our methodology explains such behaviors, clarifies the associated
  106  design choices, and offers simple tradeoffs that let the developer select the properties needed, and to understand
  107  the associated costs and implementation issues.
  108  We also explore a number of misconceptions regarding reconfigurable SMR. For example, when using the
  109  reconfigurable Paxos protocol, developers find themselves forced to choose between a simple solution with poor
  110  performance and a far more complex one that runs at higher speed (corresponding to the choice of value for the
  111  Paxos concurrency window parameter). However, a higher concurrency window may result in undesirable behavior
  112  where the sequence of state machine commands contains a mix of decisions out of their intended order. In fact,
  113  our paper grew out of a project to create a new "Virtually Synchronous Paxos" protocol, and our dynamically
  114  reconfigurable version of state machine replication in Section 7.2 achieves this objective (in particular, Figure 9
  115  can be recognized as a virtually synchronous version of the Paxos protocol).
  116  More pitfalls and anomalies of existing approaches are discussed in Section 9. This section contrasts our DSR
  117  method with respect to the three most relevant methodologies, namely, implementations of virtually synchronous
  118  protocols, implementations of the Paxos protocol for state machine replication, and dynamic atomic storage protocols.
  119  Finally, we briefly sketch a correctness argument for a sample of our solutions in the Appendix.

  120  Contribution. While some methodologies lead the developer to some single best solution, that won't be the case
  121  here; not only will the solutions we develop be incomparable in some ways, they even include application-specific
  122  considerations: the best protocols for implementing a reconfigurable reliable multicast turn out not to be directly
  123  mappable to state machine replication solution, and this illustrates just one of many such examples. Thus, readers
  124  of this paper will draw different conclusions based on the utility they individually assign to the various tradeoffs
  125  involved. Our contribution isn't some single answer, but rather a more principled treatment of the question. The
  126  methodology we offer here offers confidence in correctness, and for any given set of application-specific goals,
  127  enables steady-state performance similar to the best known hand-crafted solutions to the same problems. Also, our
  128  solution assumes less than is assumed when creating state machine solutions, and for this reason may be applicable
  129  to problems for which state machine replication is inappropriate.
  130  In summary, our paper offers an overarching and unified reconfiguration framework, which reveals relationships between a number of prior works that led to correct and yet seemingly incomparable reconfigurable solutions
  131  in this space. Doing so helps the developer understand reconfiguration against a broad spectrum of choices, to
  132  understand the implications of those choices, and also makes it possible to see protocols that might previously
  133  have been portrayed as competing options as different realizations of a single overall goal.

  134  2 Liveness Model

  135  Our aim is to provide services in asynchronous systems whose set of servers is changed through explicit reconfigurations. We assume an additional set, potentially overlapping, of clients. We do not assume any bounds on
  136  message latencies or message processing times (i.e., the execution environment is asynchronous), and messages
  137  may get lost, re-ordered, or duplicated on the network. However, we assume that a message that is delivered was
  138  previously sent by some live member and that correct members can eventually communicate any message.

  140  In order to capture a formal execution model with changing memberships, we borrow concepts which express explicit dynamism from [1], but modify the treatment for weaker requirements. We proceed with a formal
  141  definition, and follow with examples.
  142  A fixed system membership consists of some set of servers and liveness conditions. As usual, our goal is to
  143  build systems that are guaranteed to make progress as long as the conditions on the servers hold. If too many
  144  servers fail (violating the conditions for liveness), safety will not be impaired, but the system might need to stop
  145  responding to client requests until reconfiguration occurs. We further refine our liveness conditions by breaking
  146  them into two parts, one for performing read requests, and one for performing updates; by doing so, we can
  147  sometimes continue to support one kind of operation even if the other kind is temporarily unavailable. Example
  148  memberships are 'f + 1 servers, f may crash on any read, no crash tolerance on update', and 'n servers, any
  149  minority of which may fail for either read or write tolerance'.
  150  We assume an initial membership M0. Clients are provided with a membership-changing API function
  151  Reconfig(M), where M is a new membership. A call to Reconfig(M) must eventually complete, and returns an
  152  ACK response some time after its invocation. Clients might issue concurrent Reconfig(M) calls, and when this
  153  occurs, the protocol orders the reconfigurations through the sequence of ACK responses. Thus, a system might
  154  perform M0, M1, M2, . . . even though Reconfig(M2) was invoked before Reconfig(M1). We will view issues
  155  such as pre-conditions for invoking Reconfig, and any access restrictions (e.g., to designated administrative users)
  156  on using the Reconfig API as falling outside of the scope of our treament.
  157  In our model, two execution events are associated with each Reconfig call, marking its invocation and its
  158  completion. Both events change the liveness conditions, so they could be thought of as 'model-changing' events
  159  in that they transform a fixed liveness-condition into another liveness-condition. For clarity, we will continue
  160  referring to them as Reconfig invocation and response events. The first event occurs upon invocation of Reconfig.
  161  It changes the current liveness condition to incorporate the requested new membership, including its set of servers
  162  and its corresponding liveness conditions. The second event in our model marks a completion of a Reconfig call.
  163  This event signifies that the system has re-organized to switch the service to the new set of servers and transferred
  164  all necessary information to it, so that the old membership may be garbage collected.
  165  We define the startup of a membership Mk to begin with the Reconfig(Mk) invocation and end with its response event (for M0, this is defined as the point the system starts). We define the lifetime of a membership Mk to
  166  begin with the Reconfig(Mk) invocation (or with the start time of the system, for M0) and end when the succeeding
  167  reconfiguration Reconfig(Mk+1) startup is completed.

  168  Liveness: For every membership Mk in the sequence of Reconfig calls, the following holds:

  169  1. Throughout the lifetime of Mk, its read-resilience is not violated.
  170  2. There exists a future membership M`, where ` > k, such that the update-resilience of M` holds
  171  throughout the startup of Reconfig(M`).

  172  Note that it follows inductively from the definitions above that memberships Mk+1, ..., M`−1 maintain their
  173  read-resilience condition until the response event of Reconfig(M`).
  174  To illustrate the features of our liveness model, let's apply it in three simple scenarios. In the first, we have a
  175  system implemented by a single server which may be replaced as needed. Each membership consists of a singleton
  176  set. The read and update resilience conditions are identical here: Zero failures. Plugging these bounds into our
  177  liveness condition implies that, not surprisingly, a server must remain alive until a new one is installed. Moreover,
  178  the new server must be alive during its startup procedure. For example, say that initially we have M0, which
  179  contains a singleton set of servers {q0}. A client wishes to replace q0 with an upgraded server q1 by invoking
  180  Reconfig(M1), with M1 containing the set of servers {q1}. Internally, the startup procedure of Reconfig(M1)
  181  suspends q0, copies data from q0 to q1, and redirects clients to q1. Note that this startup procedure will be successful

  182  5
=== PAGE 6 ===

  183  under the assumptions of our liveness model, because both q0 and q1 remain alive while Reconfig(M1) is in
  184  progress. Once q1 stores the system state, the Reconfig(M1) procedure completes and we model this as an abstract
  185  response event. From here on, q0 may safely shut down.
  186  The second example is a service implemented by N = F +1 servers for F-tolerance, such as a primary-backup
  187  setup. Here, the read and the update thresholds are substantially different. The update-threshold is F + 1. That is,
  188  in order for the service to store updates durably, it requires participation of all servers. In case of a failure, updates
  189  become stalled, and we use the reconfiguration manager to facilitate progress. Such a service must include another
  190  system component in charge of reconfiguration, because it impossible to form a consensus decision on the next
  191  membership among the F + 1 processes alone. To reconfigure, we need both one server to be available, in order
  192  to persist the service state; and the reconfiguration manager must be available in order to initiate the Reconfig
  193  procedure.
  194  For example, say that M0 has a set of servers S0 and M1 has S1, each consisting of F + 1 servers. Upon invocation of Reconfig(M1 ), our liveness requires read availability in both S0 and S1 and that some later membership
  195  has update availability. More specifically, our model says that at most F out of each of the sets S0, S1 fail. This
  196  suffices to suspend M0 and transfer the closing state of M0 to at least one server in S1. When the closing state of
  197  M0 has been transferred to all F + 1 servers of M1 our model schedules the Reconfig(M1) response event. At
  198  that time, our liveness model changes: it drops any resilience assumption on M0. Indeed, it would be safe for all
  199  servers of S0 (that are not in S1) to shut down.
  200  To re-iterate a point made above, Reconfig calls may overlap. Hence, this scenario could develop quite differently. Say that there is a failure in S1 before we complete the state transfer to it. Hence, a client may issue
  201  Reconfig(M2) to remedy this, before Reconfig(M1) completes. In our formal model, another event occurs, marking the invocation of Reconfig(M2) and changing the liveness assumption to 'F out of each of the sets S0, S1
  202  and S2 may fail'. Now servers in S2 suspend M1 and obtain its state. Note that our read-resilience assumption
  203  implies that this is possible, despite having failures in M1 already. When all F + 1 servers in S2 obtained their
  204  state, Reconfig(M2 ) becomes completed. In this case, the completion of Reconfig(M2) also pertinently marks the
  205  completion of Reconfig(M1). Consequently, both M0 and M1 will be retired by this completion.
  206  In our last example, a membership consists of a set of N = 2F + 1 servers, F of which may crash for either
  207  read or update. Here, the read and update resilience thresholds are identical. The liveness condition is simple
  208  here: During the lifetime of a membership, at most F of its servers may crash. A membership Mk ends with the
  209  completion of Reconfig(Mk+1), when the closing state of Mk is stored on F + 1 members of Mk+1. At that time,
  210  Mk may be retired.
  211  Finally, we note that in order to reach a unique Reconfig decision, we are obviously bound by the impossibility
  212  of consensus: in order to guarantee termination for Reconfig decisions, we require an eventual leader with timely
  213  communication to a majority of the membership.

  214  3 The Dynamic Reliable Multicast Problem

  215  Let's jump in by exploring the reconfiguration question in the context of a simple but general form of reliable
  216  multicast. A reliable multicast protocol is simply a service (perhaps implemented by a library that its clients
  217  employ), which allows clients to send new multicast messages to groups of receivers, and to receive messages
  218  within groups. Multicast is a popular technology, both in the explicit form just described, and also in implicit
  219  forms, such as publish-subscribe or content-based communication infrastructures, so called enterprise message
  220  bus technologies, and many kinds of data replication and caching technologies. In three accompanying break-out
  221  boxes (Figures 1, 2, and 3) we discuss the possible mappings of our simple multicast API to more standard ones
  222  that might be used in such services.
  223  Reliable multicast is a good place to start because many distributed systems rely on some form of multicast-like
  224  mechanism at a basic level, perhaps presenting it as a multicast, or perhaps hiding it in a data or file replication

  226  How can our service model be made available through a more standard multicast package? Our multicast API
  227  is pull-based, but one could change it into a notification API by supporting an explicit group join operation. The
  228  join itself would change the group membership using a reconfiguration command, and we'll discuss these below.
  229  The Send operation maps directly to our Add() interface. Receive would then be supported as a callback from the
  230  multicast library into the application. When a process joins a multicast group for the first time, our durability rule
  231  requires it to learn the state of the group. Thus the first event that occurs upon joining would be a Get() of the
  232  sort we've included in our model: the Get(), in effect, embodies the state transfer. Subsequent receive operations,
  233  in contrast, would be modeled as Get() operations that omit messages that were previously delivered to the caller.
  234  View notification, if desired, would be implemented as an upcall that occurs when a new membership is initiated.
  235  A real implementation would also garbage collect durable multicast messages once they are processed by the
  236  group members and reflected into the group state. The needed mechanisms complicate the protocol, but have no
  237  fundamental bearing on the reconfiguration problem, hence we omit discussion of them here. See [10] for details on
  238  methods for detecting durability and using that information to drive garbage collection.

  240  mechanism. But whatever form the end-user functionality takes, the multicast communication pattern arises. If
  241  we can understand reconfiguration in a multicast protocol, we'll have taken a big step towards understanding
  242  reconfiguration as a general computing paradigm.

  243  The multicast API. Our proposed service offers two interfaces to its clients, Add() and Get(). The Add()
  244  primitive sends a new message and returns an ACK. A Get() call returns a set of messages. A message m of a
  245  completed Add() or Get() operation becomes durable. The main requirement we have of a Get() call is:

  246  Definition 1 (Multicast Durability) A Get() call returns a set of messages that contains all messages which have
  247  become durable when the call was invoked.

  248  In practice, Get() returns all durable messages of completed Add() operations and possibly also some additional messages associated with concurrently executing Add() operations. If a message m is returned by Get, it
  249  becomes durable; hence every subsequent call to Get will also return m.
  250  We don't require any particular order on operations; in particular, two concurrent calls to Get() may return
  251  disjoint sets of durable messages, and might order messages differently. We discuss this in more detail in Figure 2.

  252  Epoch-by-epoch Solution. Our approach for dynamically reconfigurable reliable multicast has two parts: A
  253  steady-state protocol for sending and delivering messages during normal, stable periods; and a reconfiguration
  254  protocol. Each of these protocols is defined relative to a single epoch, which begins when a configuration of
  255  the system becomes live and ends by running the epoch termination protocol given below. After the current epoch
  256  ends, a new epoch starts, and we can understand it as hosting a completely new incarnation of this algorithm, whose
  257  newly added messages do not mix with the previous epoch. Indeed, when changing epochs a system could modify
  258  protocol parameters or even switch to a new protocol stack entirely incompatible with the prior one, reinitialize
  259  data, agree upon new security keys (in a secured group [29]), and so forth.

  260  A Single Server Solution. It may be helpful to begin with a degenerate solution that employs just a single server,
  261  because by doing so, we create a form of reference implementation. Later when we explore distributed solutions,
  262  we can reason about their correctness by asking ourselves what properties it shares with the single server solution.

  264  Multicast protocols and systems come in many forms and flavors. Variants may offer any of a wide range of
  265  message ordering policies, such as FIFO, causal, atomic (total), etc. Several of these policies can be implemented as
  266  a filtering policy over Get(). For example, a FIFO primitive would simply add a header to each message to represent
  267  ordering information (namely, the sender's sequence number). When Get() reports a durable message history to the
  268  application, the filtering policy would suppress previously delivered messages, and then sort undelivered messages,
  269  delivering them in sequence order, delaying any messages that follow a gap. Causal ordering can be done in the
  270  same manner: here, the sender includes a vector timestamp or some other representation of the causal dependency
  271  information, and Get() delays a given message until all causally prior multicasts have already been delivered. Total
  272  ordering is more complex; we discuss the issues and how they can be solved below, when we look closely at support
  273  for dynamically reconfigurable state machine replication.
  274  Other multicast delivery primitives could implement delivery semantics beyond the model considered here. For
  275  our work here, we require durability, but many multicast systems support "early" delivery of non-durable messages,
  276  to reduce latency in situations where the application itself doesn't need durability. Failure could cause such messages
  277  to be lost, but there are applications that replicate various forms of soft or transient state and in which durability,
  278  for those updates, is unimportant, but minimizing latency is paramount (see the discussion of this topic in [11]).
  279  It is also possible to modify Get() to report non-durable messages, and the application could then manipulate its
  280  filtering policy to control which ones it will see. Doing this typically entails offering two versions of Get(), one
  281  with durability and one without. To give the application even more control, one can offer a Flush() primitive, which
  282  pauses until every multicast that was pending when it was invoked has become durable. None of these variants
  283  impose fundamentally different requirements on the underlying reconfiguration protocols, and hence for brevity, we
  284  will not explore them in detail.

  286  To implement Add(), the client in the single-server case locates the server and sends it a store request for
  287  whatever message m is being sent. The server adds m to the message history and acknowledges, and the client

  289  To implement Get(), the client contacts the server and the server sends back the entire current message history.

  336  messages by reading from a majority and storing back messages at a majority. This version can continue to

  338  need a majority of servers to remain available in order to transfer the state of the current configuration to any

  383  4.2 Reconfiguration Protocol
  384  Recall that participants initiate reconfiguration by issuing a Reconfig command. Reconfiguration entails these
  385  steps:
  386  1. The issuing client sends a wedge request to servers in the current epoch. As in our single-server solution,
  387  such a request causes a receiving server to become wedged (it may already be wedged from another Reconfig
  388  command), and to return a representation of its state.
  389  2. Since as many as F servers could be faulty, the client waits for just one response, but this response will
  390  contain all durable messages (and possibly more). These messages are used for the initial state of each
  391  server in the next epoch.
  392  3. Reconfig employs some kind of a consensus engine to form a decision both on the next membership M and
  393  on the set S of durable messages. For example, the consensus engine could be implemented by a centralized
  394  authority, which itself could be made reliable by running Paxos among replicated state machines [20].
  395  4. Servers s0 in a new membership M0 learn the reconfiguration decision either directly from the auxiliary
  396  authority, or indirectly from other members. Either way, they learn both the membership M0 of the new
  397  configuration and its initial message store S0. The initial state could also include application-specific information, or even specify an upgrade to a new version of the application or the protocols it uses. As in the
  398  single-server case, note that there are many ways to optimize state transfer so that the amount of information
  399  actually passed through the membership service could be quite small. What matters here is that a server s0
  400  in the new epoch should only process new requests after it has initialized itself appropriately.
  401  After completing state transfer, the server enables itself for handling normal client requests in the new epoch.
  402  Of course, if the server was also present in an earlier epoch, it remains wedged with respect to that epoch.
  403  5. The Reconfig command is considered completed when all servers in the new configuration have enabled
  404  themselves. Note that Reconfig may never complete. In that case, yet a further reconfiguration command
  405  would be needed before system availability is restored.
  406  The reader may wonder about reconfigurations that occur in response to a failure. In such cases, one or
  407  more servers in the current epoch will be unresponsive. However, any durable message will have been stored in
  408  all histories, and hence will be included in the initial state of all servers in the new epoch. On the other hand,
  409  consider an uncompleted operation, corresponding to a message stored in just a subset of the histories. At this
  410  stage, depending on the pattern of failures, that message could be missing from some of the surviving histories
  411  and dropped. But it could also turn out to be included in the history of the server whose state is used for the next
  412  configuration, in which case it would become durable even though the associated Add() operation may not have
  413  completed.
  414  It isn't difficult to see that such problems are unavoidable. In effect, the outcome of a Add() that was pending
  415  at the time of a reconfiguration is determined by the membership service: if the message is included into the new
  416  epoch state, the Add() should be construed as successful, and if the message is not included, the Add() has failed
  417  and should be reissued in the new epoch. The client can learn this outcome from any server in the new epoch, or
  418  from the membership service itself. We leave the details of returning these out-of-band responses to clients out of
  419  the discussion here.

  466  The same reconfiguration protocol as the fault-recovery one can be used in this case, but with a minor modification

  478  5.3 Reconfiguration Agreement Protocol
  479  We now "open" the consensus engine and flesh out a procedure that unifies forming agreement on the next epoch
  480  with state transfer. Figure 6 summarizes a multicast solution that uses N = 2F + 1 servers and contains a detailed
  481  reconfiguration decision protocol combined with state transfer. (Only the Reconfig part is changed from Figure 5,

  483  The combined reconfiguration and state transfer protocol is based on the well-known Synod algorithm [20].
  484  It is triggered by a client Reconfig command and uses a set of N = 2F + 1 servers of the current epoch. The
  485  Reconfig procedure makes use of a uniquely chosen stake (for example, an integer or some other ordered type).
  486  Clients invoking reconfiguration may repeatedly try increasingly higher stakes until a decision is reached. The
  487  protocol of a particular stake has two phases. Although we could wait for the consensus decision and then perform
  488  state transfer, it turns out that we can make efficient use of message exchanges inside the protocol to accomplish
  489  state transfer at the same time.
  490  More specifically, in Phase 1, a client performs one exchange with a majority of servers. When the client hears
  491  back from a majority, it learns:

  492  (1) Either a reconfiguration command RC, which might have been chosen. In case of multiple possibly chosen
  493  RC's, the one whose stake is highest is selected.
  494  (2) Or that no reconfiguration command was chosen.

  495  This exchange also tells the servers to ignore future proposals from any client that precedes it in the stake-order.
  496  Coupled into this (standard) Phase 1 of consensus protocol is the collect phase of our state transfer. Namely,
  497  the same exchange marks the servers wedged, which is done by obtaining a commitment from the servers not to
  498  respond to any store or collect request from processes. In their responses in Phase 1, the client collects from each
  499  server the set of messages it stores.
  500  In Phase 2, the client performs another single exchange with a majority of servers. If case (1) applies, then it
  501  tells servers to choose RC. Otherwise, in case (2), it proposes a new reconfiguration decision RC. The new RC
  502  contains the configuration that the client requested, as well as a a union of the messages it collected in the first
  503  phase.

  529  6 Coordinated State Transfer: The Virtual Synchrony Property

  530  Consider what would happen in the Reliable Multicast service if we did not include in the reconfiguration decision
  531  the set S of messages ever completed in the current configuration. Instead, imagine a protocol in which every
  532  server in the new membership independently obtains the state from a read-set of the current configuration. For
  533  those messages M whose Add or Get have completed, there will be no difference. That is, every server in the new
  534  membership will obtain M before starting the new epoch. However, messages with partially completed Add/Get
  535  may or may not be obtained by the new servers, depending on which member(s) of the current configuration they
  536  query. Such messages could later become durable, by being transferred to all servers some epoch. Let's study this
  537  case in more detail by revising our fault-recovery approach.
  538  For example, suppose that Add(a) has arrived at both servers {1,2} of some initial epoch. Say that Add(b)
  539  has reached {1} so far, and Add(c) has reached {2}. Let the reconfiguration manager establish a decision on a new
  540  epoch set {3, 4,5}. We may have server 3 suspend and pull messages {a, b} from {1}. Note that message a was
  541  completed, and b may yet complete in the future; however, we cannot distinguish between these two situations. If
  542  servers 4,5 were to do the same state transfer, it would then be possible for a client to perform a Get() in the new
  543  epoch, and return {a, b}. This is the only safe response, as Add(b) could complete meanwhile.
  544  Alternatively, servers 4,5 might pull {a, c} from {2}. At this point, because both 1 and 2 are wedged and will
  545  not acknowledge further store requests, neither b nor c may ever complete in the current configuration. However,
  546  this situation cannot be distinguished by the servers. Hence, when a client requests Get() in the new epoch, server
  547  3 must responds with {a, b}, while 4,5 must respond with {a, c}. This is fine, since the client will return the
  548  intersection {a} in response to Get().

  623  7 Dynamic State Machine Replication and Virtually Synchronous Paxos

  624  In this section, we take our Dynamic Service Replication (DSR) epoch-changing approach into the generic realm
  625  of State Machine Replication (SMR). In the state machine replication approach, replicas are implemented as deterministic state machines. The state machines start with a common initial state and apply commands in sequence
  626  order. This approach yields a protocol we refer to as Virtually Synchronous Paxos2. Paxos-users will recognize
  627  the close similarity of the protocol to the standard Paxos, yet in contrast with Reconfigurable Paxos and other
  628  reconfigurable SMR implementations, this solution achieves higher steady state performance with less complexity
  629  around the handling of reconfiguration.

  630  7.1 On Paxos Anomalies
  631  SMR operates on an infinite sequence of commands. Even though the sequence of commands is logically active
  632  all the time, in reality, systems progress in epochs: During each epoch, a fixed leader chooses a dense sequence of
  633  commands. Then, the leader is replaced, and a new epoch starts. The new leader starts where the previous leader
  634  stopped, and passes another subsequence of commands. And so on. If we envision the sequence of commands
  635  as a horizontal axis, then each leader-epoch lasts a contiguous segment along the horizontal axis. Nevertheless,
  636  abstractly, the same consensus protocol runs in the entire infinite sequence of commands, ignoring epoch and
  637  leader changes. Each instance vertically runs a succession of leaders, though each leader takes actual actions only
  638  in some instances, and none in others. The consensus-based point of view makes arguing correctness easy.
  639  Unfortunately, the seemingly obvious intuition about epochs is wrong, and may lead to undesirable effects.
  640  Consider the scenario depicted in Figure 7. We have two Leaders U and V contending for the same pair of offsets,
  641  k and k + 1. U proposes uk at k and uk+1 and k + 1, intending that uk executes before uk+1. V proposes vk
  642  and vk+1 for these offsets. Consider the case where neither Leader U nor V obtains acceptance of a majority to
  643  their proposals. Hence, a third Leader W , starts after U and V are both retired. W finds traces of the command
  644  uk+1, proposed by U, at offset k + 1, and of the command vk, proposed by V , at offset k. Paxos mandates W
  645  to re-propose vk and uk+1 at k and k + 1, respectively. If W is a stable leader, then indeed vk and uk+1 will be
  646  chosen. But this violates the intended ordering constraint of the proposers!
  647  In the above situation, Paxos permits the intended ordering of commands to be violated. As noted by the
  648  engineers who designed and built the Yahoo! ZooKeeper service [17], there are real-life situations in which leaders
  649  intend for their proposed k+ 1 command to be executed only if their proposed k command is done first. To achieve
  650  high performance many such systems are forced to pipeline their proposals, so a leader might propose command
  651  k + 1 without waiting for a decision on command k. Paxos is quite capable of choosing k + 1 but not k, and
  652  (as just noted) there are conditions under which it might choose both, but each one from a different leader. This
  653  particular case was sufficiently troublesome to convince the ZooKeeper engineers to move away from Paxos and
  654  to implement a virtually-synchronous, epoch-by-epoch type of leader election in their system.
  655  So far, we have only discussed simple leader-changes. Our scenario turns out to have even more serious
  656  ramifications for previous reconfigurable SMR solutions. Recall that vanilla Paxos allows reconfiguration of the
  657  system that implements the state machine by injecting configuration-changing commands into the sequence of
  658  state machine commands. This is a natural use of the power of consensus inherent in the implementation of
  659  SMR. It entails a concurrency barrier on the steady-state command path: Suppose we form a reconfiguration
  660  command, for example, at index y in the sequence of commands. The command determines how the system will
  661  form the agreement decision on subsequent commands, for example, at index y + 1 onward. More generally,
  662  the configuration-changing command at y may determine the consensus algorithm for commands starting at index

  687  command can be performed at a time. Batching commands can alleviate this cost: rather than one command at a
  688  time, the system might perform β at a time, for some parameter β. But this can help only to a limited degree.
  689  Our remarks may come as a surprise to readers familiar with SMR and Paxos, because many published presentations of the model and protocols omit any discussion of the complexities introduced by reconfiguration. These
  690  remarks are not entirely new, and summarize several recent works which allude to these difficulties: The causalityviolating scenario has been pointed out in [17], and Paxos reconfiguration idiosyncracies were discussed in [22].

  720  Crucially, the consensus decision contains the new configuration as well as the closing state of the current configuration. The decision value determines the configuration used in the next SMR instance and its initial object state.

  894  9 DSR in Perspective

  895  Although our development portrays reconfiguration as a relatively linear task that entails making a series of seemingly straightforward decisions, there exist many incorrect or inefficient reconfiguration solutions in the published
  896  literature. In this section, we discuss the reasoning that can lead to complexity in dynamic membership protocols,
  897  and because of that complexity, expose the solution to potential bugs. As a first step it may be helpful to express
  898  our solution as a high-level recipe. Abstractly, the DSR epoch-by-epoch reconfiguration strategy entails the steps
  899  listed below. We have numbered the steps as either A.n or B.n to indicate that steps A/B may intermix in any order:

  900  A.1 In the current configuration, form a consensus decision on the next configuration.
  901  B.1 Suspend the current configuration from serving new requests (in progress requests may continue to completion, or die).
  902  B.2 Take a snapshot of the current configuration's closing state. A legitimate (non-unique) snapshot must contain
  903  all operations ever to complete.
  904  B.3 Form agreement on a legitimate closing state. In fault-recovery solutions, this agreement must be carried by
  905  the configuration manger, or in the next configuration (after the configuration manager has designated one).
  906  In a fault-masking solution, it may be carried by either the current configuration or the next one, but we must
  907  a priori designate which one has the responsibility.
  908  A.2/B.4 Enable servers in the next configuration initialized with the consensus snapshot state formed in step B.3.

  909  With this in mind, we pause to highlight some of the more common pitfalls that have traditionally led researchers (including the authors of the current paper) to propose less than ideal solutions.

  910  9.1 Speculative-views
  911  One design decision that we recommend against arises when a service is optimized for high availability by adding
  912  a mechanism that will copy state and enable activity in a new epoch that has been proposed, but not finalized,
  913  for example during intermediate steps of the agreement protocol. Much prior work in the group communication
  914  literature falls in this category [9, 3, 4, 35, 34, 6]; a significant body of PhD dissertations and published papers on
  915  transient/ambiguous configurations, extended/weak virtual synchrony, and others, were devoted to this strategy [2,
  916  13, 18, 27]. Many other works in the group-communication area are covered in the survey by Chockler et al. [15].
  917  The group communication approach essentially performs the task of steps A.1 and B.1-B.3 inside a speculative
  918  next configuration, in an attempt to optimize and transfer-state while forming agreement on it. This works roughly
  919  as follows.

  920  A.1 In the current configuration, form a proposal on the next configuration.
  921  A.2(B.1) Suspend those members of the next configuration which persist from the current one from serving new
  922  requests.
  923  A.3(B.2-3) Among the members of the proposed next configuration, form agreement on both the transition itself,
  924  and on a legitimate snapshot of the current configuration's closing state. The snapshot decision incorporates input from those members which persist from the current configuration and from previously attempted
  925  proposed configurations.
  926  A.4 If the next configuration fails to reach consensus, go back to step A.1.

  928  A.5(B.4) Enable servers in the next configuration (their state is already initialized with the consensus snapshot
  929  state formed in step A.3.)

  930  While the resulting solutions can certainly be implemented and proved correct, they require protocol steps in
  931  which the members of a new epoch collect information from every view in which operations might have been
  932  performed, so that the view that will ultimately be used has full knowledge of the service state. Such solutions can
  933  embody substantial complexity, and this of course means that implementations will often be quite hard to debug.
  934  As an example scenario, let's revisit the scenario explored above, with an initial membership {1,2, 3}. During
  935  reconfiguration, a client could propose a new epoch configuration consisting of server-set {2,3,4}. A typical
  936  complication here was for servers {2, 3,4} to form a "transient" configuration without waiting for a majority of
  937  the previous epoch to acknowledge it. They perform a state transfer and start serving client requests immediately.
  938  Thus, servers {3,4} might respond to a Get() request with a message-set containing {b, c}. In the end, we may
  939  form consensus on a different configuration, say {1, 2,5}. If we only collect information from a majority of the
  940  previous epoch, say {1,2}, we might "forget" message c. Note that we intentionally chose a new server-set that
  941  intersects with a majority of the members of the previous epoch. We did so to illustrate that there are situations
  942  in which a set large enough to reach agreement could nonetheless not suffice if a system needs to discover every
  943  durable message.
  944  We therefore need to collect information from the transient configuration, so as not to lose message c. This
  945  is not only complex, but there is no guarantee that we will find traces of the transient epoch, unless we enforce
  946  additional constraints, the dynamic-quorum rule, as we now show.

  947  9.2 Dynamic-quorums and Cascading changes.
  948  The above scenario leads us to another common pitfall. In essence, it requires that cascading epoch changes
  949  jointly have a non-empty intersection. This guarantees that speculative epochs all intersect and become aware of
  950  one another. In the example above, server 2 is in the intersection of all attempted changes, and so we rely on
  951  information collected from it to discover the transient epoch, and collect information from its majority as well.
  952  More generally, a line of related works emanating from those with speculative configurations was devoted to
  953  the issue of handling "cascading reconfigurations", and to quorum intersection rules that guarantee to maintain a
  954  unique primary quorum through such cascading changes, e.g., [30, 37]. This constraint is unnecessary.
  955  In our method, consensus is reached by a majority in the current epoch; the next epoch need not share any
  956  servers with the previous one. More generally, our epoch-by-epoch approach emphasizes that one only needs
  957  to deal with one epoch at a time, and that cascading or chained epochs just aren't necessary (nor do they have
  958  any performance or code complexity benefit; indeed, quite the opposite). The correct algorithmic foundation we
  959  establish terminates an epoch with a configuration-changing decision. It does not matter if we have a centralized
  960  configuration manager, or run a distributed consensus protocol; both are part of the current epoch, and it is well
  961  defined who determines the next configuration. The next epoch is uniquely determined in the current epoch, and
  962  transition to it is irreversible. When an epoch ends, a new epoch starts a new incarnation of the same algorithm
  963  (albeit with a non-empty initial service state), whose participants and messages do not mix with the current epoch.
  964  The new epoch may itself decide to reconfigure, and we iterate through epoch changes again. Any number of
  965  reconfigurations can be chained in this manner, with no difficulty at all.
  966  In the example above, either we decide to transition to {2,3,4} or not. If a decision is made, it is irreversible.
  967  A future decision to switch to {1,2,5} must be taken by the new epoch, after it is enabled. It is achieved with
  968  the normal decision and state transfer mechanism. Conversely, if there is no decision on {2,3, 4}, then no client
  969  operation executes in that epoch. Later, when a different decision is reached the new epoch starts with servers
  970  {1,2,5}.

  972  9.3 Off-line Versus on-line reconfiguration
  973  We need to comment briefly on our decision to use an off-line reconfiguration strategy. A recent line of works, pioneered by the RAMBO project [24] and continued in Rambo II [14], RDS [12], DynaStore [1] and DynaDisk [33],
  974  emphasize the importance of non-blocking reconfiguration. These systems tackle dynamic Read/Write storage
  975  services, but the same design issues occur in Reliable Multicast and other, similar services.
  976  In the "RAMBO" approach, the solution is further optimized by mechanisms that prevent a client from ever
  977  encountering a suspended service. This on-line transition comes, however, at the cost of greater system complexity.
  978  RAMBO clients continue accessing the Read/Write objects by interacting both with the current and the next
  979  epoch. Every Read operation copies the object it accesses from one epoch to the next. Every Write operation also
  980  accesses a majority in the previous epoch, but no copying of data is necessary. Even if clients access objects which
  981  have already been copied to the new epoch (which would be the common case for "hot objects"), they still need to
  982  access majorities in all active configurations. The advantage is that RAMBO never blocks any client request, even
  983  momentarily, but the disadvantage is the complication of sorting through responses from two epochs and figuring
  984  out which one is more current.
  985  Even more importantly, the on-line reconfiguration approach prevents a client from ever "sealing" partiallycompleted operations which were initiated in the current epoch. Traces of those may transfer to later epochs,
  986  and become durable arbitrarily far in the future. Such behavior may not be desirable for applications engineered
  987  with a Reliable Multicast (or Read/Write) infrastructure, as we saw earlier in our discussion of Virtual Synchrony
  988  (Section 6).
  989  By comparison, the advantage of off-line reconfiguration is simplicity: clients only deal with a single system
  990  view at a time. We therefore see the off-line reconfiguration strategy above as occupying an attractive sweet-spot,
  991  despite the need to briefly block the service while switching from the old to the new configuration. On the other
  992  hand, off-line reconfiguration comes with its own form of complexity: the need to think hard about state transfer.
  993  If the service state might become large (for example, if the server is an entire file system or database) state transfer
  994  may entail moving a huge amount of data from the old to the new servers. Blocking the service while this occurs
  995  can be prohibitive, and even doing it rapidly may degrade the responsiveness of a service to an unacceptable degree.
  996  Thus our suggestion that state be transferred in advance of the reconfiguration, as much as possible, is important
  997  and may be key to using this approach in settings where service unavailability must be minimized.
  998  By pre-transferring server state, the amount of state actually moved in the last steps of the procedure can be
  999  minimized and clients prevented from seeing much of a disruption. Moreover, the actual mechanism needed for
 1000  this sort of anticipatory state transfer is fairly simple; the new server needs to fetch and load the state, and also
 1001  needs some way to compute the delta between the loaded state and the initial state assigned to it in the new epoch,
 1002  which will reflect operations that completed after it fetched that preloaded state and before the consensus decision
 1003  was made.

 1004  9.4 Paxos Anomaly
 1005  The anomalous behavior we encountered when discussing Paxos reconfiguration illustrated a different kind of
 1006  problem. Recall that vanilla Paxos is quite simple but runs in a fixed configuration. In contrast, reconfigurable
 1007  Paxos introduced not just a leader-changing procedure, but also a set of secondary issues that represent side-effects
 1008  of the way in which reconfiguration is supported. Although the per-command leader transition in Paxos entails a
 1009  virtually-synchronous reconfiguration, the actions of a new leader are made independently for each command. In
 1010  this way, leader W in our example above could not know that leader V 's proposal to command k + 1 implies that
 1011  no command was committed by its predecessor, leader U, at command k.
 1012  Reconfigurable Paxos with α > 1 indeed forms an orderly succession of configurations, but suffers from the
 1013  same leader-change anomaly above within the window of α commands.

 1015  10 Related Work

 1016  Our primary contribution in this paper is to offer a unifying framework for the problem of dynamic service reconfiguration. We are not aware of any prior work on this question. Closest was the survey of Chockler, et al. [15],
 1017  which offers a very interesting review of group communication systems in a single model. However, the main
 1018  objective in that work was to contrast the guarantees offered by each category of solution and to categorize the
 1019  weakest progress (liveness) assumptions made in each. In particular, the model used didn't formalize the reconfiguration topic as a separate mechanism, and hence doesn't get at the kinds of fine-grained choices, and their
 1020  consequences, explored here.
 1021  Our epoch-by-epoch approach draws heavily on prior work on group communication. As mentioned, a good
 1022  survey of group communication specifications appears in [15]. The approach resembles Virtual Synchrony [7, 11],
 1023  but unlike our approach Virtual Synchrony does not provide linearizability. The approach also resembles Reconfigurable Paxos (as worked out in SMART [23]), but as pointed out in Section 7 this approach allows anomalous
 1024  behavior. Approaches like Boxwood [25] and Chain Replication [36] allow reconfiguration with the help of an
 1025  external configuration service.
 1026  There has also been considerable work on reconfigurable read/write stores, such as RAMBO [24], Dynamic
 1027  Byzantine Storage [26], and DynaStore [1]. Our approach allows more general operations. Our liveness model is
 1028  entirely based on that of DynaStore, however.
 1029  This review of prior work could cite a huge number of papers on specific multicast protocols, message-queuing
 1030  middleware products, publish-subscribe products, enterprise service buses ands related technologies. However, we
 1031  concluded that to do so would be tangential and perhaps even confusing to the reader. First, only some of these
 1032  prior technologies offered durability, and among the ones that did (such as message-queuing middleware), some
 1033  accomplished that goal using non-reconfigurable databases or logging servers. Thus, while the area is rich in prior
 1034  work, the prior work on durability across reconfigurations is much scantier, and we believe the key works on that
 1035  specific topic are precisely the ones we looked at closely in the body of this paper.

 1118  A Correctness

 1119  Our goal in this section is to give a flavor of the correctness argument regarding our DSR epoch-by-epoch reconfiguration methodology. We view a formal specification and correctness proofs as being outside the scope of this
 1120  paper. In this section, we provide sketch arguments on two sample solutions, the fault-recovery Reliable Multicast solution (Figure 4) and the fault-masking Reliable Multicast solution (Figure 6). The proof arguments for
 1121  our other protocols, for SMR and for Read/Write storage, are similar in their core arguments concerning reconfiguration. They differ mostly in details regarding the specific problem models, namely, consensus and atomic
 1122  Read/Write storage, which are well studied in the literature.

 1123  A.1 Correctness of Fault-Recovery Reliable Multicast Solution
 1124  In this section, we show that our fault-recovery protocol of Figure 4 maintains Multicast Durability (see Definition 1) and Virtual Synchrony (see Definition 3). Our proof shows that these conditions hold from one configuration
 1125  to the next; proving that it holds for a sequence of reconfigurations follows by induction.
 1126  Refer to a current, initial configuration as C0 and a new configuration as C1. Recall that a message is durable
 1127  if it belongs to a completed Add() or Get() operation.

 1128  Claim 1 If a message m ever becomes durable by Add(m) or Get() operations in C0, and a server q that learns
 1129  about C1 transfers to C1 a set S0 of messages, then m ∈ S0.

 1130  Proof: This claim is the core of the correctness claim. Although it is simple to prove given our algorithmic
 1131  foundation, it is crucial to note that the set of messages sent/delivered in C0 may continue changing after a decision
 1132  on C1 was made, and also subsequent to a state transfer. Nevertheless, the precise statement of this claim is in fact
 1133  stable: Any message m which is ever sent/delivered in C0 is included in S0. We now prove it.
 1134  Since m is in a Add(m) or Get() which completes in C0, every process in C0 performs the ⟨store, m⟩ request
 1135  and acknowledges it. Since wedged servers do not respond to store requests, q has responded to the store request
 1136  before it became wedged for state transfer. Therefore, at step 2 of the state transfer, q already stores m, and includes
 1137  it in the set S0 which it transfers to C1.

 1138  Claim 2 If a message m becomes durable by Add(m) or Get() operations in C0 and a subsequent Get() command
 1139  completes at some process p, then p's response contains m.

 1140  Proof: A Get() request which is subsequent to the completion of the Add(Get) command containing m may
 1141  either occur in C0 or in C1. In C0, every process already acknowledged ⟨store, m⟩. Hence, every server contacted
 1142  by the requesting client will include m in the delivery response. In C1 , by Claim 1 above, every server also stores
 1143  m by the time state transfer has completed. Therefore, Get() in C1 also includes m.

 1144  Claim 3 If a message m becomes durable by Add(m) or Get() operations in C1 and a subsequent Get() command
 1145  completes at some process p, then p's delivery response contains m.

 1146  Proof: We first note that a Get() request that arrives at C0 after m was sent/delivered in C1 is not served by any
 1147  server that participated in reconfiguration, because they are wedged; hence, the deliver request does not complete
 1148  in C0, and is deferred to C1. In C1, every process stores already acknowledged ⟨store, m⟩. Hence, every server
 1149  contacted by the requesting client will include m in the delivery response.

 1150  Claim 4 The fault-recovery protocol maintains Multicast Durability (Definition 1); that is, if a message m becomes durable by Add(m) or Get() operations, and a subsequent Get() command completes at some process p,
 1151  then p's delivery response contains m.
