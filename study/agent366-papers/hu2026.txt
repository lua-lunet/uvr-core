abstract: >-
  Maintaining low tail latency is crucial for cloud storage services. In ALIBABA CLOUD, our Elastic Block Storage (EBS), like many others, adopts layers of load balancing to avoid hot-spot I/Os, a dominant contributor to tail latency. However, in the field, EBS has still been suffering from tail latency spikes. Through extensive analysis of production workloads, we have identified the root cause: the workload bursts caused by a small group of Virtual Disks (VDs), which fundamentally influence the tail latency of the entire cluster. We hence propose a lightweight dual-bucket throttling mechanism to effectively mitigate the issue while maintaining fairness. In addition, we discover that, even under underloaded scenarios, the tail latency remains suboptimal due to the event-loop thread model. We propose a prioritybased scheduling mechanism to separate I/O-related tasks from I/O-unrelated ones. Our evaluation shows that the proposed mechanisms can reduce the tail latency by up to 97% in burst and 43% in underloaded scenarios. Our mechanisms have been deployed across dozens of clusters for more than three months, and have served hundreds of trillions of I/O requests. They reduce the P99999 tail latency of steady segments by 59.7% under burst scenarios and of all I/Os by 22% in underloaded scenarios.

   36  1 Introduction
   37  Elastic Block Storage (EBS) plays an essential role in today's
   38  cloud ecosystem. Formatted as Virtual Disks (VDs) to Virtual Machines (VMs), EBS has been supporting workloads
   39  from single-server databases to large-scale data analytics.
   40  The high-performance nature of these applications has
   41  placed demanding requirements on EBS. Hence, many cloud
   42  vendors, including Azure [5], AWS [2], and us at ALIBABA
   43  CLOUD, choose the compute-to-storage disaggregation architecture for building EBS. Under this setup, the compute cluster—which accommodates the nodes for hosting the
   44  VMs—does not store data. Rather, it interconnects through
   45  the datacenter network to a backend storage cluster which
   46  typically includes two sets of servers, proxy servers (for address translation) and storage servers (for data persistence).
   47  This architecture allows a VD to be served across nodes in
   48  the storage backend, providing a highly scalable throughput.

  116  to reduce the tail latency of steady segments during bursts.
  117  At a high level, the mechanism works by setting up two token buckets in each proxy server and refilling the buckets at
  118  a fixed rate on an interval basis (every 10ms in our case).
  119  The segment can issue an I/O after obtaining a token from
  120  the buckets. The main bucket is up for grabs for all segments, while the cheque bucket is only accessible to steady
  121  ones. The goal of the cheque bucket is to allow temporary
  122  I/O bursts (i.e., at most one interval) and preserve the bandwidth for steady segments.

  241  2.2 Load Balancing in EBS
  242  Achieving a balanced load distribution is crucial for improving the overall performance and, especially, the tail latency.
  243  To reach this goal, first, the Storage Client (on the compute
  244  node) is equipped with multiple hyper threads (e.g., 4-8) to
  245  handle VDs' requests in a round-robin fashion, avoiding the
  246  storage client being drained by a single VD. Furthermore,
  247  along the stack, it is obvious that the requests from VDs are
  248  shuffled among the proxy servers, thereby making the proxy
  249  server an ideal place for performing load balancing. Currently, at the proxy layer, EBS mainly employs three strategies including segmentation, allocation and migration. In
  250  particular, VDs of each client are evenly distributed across
  251  clusters within an availability zone during allocation, preventing over-concentration on any single cluster.
  252  Segmentation. Recall that a VD's LBA is divided into a series of fixed-size segments, and the proxy server operates at
  253  the segment level. Thus, a VD is handled by different proxy
  254  servers. However, such granularity is not enough for balancing the load. One major shortcoming is that the burst I/Os often tend to concentrate on a short range of LBA (i.e., several
  255  MiBs) which is much smaller than the default segment size
  256  (i.e., 32 GiB). If we adjust the segment size to more closely
  257  align with the MiB-level of typical hot spots, the overhead of
  258  metadata can increase by three orders of magnitude or more,
  259  severely stressing the metadata servers.
  260  Therefore, we choose to first divide the entire LBA space
  261  into 2-MiB data blocks and then place them into the 32-GiB
  262  segments in a round-robin manner. For example, if a VD's
  263  capacity is 320 GiB (i.e., 10 segments), then the first segment
  264  will contain the 2-MiB data blocks from LBA 0-2 MiB, 20-
  265  22 MiB, and so on. This way, the hot spots are much more
  266  likely to be distributed across different proxy servers, thus
  267  improving the load balancing.

  293  3 Motivation

  294  3.1 What Went Wrong?
  295  Due to the consequential impacts on Service Level Objectives (i.e., user experiences), handling high tail latency has
  296  always been a priority of maintaining cloud services [6]. Previous work has shown a major cause of high tail latency
  297  to be I/O bursts [7]. With different levels of load balancing mechanisms in EBS enabled (see §2), one question inevitably arises: do the layered load balancing mechanisms
  298  successfully defend against the long tail latency in EBS?
  299  The short answer, unfortunately, is no. In production, we
  300  have still been experiencing frequent occurrences of tail latency spikes. In Figure 1, we demonstrate various levels of
  301  tail (from P99 to P99999)† I/O latency in an EBS cluster for
  302  nearly an hour. We can see that both read and write have suffered multiple tail latency spikes where P99999 metrics can

  305  widely spread across EBS clusters. We collected the latency
  306  statistics of I/O requests from around 100 clusters in a recent week, and we recorded 649 tail latency spikes. 289 of
  307  them even occurred in the same cluster. Furthermore, during

  389  questions (RQs), we intend to explore the rationale, the consequences, and the potential solutions.
  390  RQ1: Why is this phenomenon bound to happen? To avoid
  391  over-concentration of traffic, we have already distributed
  392  VDs from major clients across different servers and clusters.
  393  Yet, the sheer volume of requests nevertheless drives all related servers (and the cluster) to be overloaded. Note that,
  394  enforcing a more stringent distribution policy, say setting the
  395  maximum number of VDs a client can own in a cluster, can
  396  be challenging due to the limited number of clusters in an
  397  availability zone. In addition, while certain "bursty" traf-

  398  USENIX Association

  399  fic is foreseeable before events like shopping festivals, the
  400  individual suddenness remains. Therefore, it is difficult to
  401  predict and (re)allocate resources in advance. RQ2: Do the
  402  impacts only affect the major clients? A na¨ıve opinion is that
  403  the latency spikes are acceptable since it is the major clients
  404  who flooded the clusters in the first place. Unfortunately, the
  405  reality is that the latency spikes are not limited to the major
  406  clients. In fact, while the major clients are certain to take
  407  severe hits, the latency spikes also exist among the minors,
  408  with a 20× tail latency increase for I/Os of the "innocent"
  409  users.
  410  RQ3: Why not limit the traffic from compute ends? One
  411  straightforward solution is to directly limit the traffic inside
  412  the storage client. Obviously, placing a limit on the bandwidth of major clients can alleviate the latency spikes. However, setting an appropriate threshold is challenging. On the
  413  one hand, a fixed limit can lead to either resource underutilization (for being too strict) or ineffectiveness (for being
  414  too generous). For example, if we set the bandwidth limit
  415  of a major client to 60 MBps, the proxy server, on average, leaves 27% headroom left unused. On the other hand,
  416  if we dynamically adjust the threshold, it would require a
  417  near real-time monitoring and feedback mechanism given
  418  that bursts are short-lived (typically last ten to hundreds of
  419  milliseconds, though some extend to tens of seconds). This
  420  is undesirable in practice as accurately adjusting dynamic
  421  thresholds requires frequent interactions among all (tens of
  422  thousands) VMs employing the same proxy server cluster.
  423  Similarly, imposing stricter per-client VD caps would directly reduce the amount of sellable storage capacity, negatively impacting revenue—an unacceptable trade-off for a
  424  commercial cloud service.
  425  RQ4: Why not migrating the hot traffic on storage ends? Alternatively, one might suggest adopting a reactive approach
  426  by migrating the hot traffic to other clusters. The rationale
  427  is that, while a burst may easily flood the current cluster, it
  428  is unlikely such a workload would push all the clusters in
  429  an availability zone to their limits. Therefore, migrating the
  430  hot traffic to cold clusters seems promising. Yet, in practice,
  431  the bursts are both short-lived and unpredictable, thereby
  432  making the migration—which usually takes 20 minutes—
  433  infeasible.
  434  Key Takeaways. In shared storage systems, load balancing spreads localized bursts cluster-wide, amplifying
  435  rather than mitigating tail-latency degradation. Static
  436  rate caps can curb bursts but waste capacity under normal load. This calls for a mechanism that protects minor
  437  clients' tail latency during overload without sacrificing
  438  cluster utilization in the common case.

  469  the fundamental event-based thread model is the culprit for
  470  the high tail latency. As shown in Figure 7, in the proxy

  481  dedicating separate cores for task processing. This allocation initially showed promise by improving task processing
  482  efficiency. By diverting unrelated tasks (i.e., the tasks enclosed by the dashed box in Figure 7) from the I/O thread to
  483  other processing units, the I/O thread could complete its current loop processing more swiftly, facilitating quicker transitions to the next loop for efficient I/O handling. However,
  484  this approach has its limitations. While the majority of tasks
  485  could be offloaded successfully, some tasks remained inherently tied to the I/O processing logic, sharing critical data
  486  structures. Attempting to separate these tasks led to data contention issues, resulting in performance degradation rather
  487  than improvement.
  488  Additionally, existing work primarily focuses on optimizing the user-level thread model [20, 26, 25], which requires
  489  significant architectural changes to the code, making it difficult to quickly address our issues. On the other hand, userlevel thread scheduling introduces additional overhead compared to event-based thread models, and may also require
  490  specialized hardware support [25], making them hard to deploy at scale.
  491  Key Takeaways. Even in the underloaded scenario, tail
  492  latency remains problematic. In event-loop architectures,
  493  I/O-related and I/O-unrelated tasks inevitably share the
  494  same thread and are processed in batches. This batched
  495  execution delays newly arrived I/O requests even when
  496  overall utilization is low. This calls for a scheduling
  497  mechanism that minimizes the latency impact on I/Orelated tasks while ensuring I/O-unrelated tasks still execute reliably.

  498  4 Design

  499  4.1 Design Goals

  500  Goal-1: practical and lightweight. The previous analysis shows that the tail latency is not well optimized under
  501  both burst and underloaded scenarios. Our earlier latency
  502  breakdowns have suggested that the hardware has also contributed significantly to the tail latency. While we notice that
  503  recent advancements in hardware (e.g., 2×200Gbps RDMA
  504  and ZNS NVMe) have offered better guarantees on tail latency, we, as a cloud vendor, can hardly depend on hardware
  505  upgrades to alleviate the tail latency. Apart from being unfavorable to the CapEx due to the large number of deployed
  506  servers, this can also incur huge engineering effort since
  507  a complete overhaul of certain parts or the entire software
  508  stack is often needed for fully utilizing the new hardware.
  509  Therefore, we aim to propose solutions that do not require
  510  extensive restructuring of the system architecture and only
  511  touch the necessary parts of the data path.
  512  Goal-2: effective and fair. Obviously, our solutions need
  513  to be effective in reducing the tail latency in both burst and
  514  underloaded scenarios. Our discussion on the simple fixes
  515  has shown that certain methods can indeed reduce the tail
  516  latency but at the cost of introducing side effects. Again, as a
  517  cloud vendor, we emphasize the fairness and thus expect that
  518  our approaches, if not beneficial to all clients, would at least
  519  not worsen the experiences (i.e., Service Level Objectives,
  520  SLOs).

  529  4.2 Handling Burst Scenario

  530  Observation. In § 3, we have shown that limiting I/O rates
  531  on major clients is suboptimal in that it would backfire on
  532  the utilization of the proxy server on average. This motivates
  533  us to revisit the field traces and leads to the following observations.
  534  • I/O carries different service guarantees in EBS. When a
  535  virtual disk is created, users are provisioned with a baseline level of throughput/IOPS, which defines the SLAbound base I/O that must be satisfied in terms of available bandwidth. Demand beyond this baseline is treated
  536  as burst I/O and is served on a best-effort basis without explicit guarantees. Under high load, our goal is to
  537  sustain throughput while protecting SLA-bound base I/O
  538  from burst interference.
  539  • Not all segments of the major clients under bursts are hot.
  540  In fact, most of them are not. As aforementioned, the average CV of segments in the same VD is 47.7, revealing
  541  a weak correlation among those segments. Therefore, instead of throttling the entire client, we should operate at a
  542  finer granularity, i.e., segment-level.
  543  • The underlying DFS can tolerate I/O bursts, at least for
  544  a short period of time. Recall that the bandwidth of a
  545  proxy server is limited to 1.2 GBps to avoid overloading the DFS. This strict limit is set to reserve enough
  546  resources for handling extremely high background loads
  547  (e.g., GC). However, temporarily exceeding this limit during low background loads would not cause a severe performance penalty.
  548  • The bursts are short-lived. We have profiled the burst durations and found that the majority of the bursts last less
  549  than 100ms with a few exceptional cases persisting over a
  550  few seconds.
  551  Key idea. The observations above motivate a key idea: instead of throttling hot segments from major clients during
  552  bursts, we let steady segments overdraw tokens, which simultaneously preserves overall resource utilization and ensures that minor clients' I/O demands are not disrupted.
  553  Moreover, by estimating the actual available resources, we
  554  can compensate the steady segments with dedicated tokens

  595  Dual-bucket throttling. To enable the steady segments to
  596  overdraw tokens, we introduce a dual-bucket mechanism.
  597  The first bucket, called the main bucket, functions as usual
  598  and is shared by all segments. The size of the main bucket
  599  is equal to the total tokens issued in an interval. The second
  600  bucket, called the cheque bucket, is only accessible to the
  601  steady segments. The size of the cheque token bucket is dynamically set based on the detected bandwidth over a recent
  602  period.
  603  In practice, the initialization process fills both buckets
  604  with tokens to full capacity. Under normal pressure, all
  605  segments are "steady" and consume tokens from the main
  606  bucket. Every interval, the proxy server first refills the main
  607  bucket. Then, if the cheque bucket is not full, the main
  608  bucket will give enough tokens to fulfill the cheque bucket
  609  before serving the real I/O requests.
  610  When a burst occurs, the monitor would identify and mark
  611  the hot segments. And, the main bucket would be (presumably) quickly drained by the hot segments. Now, the steady
  612  segments can consume tokens from the cheque bucket and
  613  hot ones are thereby throttled. After the periodic interval T,
  614  the proxy server would refill the cheque bucket with δpT,
  615  and fill the main bucket with the remaining tokens.
  616  Now, if the burst diminishes, the hot segments would be
  617  marked as steady in the next interval. And, all things go
  618  back to normal as the steady segments can grab 100% of
  619  the tokens. Both buckets would be full again in the next interval(s). If the burst persists, the remaining hot segments
  620  would continue to consume tokens from the main bucket
  621  while the steady can draw tokens from both.
  622  The key of dual-bucket throttling is to reduce the impact of
  623  bursts on the steady segments. Therefore, the cheque bucket
  624  allows the steady segments to get tokens even when the main
  625  bucket is depleted. Yet, such an overdraw mechanism does
  626  not last as the underlying DFS would eventually be oversaturated. Therefore, we only allow overdrawing for an interval. This mechanism helps to throttle the hot segments'
  627  I/O rate by forcing them to only consume tokens from the
  628  main bucket.
  629  Configuration parameters. The success of dual-bucket
  630  throttling depends on two key parameters: the span of the
  631  interval and the size of the cheque bucket. First, the span
  632  of the interval should be long enough to endure most burst
  633  scenarios while short enough to avoid over-saturation of the
  634  DFS. Based on the fact that the average duration of bursts is
  635  hundreds of milliseconds, we set the interval T to be 10ms
  636  to timely react to the occurrence of bursts.
  637  Second, the size of the cheque bucket should be set to a
  638  value that can allow the steady segments to overdraw enough
  639  tokens to avoid most tail latency spikes. Meanwhile, having
  640  an overly large cheque bucket would backfire by worsening
  641  the tail latency for the hot segments. Based on field statistics,
  642  we set the size as 20% which can also be adjusted on-the-fly.

  657  4.3 Handling Underloaded Scenario

  658  Root causes. The latency breakdown indicates that simply
  659  shifting some tasks (e.g., GC, snapshot and index compact)
  660  has limited effectiveness. The reasons are two-fold. First,
  661  the proxy server runs the event-loop model, similar to common practices [30], in the idle mode and thus the pending
  662  requests in the rx buffer are not handled in a timely fashion.
  663  Second, while all client I/Os are processed in the frontend as
  664  tasks, not all tasks are directly related to I/O processing. In
  665  fact, the I/O-unrelated tasks (including monitoring, statistics
  666  and dump) can delay I/O processing. Hence, our target is
  667  to prioritize the I/O-related tasks and minimize the rx buffer
  668  waiting time.
  669  Priority-based task scheduling. Figure 9 shows that, for the
  670  frontend event loop, we further enforce the following three
  671  policies in the task scheduling:
  672  • High priority first. We have set up two queues for I/Orelated and I/O-unrelated tasks. The I/O-related task queue
  673  includes journal cache lookup, compressing the write requests and decompressing messages in processing read requests. The I/O-unrelated task queue includes I/O thread
  674  monitoring, resource statistics, dump operations and others. During each loop, the proxy server now always starts
  675  with the execution of I/O-related tasks and will not process
  676  the I/O-unrelated tasks until all enqueued I/O-related tasks
  677  are done.
  678  • Fixed span for I/O-unrelated tasks. To prevent new I/Orelated tasks (pending in the rx buffer) from waiting too
  679  long, we introduce a loop timeout LT. This timeout caps
  680  only the execution of I/O-unrelated tasks: if I/O-related
  681  tasks exceed LT, their execution is not interrupted; the
  682  timeout only prevents subsequent I/O-unrelated tasks from
  683  running in the current loop.
  684  • Frequent rx buffer check. To promptly receive and enqueue
  685  the I/O-related tasks for timely execution, we check the rx
  686  buffer at the beginning of every loop as long as the I/Orelated queue is empty.

  952  7 Potential Limitations

  955  on the large number of users (i.e., millions of VDs deployed)
  956  and widespread major clients' bursts, we are convinced that
  957  this phenomenon is indeed representative. In addition, the

  981  8 Conclusion

  982  In this paper, we systematically revisited a pressing issue in
  983  our cloud block store system, high tail latency. We first identify the root causes of suboptimal tail latency in production—
  984  despite the presence of multiple layers of load balancing—to
  985  be workload bursts caused by major clients and the inefficient event-loop thread model. We then propose dual-bucket
  986  throttling and priority-based scheduling, to mitigate the issue. Results in production show that the proposed mechanisms, while simple and lightweight, can significantly reduce
  987  the tail latency in varying scenarios.

 1175  A M/M/1 Model Derivation
 1176  The process of a proxy server can be abstracted as a singleserver queueing model [21]. In the M/M/1 model, requests
 1177  arrive at rate λ (following a Poisson process) and the server
 1178  processes them at rate µ (exponentially distributed). The total I/O latency W (including both queuing time and service
 1179  time) follows an Exponential distribution. The M/M/1 queue
 1180  has the following characteristics:

 1181  • The probability density function (PDF) of service time
 1182  (i.e., the processing latency of a single I/O in Proxy Layer
 1183  and Storage Layer) is:

 1184  f (t) = µe−µt, t ≥ 0 (3)
 1185  s

 1186  • The PDF of inter-arrival time of two adjacent I/Os is:

 1187  f (t) = λe−λt, t ≥ 0 (4)
 1188  a

 1189  The overall waiting time (i.e., I/O latency) w in the system
 1190  is the time from I/O arrival to completion, including both
 1191  waiting in the queue and service time. The PDF for the I/O
 1192  latency fW (w) can be calculated as:

 1193  λ
 1194  f (w) = λe−λw 1 − e−(µ−λ)w, w ≥ 0 (5)
 1195  W µ

 1196  The cumulative distribution function (CDF) FW (w) of the
 1197  I/O latency is:

 1198  F (w) = 1 − e−(µ−λ)w (6)
 1199  W
 1200  As a result, the expected I/O latency for any percentile (p)
 1201  can be calculated as:
 1202  ln(1 − p)
 1203  wp = α (7)
 1204  µ − λ
 1205  where α is a correction factor to account for the deviation
 1206  from ideal exponential distributions in practice.
