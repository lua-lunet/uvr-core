   14  Abstract
   15  This paper considers the problem of building fault-tolerant shared objects when processes can
   16  crash and recover but lose their persistent state on recovery. This Diskless Crash-Recovery
   17  (DCR) model matches the way many long-lived systems are built. We show that it presents
   18  new challenges, as operations that are recorded at a quorum may not persist after some of the
   19  processes in that quorum crash and then recover.
   20  To address this problem, we introduce the notion of crash-consistent quorums, where no recoveries happen during the quorum responses. We show that relying on crash-consistent quorums
   21  enables a recovery procedure that can recover all operations that successfully finished. Crashconsistent quorums can be easily identified using a mechanism we term the crash vector, which
   22  tracks the causal relationship between crashes, recoveries, and other operations.
   23  We apply crash-consistent quorums and crash vectors to build two storage primitives. We
   24  give a new algorithm for multi-writer, multi-reader atomic registers in the DCR model that
   25  guarantees safety under all conditions and termination under a natural condition. It improves
   26  on the best prior protocol for this problem by requiring fewer rounds, fewer nodes to participate
   27  in the quorum, and a less restrictive liveness condition. We also present a more efficient singlewriter, single-reader atomic set – a virtual stable storage abstraction. It can be used to lift any
   28  existing algorithm from the traditional Crash-Recovery model to the DCR model. We examine
   29  a specific application, state machine replication, and show that existing diskless protocols can
   30  violate their correctness guarantees, while ours offers a general and correct solution.

   45  1 Introduction

   46  Today's distributed systems are key pieces of infrastructure that must remain available
   47  even though the servers that implement them are constantly failing. These systems are
   48  long-lived and must be able to tolerate nodes crashing and rejoining the system. In particular,
   49  nodes must be able to rejoin the system even after losing their disk state, a real concern for
   50  large-scale data centers where hard drive failures are a regular occurrence [7].
   51  This paper addresses the problem of how to build recoverable shared objects even when
   52  processes lose their entire state. We consider the Diskless Crash-Recovery model: each
   53  process in the system may go down at any time; upon recovery, it loses all state it had
   54  before the crash except for its identity. However, processes can run a recovery protocol to
   55  reconstruct their state before deeming themselves operational again. This model matches
   56  the way that many distributed systems are built in practice.
   57  The Diskless Crash-Recovery model (DCR) is more challenging than the traditional
   58  Crash-Stop model (CS) or the Crash-Recovery with Stable Storage model (CRSS). The main
   59  challenge is that an invariant that holds at one process may not hold on that process's next
   60  incarnation after recovery. This leads to the problem of unstable quorums: it is possible
   61  for a majority of processes to acknowledge a write operation, and yet processes can still
   62  subsequently lose that knowledge after crash and recovery.
   63  We provide a general mechanism for building recoverable shared objects in the DCR
   64  model. We show that an operation can be made recoverable once it is stored by a crashconsistent quorum, which we informally define as one where no recoveries happen during the
   65  quorum responses. Crash-consistent quorums can be efficiently identified using a mechanism
   66  called the crash vector: a vector, maintained by each process, that tracks the latest known
   67  incarnation of each process. By including crash vectors in protocol messages, processes can
   68  identify the causal relationship between crash recoveries and other operations. This makes it
   69  possible to discard responses that are not part of a crash-consistent quorum. We show that
   70  this is sufficient to make storage mechanisms recoverable.
   71  The crash-consistent quorum approach is a general strategy for making storage primitives
   72  recoverable. We give two concrete examples in this paper, both of which are always safe and
   73  guarantee liveness during periods of stability; other storage primitives are also possible:
   74  First, we build a multi-writer, multi-reader atomic register by extending the well-known
   75  ABD protocol [3] with crash vectors. This improves on the best prior protocol by Konwar
   76  et al. [17], RADON(S), for this problem: it requires fewer rounds (2 rather than 3),
   77  R
   78  requires fewer nodes to participate in the protocol (a simple majority vs 3/4), and has a
   79  less restrictive liveness condition.
   80  Second, we construct a single-writer, single-reader atomic set, which has weaker semantics
   81  yet permits a more efficient implementation, requiring only a single round of communication for writes. We refer to this algorithm as virtual stable storage, as it offers consistency
   82  semantics similar to a local disk. We show that the virtual stable storage protocol can be
   83  used to transform any protocol that operates in the traditional CS or CRSS models to
   84  one that operates in DCR.
   85  We discuss the application of this work to state machine replication, a widely used
   86  distributed system technique. Recovering from disk failures is an important concern in
   87  practice, and recent replication protocols attempt to support recovery after complete loss
   88  of state. Surprisingly, we find that each of the three such protocols [7, 16, 22] can lose data.
   89  We identify a general problem: while these protocols go to great lengths to ensure that a
   90  recovering replica reconstructs the set of operations it previously processed, they fail to

   96  To summarize, this paper makes the following contributions:
   97  It formalizes a Diskless Crash-Recovery (DCR) failure model in a way that captures the
   98  challenges of long-lived applications (Section 3).
   99  It introduces the notion of crash-consistent quorums and provides two communication

  101  It presents algorithms built on top of our communications primitives for two different
  102  shared objects in the DCR model: an atomic multi-writer, multi-reader register and an
  103  atomic single-writer, single-reader set. The former is a general purpose register which
  104  demonstrates the generality of our approach, while the latter provides a virtual stable
  105  storage interface that can be used to port any protocol in the CRSS model to one for the
  106  DCR model (Section 5).
  107  Finally, it examines prior protocols for state machine replication in the DCR failure model
  108  and demonstrates flaws in these protocols that lead to violations of safety properties. Our
  109  two communication primitives can provide correct solutions (Section 6).

  110  2 Background and Related Work

  111  Static Systems. A static system comprises a fixed, finite set of processes. Fault-tolerant
  112  protocols for reliable storage for static systems have been studied extensively in the Crash-Stop
  113  (CS) failure model, where processes that fail never rejoin the system, and the Crash-Recovery
  114  with Stable Storage model (CRSS). In the latter model, processes recover with the same state
  115  after a crash. Consensus and related problems, in particular, have been studied extensively
  116  in these settings [8, 12, 30]. In CRSS, a crashed and recovered node is no different than one
  117  which was temporarily unavailable; asynchronous algorithms that tolerate lossy networks are
  118  inherently robust to these types of failures [8].
  119  Prior work on fault-tolerant shared objects and consensus without stable storage generally
  120  requires some subset of the processes to never fail [2, 11]. Aguilera et al. [2] showed an
  121  impossibility result for a crash-recovery model: even with certain synchrony assumptions,
  122  consensus cannot be solved without at least one process that never crashes. The main
  123  differentiator between that work and this paper is that in their model, the states of processes
  124  were binary – either "up" or "down." We overcome this limitation by adding an extra
  125  "recovering" state. As long as the number of processes which are "down" or "recovering" at
  126  any given time is bounded, certain problems can be solved even without processes that never
  127  fail.
  128  Recently, Konwar et al. [17] presented a set of algorithms for implementing an atomic
  129  multi-writer, multi-reader (MWMR) register in a model similar to ours. We generalize and
  130  improve on this work using new primitives for crash-consistent quorums. Our techniques are
  131  applicable to other forms of shared objects as well, and our MWMR register is more efficient:
  132  it requires one fewer phase and a simple majority quorum (vs 3/4).
  133  Several recent practical state machine replication systems [7, 16, 22] incorporate ad hoc
  134  recovery mechanisms for nodes to recover from total disk loss. The common intuition behind
  135  these approaches is that a write to disk can be replaced with a write to a quorum of other
  136  nodes, recovering after a failure by performing a quorum read. However, we show that these
  137  protocols are not correct; they can lose data in certain failure scenarios. A more recent
  138  design, Replacement [13], provides a mechanism for replacing failed processes. Like our work

  144  Dynamic Systems. In a dynamic setting, processes may leave or join the system at will.
  145  Although we consider a static system, DCR may be viewed as a dynamic system with a
  146  finite concurrency level [26], i.e, where there is a finite bound on the maximum number of
  147  processes that are simultaneously active, over all runs. Here, a recovering process without
  148  state is equivalent to a newly joined process.
  149  Many dynamic systems implement reconfiguration protocols [1, 10, 21–24, 33]. Reconfiguration allows one to change the set of members allowed to participate in the computation.
  150  This process allows both adding new processes and removing processes from the system.
  151  Reconfiguration is a more general problem than recovery: it can be used to handle disk failure
  152  by introducing a recovering node as a new member and removing its previous incarnation.
  153  However, general reconfiguration protocols are a blunt instrument, as they must be able to
  154  handle completely changing the membership to a disjoint set of processes . As a result, these
  155  protocols are costly. Most use consensus to agree on the order of reconfigurations, which
  156  delays the processing of concurrent operations [28]. DynaStore [1] is the first proposal which
  157  does not require consensus, but reconfigurations can still delay R/W operations [28]. Smart-
  158  Merge [14] improves on DynaStore by offering a more expressive reconfiguration interface.
  159  Recovery is a special case of reconfiguration, where each recovering process replaces, and
  160  has the same identity as, a previously crashed process. As a result, it permits more efficient
  161  solutions.
  162  Other protocols implement shared registers and other storage primitives in churn-prone
  163  systems [4–6, 15]. In these systems, processes are constantly joining and leaving the system,
  164  but at a bounded rate. These protocols remain safe only when churn remains within the
  165  specified bound, in contrast to our work which is always safe. Most of these protocols also
  166  require synchrony assumptions for correctness. However, under these assumptions they are
  167  able to provide liveness guarantees even during constant churn.

  168  3 System Model

  169  We begin by defining our failure model: Diskless Crash-Recovery (DCR), a variant of the
  170  classic Crash-Recovery model where processes lose their entire state upon crashing.
  171  We consider an asynchronous distributed system which consists of a fixed set of n
  172  processes, Π. Each process has a unique name (identifier) of some kind; we assume processes
  173  are numbered 1, . . . , n for simplicity. Each process executes a protocol (formally, it is an
  174  I/O automaton [25]) while it is up. An execution of a protocol proceeds in discrete time
  175  steps, numbered with N, starting at t = 0. At each step, at most one process either processes
  176  an input action, processes a message, crashes, or restarts. If it crashes, the process stops
  177  receiving messages and input actions, loses its state, and is considered down. A process that
  178  is down can restart and transition back to the up state. We make the following assumptions
  179  about a process that restarts: (1) it knows it is restarting, (2) it knows its unique name
  180  and the names of the other processes in the system (i.e., this information survives crashes),
  181  and (3) it can obtain an incarnation ID that is distinct from all the ones that it previously
  182  obtained. Note that the incarnation ID need only be unique among different incarnations
  183  of a specific process, not the entire system. These are reasonable assumptions to make for
  184  real-world systems: (1) and (2) are fixed for a given deployment, and (3) can be obtained,
  185  for example, from a source of randomness or the local processor clock.

  187  Processes are connected by an asynchronous network. Messages can be duplicated a finite
  188  number of times or reordered arbitrarily – but not modified – by the network. We assume
  189  that if an incarnation of a process remains up, sends a message, and an incarnation of the
  190  destination process stays up long enough, that message will eventually be delivered.1
  191  The unique incarnation ID makes it possible to distinguish different incarnations of the
  192  same process. Without unique incarnation IDs, processes are vulnerable to "replay attacks:"
  193  I Theorem 1. Any state reached by a process that has crashed, restarted, and taken steps
  194  without receiving an input action or crashing again will always be reachable by that process.
  195  Proof. Suppose process p has crashed, restarted, and taken some number of steps without
  196  crashing or receiving an input action. That is, suppose that after it restarted, p received some
  197  sequence of messages, M. Because p is an I/O automaton without access to randomness or
  198  unique incarnation IDs, anytime p crashes and restarts, it restarts into the exact same state.
  199  Furthermore, if p crashes, restarts, and receives the same sequence of messages, M, having
  200  been duplicated by the network, p will always end up in the same state. J

  201  A corollary to Theorem 1 is that any protocol in the DCR model without unique incarnation
  202  IDs satisfying the safety properties of consensus – or even a simple shared object such as a
  203  register – can reach a state from which terminating states are not reachable (i.e., a state
  204  of deadlock). If all processes crash and recover before deciding a value or receiving a write,
  205  they can always return to this earlier state, so the protocol cannot safely make progress.
  206  For simplicity of exposition, we assume that the incarnation ID increases monotonically.
  207  We explain in our technical report [27] how to eliminate this requirement.
  208  A restarting process must recover parts of its state. To do so, it runs a distinct recovery
  209  protocol. This protocol can communicate with other processes to recover previous state.
  210  Once the recovery protocol terminates, the process declares recovery complete and resumes
  211  execution of its normal protocol. We describe a process that is up as recovering if it is
  212  running its recovery protocol and operational when it is running the initial automaton. A
  213  protocol in this model should satisfy recovery termination: a recovering process eventually
  214  becomes operational, as long as it does not crash again in the meantime. This precludes
  215  vacuous solutions where recovering process never again participate in the normal protocol.
  216  Using a separate recovery protocol matches the design of existing protocols like Viewstamped Replication [22]. Importantly, the distinction between recovering and operational allows failure bounds in terms of the number of operational processes, e.g., that
  217  fewer than half of the processes can be either down or recovering at any moment. This
  218  circumvents Aguilera et al.'s impossibility result for consensus [2], which does not make such
  219  a distinction (i.e., restarting processes are immediately considered operational).

  220  4 Achieving Crash-Consistent Quorums

  221  Making shared objects recoverable in the DCR model requires a new type of quorum to
  222  capture the idea of persistent, recoverable knowledge. A simple quorum does not suffice. We
  223  demonstrate the problem through a simple straw-man example, and introduce the concepts
  224  of crash-consistent quorums and crash vectors to solve the problem. We use these to build
  225  generic quorum communication and recovery primitives.

  230  4.1 Unstable Quorums: Intuition
  231  Consider an intentionally simple example: a fault-tolerant safe register that supports a
  232  single writer and multiple readers. A safe register [19] is the weakest form of register, as
  233  the behavior of READ operations is only defined when there are no concurrent WRITEs.
  234  We further constrain the problem by allowing the writer to only ever execute one WRITE
  235  operation. That is, the only safety requirement is that once the WRITE completes, all
  236  subsequent READs that return must return the value written.
  237  In the Crash-Stop model, a trivial quorum protocol suffices: WRITE(val) broadcasts val
  238  and waits for acknowledgments from a quorum. Here, we consider majority quorums:
  239  I Definition 2. A quorum Q is a set of processes such that Q ∈ Q = {Q : Q ∈ 2Π ∧ |Q| >
  240  n/2}.
  241  A subsequent READ would then be implemented by reading from a quorum. The quorum
  242  intersection property (i.e., ∀Q1, Q2 ∈ Q Q1 ∩ Q2 6= {} ) guarantees that at least one process
  243  will return val for a READ that happens after the WRITE. It is easy to extend this protocol
  244  to the CRSS model simply by having each process log val to disk before replying to a WRITE.
  245  Could we use this same quorum protocol in our DCR model, where processes that crash
  246  recover without stable storage, by augmenting it with a recovery protocol that satisfies
  247  recovery termination? In fact, for this particular protocol, there is no recovery protocol that
  248  both guarantees the safety requirement and recovery termination – even if there is a majority
  249  of processes which are operational at any instant! In order to tolerate the crashes of a
  250  minority of processes and satisfy recovery termination, any recovery protocol must be able to
  251  proceed after communicating with only a simple majority of processes. However, if a process
  252  crashes in the middle of the WRITE procedure – after acknowledging val – it may recover
  253  before a majority of processes have received val. No recovery procedure that communicates
  254  only with this quorum of processes can cause the process to relearn val.
  255  We term the resulting situation an unstable quorum: the WRITE operation received
  256  responses from a quorum, and yet by the time it completes there may no longer exist a
  257  majority of processes that know val. It is thus possible to form a quorum of processes that
  258  either acknowledged val but then lost it during recovery, or never received the write request
  259  (delayed by the network). A subsequent READ could fail by reading from such a quorum.
  260  Although this is a simple example, many important systems suffer from precisely this
  261  problem of unstable quorums. We show in Section 6 that essentially this scenario can cause
  262  three different state machine replication protocols to lose important pieces of state.

  263  4.2 Crash-Consistent Quorums
  264  We can avoid this problem – both for the straw-man problem above and in the general case –
  265  by relying not just on simple quorums of responses but crash-consistent ones.

  266  Crash Consistency. We informally define a crash-consistent quorum to be one where no
  267  recoveries of processes in the quorum happen during the quorum responses. More precisely:
  268  I Definition 3. Let E be the set of all events in an execution. A set of events, E ⊆ E, is
  269  crash-consistent if ∀e1, e2 ∈ E there is no e3 ∈ E that takes place at a later incarnation of
  270  the same process as e1 such that e3 → e2. Here, → represents Lamport's happens-before
  271  relation [18].
  272  In Section 4.3, we show how to build recoverable primitives using crash-consistent quorums,
  273  in which all quorum replies (i.e. the message send events at a quorum) are crash-consistent.

  275  Crash Vectors. How does a process acquire a crash-consistent quorum of responses? The
  276  mechanism that allows us to ensure a crash-consistent quorum is the crash vector. This
  277  is a vector that contains, for each process, its latest known incarnation ID. Like a version
  278  vector, processes attach their crash vector to the relevant protocol messages and use incoming
  279  messages to update their crash vector. The crash vector thus tracks the causal relationship
  280  between crash recoveries and other operations. When acquiring a quorum on a WRITE
  281  operation, we check whether any of the crash vectors are inconsistent with each other,
  282  indicating that a recovery may have happened concurrently with one of the responses. We
  283  then discard any responses from previous incarnations of the recovering process, ensuring a
  284  crash-consistent quorum, and thus avoiding the aforementioned problem.

  285  4.3 Communication Primitives in DCR

  286  We now describe in detail two generic quorum communication primitives, one of which acquires
  287  a crash-consistent quorum, as well as a generic recovery procedure. These primitives require
  288  their users to implement an abstract interface: Read-State, which returns a representation
  289  of the state of the shared object; Update-State, which alters the current state with a
  290  specific value; and Rebuild-State, which is called during recovery and takes a set of state
  291  representations and combines them.
  292  The Acquire-Quorum primitive writes a value to a crash-consistent quorum and returns
  293  the latest state. The Read-Quorum primitive returns a fresh – but possibly inconsistent –
  294  snapshot of the state as maintained at a quorum of processes. If Acquire-Quorum(val)
  295  succeeds, then any subsequent Read-Quorum will return at least one response from a
  296  process that knows (i.e., has previously updated its state with) val.
  297  The detailed protocol implementing the two primitives and the recovery procedure is
  298  presented as pseudo-code in Algorithm 1. We present the algorithm using a modified I/O
  299  automaton notation. In our protocol, procedures are input actions that can be invoked
  300  at any time (e.g., in a higher level protocol); functions are simple methods; and upon
  301  clauses specify how processes handle external events (i.e., messages, system initialization, and
  302  recovery). We use guards to prevent actions from being activated under certain conditions.
  303  If the guard of a message handler or procedure is not satisfied, no action is taken, and the
  304  message is not consumed (i.e., it remains in the network undelivered).
  305  Each of the n process in Π maintains a crash vector, v, with one entry for each process in
  306  the system. Entry i in this vector tracks the latest known incarnation ID of process i. During
  307  an incarnation, a process numbers its acquire and read messages using the local variable
  308  c to match messages with replies. When a process recovers, it gets a new value from its
  309  local, monotonic clock and updates its incarnation ID in its own vector. When the recovery
  310  procedure ends, the process becomes operational and signals this through the op flag. A
  311  process's crash vector is updated whenever a process learns about a newer incarnation of
  312  another process. Crash vectors are partially ordered, and a join operation, denoted t, is
  313  defined over vectors, where (v1 t v2)[i] = max(v1[i], v2[i]). Initially, each process's crash
  314  vector is [⊥, . . . , ⊥], where ⊥ is some value smaller than any incarnation ID.
  315  The Acquire-Quorum function handles both writing values and recovering. Acquire-
  316  Quorum ensures the persistence of both the process's current crash vector – in particular
  317  the process's own incarnation ID in the vector – as well as the value to be written, val. It
  318  provides these guarantees by collecting responses from a quorum of processes and ensuring
  319  that those responses are crash-consistent. It uses crash vectors to detect when any process
  320  that previously replied could have crashed and thus could have "forgotten" the written value.

  505  4.4.2 Liveness

  506  Acquire-Quorum and Read-Quorum terminate if there is some quorum of processes that
  507  all remain operational for a sufficient period of time.2 This is easy to see since a writing
  508  or recovering process will eventually get an acquire-rep from each of these operational
  509  processes, and those replies must be crash-consistent. Note that the termination of Acquire-
  510  Quorum implies the termination of the recovery procedure, Recover. Therefore, the same
  511  liveness conditions are required for Acquire-Quorum and for recovery termination.
  512  We define a sufficient liveness condition, LC, below. It is a slightly weaker version of
  513  the network stability condition N2 from [17]: the period in which processes must remain
  514  operational is shorter.
  515  I Definition 12 (Liveness Condition (LC)). Consider a process p executing either the
  516  Acquire-Quorum or Read-Quorum function, φ, and consider the following statements:
  517  1. There exists a quorum of processes, Q, all of which consume their respective messages
  518  sent from φ.
  519  2. Every process in Q either
  520  a. remains operational during the interval [T1, T2], where T1 is the point in time
  521  at which φ was invoked and T2 the earliest point in time at which p completes the
  522  consumption of all the responses sent by the processes in Q or
  523  b. becomes down and remains down during the same interval after p consumed its
  524  response.
  525  If these two statements are true for every invocation of a Acquire-Quorum or Read-
  526  Quorum function, then we say that LC is satisfied.

  534  5 Recoverable Shared Objects in DCR

  535  In this section we demonstrate the benefits of our quorum communication primitives for DCR:
  536  generality and efficiency. We present protocols for two different shared objects: a multi-writer,
  537  multi-reader (MWMR) atomic register and a single-writer, single-reader (SWSR) atomic set.
  538  In both protocols, Read and Write are intended to be invoked serially.
  539  The first protocol implements a shared, fault-tolerant MWMR atomic register in DCR. It
  540  is more efficient and has better liveness conditions than prior work. The second protocol
  541  implements a weaker abstraction – a shared, fault-tolerant SWSR atomic set. We use this
  542  set as a basic storage primitive to provide processes with access to their own virtual stable
  543  storage (VSS), an emulation of a local disk. This enables easy migration of protocols to DCR.

  544  5.1 Multi-writer, Multi-reader Atomic Register

  548  for version control, as in the original protocol. A timestamp is defined as a triple (z, i, v[i]),

  617  6 Recoverable Replicated State Machines in DCR

  618  We further extend our study of DCR to another specific problem: state machine replication
  619  (SMR). SMR is a classic approach for building fault-tolerant services [18, 32] that calls for the
  620  service to be modeled as a deterministic state machine, replicated over a group of replicas.
  621  System correctness requires each replica to execute the same set of operations in the same
  622  order, even as replicas and network links fail. This is typically achieved using a consensusbased replication protocol such as Multi-Paxos [20] or Viewstamped Replication [22, 29] to
  623  establish a global order of client requests.

  626  We examined three diskless recovery protocols for SMR: Viewstamped Replication [22],
  627  Paxos Made Live [7], and JPaxos [16]. We found that each of these protocols suffers from the
  628  problem illustrated in the example at the beginning of Section 4: they use regular quorums of
  629  responses (instead of crash-consistent ones) when persisting critical data, which could violate
  630  their invariants. This can lead to operations being lost, or different operations being executed
  631  at different replicas, both serious correctness violations. We provide here brief explanations
  632  of the problems in each of these protocols. For more details on how the protocols work and
  633  complete traces, see our technical report [27].
  634  Viewstamped Replication [29] is the first consensus-based SMR protocol. The original
  635  version of the protocol requires a single write to disk, during a view change. A recent VR
  636  variant [22] replaces the write to disk with a write to a quorum of replicas, in an attempt to
  637  eliminate the necessity for disks. However, it uses simple quorum responses, allowing the
  638  recovering replica to violate an important invariant: once a replica committed to take part
  639  in a new view, it will never operate in a lower view. As a result, an operation can complete
  640  successfully and then be lost after a view change.
  641  Paxos Made Live [7] is Google's Multi-Paxos implementation. To handle corrupted
  642  disks, it lets a replica rejoin the system without its previous state and runs an (unspecified)
  643  recovery protocol to restore the application state. The replica must then wait to observe
  644  a full instance of successful consensus before participating. This successfully prevents the
  645  replica from accepting multiple values for the same instance (e.g., one before and one after the
  646  crash). However, it does not prevent the replica from sending different promises (i.e., leader
  647  change commitments) to potential new leaders, which can lead to a new leader deciding a
  648  new value for a prior successful instance of consensus.
  649  JPaxos [16], a hybrid of Multi-Paxos and VR, provides a variety of deployment options,
  650  including a diskless one. Nodes in JPaxos maintain an epoch vector that tracks which nodes
  651  have crashed and recovered to discard lost promises made by prior incarnations of recovered
  652  nodes. However, like VR and PML, certain failures during node recovery can cause the
  653  system to lose state and violate safety properties.
  654  All of these protocols can be correctly migrated to DCR, with little effort, using VSS
  655  write operations, as explained in Section 5.2. This approach is straightforward, efficient, and
  656  requires no invasive protocol modifications.

  657  7 Conclusion

  658  This paper examined the Diskless Crash-Recovery model, where process can crash and recover
  659  but lose their state. We show how to provide persistence guarantees in this model using
  660  new quorum primitives that write to and read from crash-consistent quorums. These general
  661  primitives allow us to construct shared objects in the DCR model. In particular, we show a
  662  MWMR atomic register protocol requiring fewer communication rounds and weaker liveness
  663  assumptions than the best prior work. We also build a SWSR atomic set that can be used
  664  to provide each process with virtual stable storage, which can be used to easily migrate any
  665  protocol from traditional Crash-Recovery models to DCR.
