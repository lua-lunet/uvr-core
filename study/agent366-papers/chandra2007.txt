    6  Abstract
    7  We describe our experience building a fault-tolerant data-base using the Paxos consensus algorithm.
    8  Despite the existing literature in the field, building such a database proved to be non-trivial. We describe
    9  selected algorithmic and engineering problems encountered, and the solutions we found for them. Our
   10  measurements indicate that we have built a competitive system.

   11  1 Introduction

   12  It is well known that fault-tolerance on commodity hardware can be achieved through replication [17, 18]. A
   13  common approach is to use a consensus algorithm [7] to ensure that all replicas are mutually consistent [8,
   14  14, 17]. By repeatedly applying such an algorithm on a sequence of input values, it is possible to build an
   15  identical log of values on each replica. If the values are operations on some data structure, application of
   16  the same log on all replicas may be used to arrive at mutually consistent data structures on all replicas. For
   17  instance, if the log contains a sequence of database operations, and if the same sequence of operations is
   18  applied to the (local) database on each replica, eventually all replicas will end up with the same database
   19  content (provided that they all started with the same initial database state).
   20  This general approach can be used to implement a wide variety of fault-tolerant primitives, of which a
   21  fault-tolerant database is just an example. As a result, the consensus problem has been studied extensively
   22  over the past two decades. There are several well-known consensus algorithms that operate within a multitude
   23  of settings and which tolerate a variety of failures. The Paxos consensus algorithm [8] has been discussed in
   24  the theoretical [16] and applied community [10, 11, 12] for over a decade.
   25  We used the Paxos algorithm ("Paxos") as the base for a framework that implements a fault-tolerant
   26  log. We then relied on that framework to build a fault-tolerant database. Despite the existing literature on
   27  the subject, building a production system turned out to be a non-trivial task for a variety of reasons:

   28  • While Paxos can be described with a page of pseudo-code, our complete implementation contains several
   29  thousand lines of C++ code. The blow-up is not due simply to the fact that we used C++ instead
   30  of pseudo notation, nor because our code style may have been verbose. Converting the algorithm into
   31  a practical, production-ready system involved implementing many features and optimizations – some
   32  published in the literature and some not.

   33  • The fault-tolerant algorithms community is accustomed to proving short algorithms (one page of pseudo
   34  code) correct. This approach does not scale to a system with thousands of lines of code. To gain
   35  confidence in the "correctness" of a real system, different methods had to be used.

   36  • Fault-tolerant algorithms tolerate a limited set of carefully selected faults. However, the real world
   37  exposes software to a wide variety of failure modes, including errors in the algorithm, bugs in its

   44  This paper discusses a selection of the algorithmic and engineering challenges we encountered in moving

   47  The rest of this paper is organized as follows. The next two sections expand on the motivation for this

   50  in the literature, software engineering challenges, and unexpected failures. We conclude with measurements
   51  of our system, and some broader observations on the state of the art in our field.

   52  2 Background

   53  Chubby [1] is a fault-tolerant system at Google that provides a distributed locking mechanism and stores
   54  small files. Typically there is one Chubby instance, or "cell", per data center. Several Google systems – such
   55  as the Google Filesystem (GFS) [4] and Bigtable [2] – use Chubby for distributed coordination and to store
   56  a small amount of metadata.
   57  Chubby achieves fault-tolerance through replication. A typical Chubby cell consists of five replicas,
   58  running the same code, each running on a dedicated machine. Every Chubby object (e.g., a Chubby lock,
   59  or file) is stored as an entry in a database. It is this database that is replicated. At any one time, one of
   60  these replicas is considered to be the "master".
   61  Chubby clients (such as GFS and Bigtable) contact a Chubby cell for service. The master replica serves
   62  all Chubby requests. If a Chubby client contacts a replica that is not the master, the replica replies with
   63  the master's network address. The Chubby client may then contact the master. If the master fails, a new
   64  master is automatically elected, which will then continue to serve traffic based on the contents of its local
   65  copy of the replicated database. Thus, the replicated database ensures continuity of Chubby state across
   66  master failover.
   67  The first version of Chubby was based on a commercial, third-party, fault-tolerant database; we will
   68  refer to this database as "3DB" for the rest of this paper. This database had a history of bugs related to
   69  replication. In fact, as far as we know, the replication mechanism was not based on a proven replication
   70  algorithm and we do not know if it is correct. Given the history of problems associated with that product
   71  and the importance of Chubby, we eventually decided to replace 3DB with our own solution based on the
   72  Paxos algorithm.

   73  3 Architecture outline

   74  Figure 1 illustrates the architecture of a single Chubby replica. A fault-tolerant replicated log based on the
   75  Paxos algorithm sits at the bottom of the protocol stack. Each replica maintains a local copy of the log. The
   76  Paxos algorithm is run repeatedly as required to ensure that all replicas have identical sequences of entries
   77  in their local logs. Replicas communicate with each other through a Paxos-specific protocol.
   78  The next layer is a fault-tolerant replicated database which includes a local copy of the database at each
   79  replica. The database consists of a local snapshot and a replay-log of database operations. New database
   80  operations are submitted to the replicated log. When a database operation appears at a replica, it is applied
   81  on that replica's local database copy.
   82  Finally, Chubby uses the fault-tolerant database to store its state. Chubby clients communicate with a
   83  single Chubby replica through a Chubby-specific protocol.

   95  The replicated log does not create its own threads but can be invoked concurrently by any number of threads.
   96  This approach to threading helps in testing the system, as we will show in more detail later in the paper.

   97  4 On Paxos

   98  In this section we give an informal overview of the basic Paxos algorithm and outline how to chain together
   99  multiple executions of it (Multi-Paxos). We refer the reader to the literature for more formal descriptions
  100  and correctness proofs [8, 9, 16]. Readers who are familiar with Paxos may skip directly to the next section.

  101  4.1 Paxos Basics
  102  Paxos is a consensus algorithm executed by a set of processes, termed replicas, to agree on a single value
  103  in the presence of failures. Replicas may crash and subseqently recover. The network may drop messages

  106  between replicas. Replicas have access to persistent storage that survives crashes. Some replicas may submit
  107  values for consensus. If eventually a majority of the replicas run for long enough without crashing and there
  108  are no failures, all running replicas are guaranteed to agree on one of the values that was submitted. In our
  109  system, the value to be agreed upon is the next entry in a (replicated) log as described in the introduction.
  110  The algorithm consists of three phases, which may be repeated (because of failures):

  111  1. Elect a replica to be the coordinator.
  112  2. The coordinator selects a value and broadcasts it to all replicas in a message called the accept message.
  113  Other replicas either acknowledge this message or reject it.
  114  3. Once a majority of the replicas acknowledge the coordinator, consensus has been reached, and the
  115  coordinator broadcasts a commit message to notify replicas.

  116  To provide some intuition about how the algorithm works, consider first the case in which there is only
  117  a single coordinator and no failures. Consensus is reached once a majority of replicas receive the accept
  118  message from the coordinator and acknowledge it. Subsequently, if any minority of the replicas fail, we are
  119  still guaranteed that at least one replica will be alive that received the consensus value.
  120  In reality the coordinator may fail. Paxos does not require that only one replica act as coordinator at a
  121  time. Multiple replicas may decide to become coordinators and execute the algorithm at any time. Typically
  122  the system is engineered to limit coordinator turnover, as it can delay reaching consensus.
  123  This flexible election policy means there may be multiple replicas who simultaneously believe they are
  124  the coordinator. Further, these coordinators may select different values. Paxos ensures consensus can be
  125  reached on a single value (it can be from any coordinator) by introducing two extra mechanisms: 1) assigning
  126  an ordering to the successive coordinators; and 2) restricting each coordinator's choice in selecting a value.
  127  Ordering the coordinators allows each replica to distinguish between the current coordinator and previous
  128  coordinators. In this way, replicas can reject messages from old coordinators and prevent them from disrupting consensus once it is reached. Paxos orders the coordinators by assigning them an increasing sequence
  129  number as follows. Each replica keeps track of the most recent sequence number it has seen so far. When a
  130  replica wants to become coordinator, it generates a unique1 sequence number higher than any it has seen,

  135  and broadcasts it to all replicas in a propose message. If a majority of replicas reply and indicate they have
  136  not seen a higher sequence number, then the replica acts as a coordinator. These replies are called promise
  137  messages since replicas promise henceforth to reject messages from old coordinators. This propose/promise
  138  message exchange constitutes step 1 listed above.
  139  Once consensus is reached on a value, Paxos must force future coordinators to select that same value
  140  in order to ensure continued agreement. To guarantee this, the promise messages from replicas include the
  141  most recent value they have heard, if any, along with the sequence number of the coordinator from whom
  142  they heard it. The new coordinator chooses the value from the most recent coordinator. If none of the
  143  promise messages contain a value, the coordinator is free to choose a submitted value.
  144  The reasoning why this works is subtle, but proceeds roughly as follows. The new coordinator requires
  145  a response to the propose message from a majority of replicas. Therefore, if consensus was achieved by a
  146  previous coordinator, the new coordinator is guaranteed to hear about the value decided upon from at least
  147  one replica. By induction, that value will have the highest sequence number of all responses received, and
  148  so will be selected by the new coordinator.

  149  4.2 Multi-Paxos
  150  Practical systems use Paxos as a building block to achieve consensus on a sequence of values, such as in a
  151  replicated log. The simple way to implement this is to repeatedly execute the Paxos algorithm. We term
  152  each execution an instance of Paxos. We refer to submitting a value to Paxos (or equivalently, to the log) to
  153  mean executing an instance of Paxos while submitting that value.
  154  In Multi-Paxos some slow (lagging) replicas might not have participated in recent Paxos instances. We
  155  use a catch-up mechanism to enable lagging replicas to catch up with leading replicas.
  156  Each replica maintains a locally persistent log to record all Paxos actions. When a replica crashes and
  157  subsequently recovers, it replays the persistent log to reconstruct its state prior to crashing. Replicas also
  158  use this log when helping lagging replicas to catch up. The Paxos algorithm as described thus far requires
  159  all message senders to log their state before sending messages – thus the algorithm requires a sequence of
  160  five writes (for each of the propose, promise, accept, acknowledgment, and commit messages) to disk on its
  161  critical path. Note that all writes have to be flushed to disk immediately before the system can proceed any
  162  further. In a system where replicas are in close network proximity, disk flush time can dominate the overall
  163  latency of the implementation.
  164  There is a well-known optimization to reduce the number of messages involved by chaining together
  165  multiple Paxos instances [9]. Propose messages may be omitted if the coordinator identity does not change
  166  between instances. This does not interfere with the properties of Paxos because any replica at any time
  167  can still try to become coordinator by broadcasting a propose message with a higher sequence number. In
  168  order to avail itself of this optimization, a Multi-Paxos algorithm may be designed to pick a coordinator for
  169  long periods of time, trying not to let the coordinator change. We refer to this coordinator as the master.
  170  With this optimization, the Paxos algorithm only requires a single write to disk per Paxos instance on each
  171  replica, executed in parallel with each other. The master writes to disk immediately after sending its accept
  172  message and other replicas write to disk prior to sending their acknowledge message.
  173  In order to get additional throughput in a concurrent system, it is possible to batch a collection of values
  174  submitted by different application threads into a single Paxos instance.

  175  5 Algorithmic challenges

  176  While the core Paxos algorithm is well-described, implementing a fault-tolerant log based on it is a non-trivial
  177  endeavor. Some of the complications are due to imperfections found in the real world (such as hard disk
  178  failures, or finite resources), and some are due to additional requirements (for instance, "master leases").
  179  Many of these challenges have algorithmic solutions that are intimately connected with the core Paxos
  180  algorithm. In the following we describe a number of mechanisms that we introduced.

  182  5.1 Handling disk corruption
  183  Replicas witness disk corruption from time to time. A disk may be corrupted due to a media failure or due to
  184  an operator error (an operator may accidentally erase critical data). When a replica's disk is corrupted and
  185  it loses its persistent state, it may renege on promises it has made to other replicas in the past. This violates
  186  a key assumption in the Paxos algorithm. We use the following mechanism to address this problem [14].
  187  Disk corruptions manifest themselves in two ways. Either file(s) contents may change or file(s) may
  188  become inaccessible. To detect the former, we store the checksum of the contents of each file in the file2.
  189  The latter may be indistinguishable from a new replica with an empty disk – we detect this case by having
  190  a new replica leave a marker in GFS after start-up. If this replica ever starts again with an empty disk, it
  191  will discover the GFS marker and indicate that it has a corrupted disk.
  192  A replica with a corrupted disk rebuilds its state as follows. It participates in Paxos as a non-voting
  193  member; meaning that it uses the catch-up mechanism to catch up but does not respond with promise or
  194  acknowledgment messages. It remains in this state until it observes one complete instance of Paxos that was
  195  started after the replica started rebuilding its state. By waiting for the extra instance of Paxos, we ensure
  196  that this replica could not have reneged on an earlier promise.
  197  This mechanism enables the following optimization to improve the latency of the system. Since the
  198  system can now deal with occasional disk corruption, under some circumstances it may be acceptable not
  199  to flush writes to disk immediately3. While we have considered schemes to exploit this observation, we have
  200  not implemented them yet.

  201  5.2 Master leases
  202  When the basic Paxos algorithm is used to implement a replicated data structure, reads of the data structure
  203  require executing an instance of Paxos. This serializes the read with respect to updates and ensures that
  204  the current state is read. In particular, read operations cannot be served out of the master's copy of the
  205  data structure because it is possible that other replicas have elected another master and modified the data
  206  structure without notifying the old master. In this case, the read operation at the master runs the risk of
  207  returning stale data. Since read operations usually comprise a large fraction of all operations, serializing
  208  reads through Paxos is expensive.
  209  The workaround is to implement master leases [5] with the following semantics: as long as the master has
  210  the lease, it is guaranteed that other replicas cannot successfully submit values to Paxos. Thus a master with
  211  the lease has up-to-date information in its local data structure which can be used to serve a read operation
  212  purely locally. By making the master attempt to renew its lease before it expires we can ensure that a master
  213  has a lease most of the time. With our system, masters successfully maintain leases for several days at a
  214  time.
  215  In our implementation, all replicas implicitly grant a lease to the master of the previous Paxos instance
  216  and refuse to process Paxos messages from any other replica while the lease is held. The master maintains
  217  a shorter timeout for the lease than the replicas – this protects the system against clock drift. The master
  218  periodically submits a dummy "heartbeat" value to Paxos to refresh its lease.
  219  The Multi-Paxos optimization exhibits the following stability problem when there are intermittent network outages. When a master temporarily disconnects, Paxos will elect a new master. The new master will
  220  maintain a fixed sequence number across instances of Paxos. In the mean time, when the disconnected old
  221  master tries to run the Paxos algorithm, if it manages to connect with another replica, it may increase its
  222  sequence number. When it reconnects, it may have a higher sequence number than the new master and be
  223  able to replace the new master. Later it may disconnect again, and the cycle can repeat itself.
  224  This behavior is undesirable as Chubby master changes have a negative impact on some of its users.
  225  Furthermore, this behavior can degenerate into rapid master changes in a network with poor connectivity.

  227  handle it explicitly. Our distributed checksum mechanism, described later, may detect this type of problem.
  228  3For example, if the operating system and the hardware underlying each replica rarely fail and failures at different replicas

  231  In our implementation the master periodically boosts its sequence number by running a full round of the

  234  Note that it is possible to extend the concept of leases to all replicas. This will allow any replica with

  238  5.3 Epoch numbers
  239  Requests (by a Chubby client) submitted to a Chubby cell are directed to the current Chubby master replica.
  240  From the time when the master replica receives the request to the moment the request causes an update of
  241  the underlying database, the replica may have lost its master status. It may even have lost master status and
  242  regained it again. Chubby requires an incoming request to be aborted if mastership is lost and/or re-acquired
  243  during the handling of the request. We needed a mechanism to reliably detect master turnover and abort
  244  operations if necessary.
  245  We solved this problem by introducing a global epoch number with the following semantics. Two requests
  246  for the epoch number at the master replica receive the same value iff that replica was master continuously
  247  for the time interval between the two requests. The epoch number is stored as an entry in the database, and
  248  all database operations are made conditional on the value of the epoch number.

  249  5.4 Group membership
  250  Practical systems must be able to handle changes in the set of replicas. This is referred to as the group
  251  membership problem in the literature [3]. Some Paxos papers point out that the Paxos algorithm itself
  252  can be used to implement group membership [8]. While group membership with the core Paxos algorithm
  253  is straightforward, the exact details are non-trivial when we introduce Multi-Paxos, disk corruptions, etc.
  254  Unfortunately the literature does not spell this out, nor does it contain a proof of correctness for algorithms
  255  related to group membership changes using Paxos. We had to fill in these gaps to make group membership
  256  work in our system. The details – though relatively minor – are subtle and beyond the scope of this paper.

  257  5.5 Snapshots
  258  As described thus far, the repeated application of a consensus algorithm to create a replicated log will lead
  259  to an ever growing log. This has two problems: it requires unbounded amounts of disk space; and perhaps
  260  worse, it may result in unbounded recovery time since a recovering replica has to replay a potentially long
  261  log before it has fully caught up with other replicas. Since the log is typically a sequence of operations to
  262  be applied to some data structure, and thus implicitly (through replay) represents a persistent form of that
  263  data structure, the problem is to find an alternative persistent representation for the data structure at hand.
  264  An obvious mechanism is to persist – or snapshot – the data structure directly, at which point the log of
  265  operations leading to the current state of the data structure is no longer needed. For example, if the data
  266  structure is held in memory, we take a snapshot by serializing it on disk. If the data structure is kept on
  267  disk, a snapshot may just be an on-disk copy of it.
  268  By itself, the Paxos framework does not know anything about the data structure we are trying to
  269  replicate; its only concern is the consistency of the replicated log. It is the particular application using the
  270  Paxos framework that has all the knowledge about the replicated data structure. Thus the application must
  271  be responsible for taking snapshots. Our framework provides a mechanism that allows client applications, e.g.
  272  our fault-tolerant database, to inform the framework that a snapshot was taken; the client application is free
  273  to take a snapshot at any point. When the Paxos framework is informed about a snapshot, it will truncate
  274  its log by deleting log entries that precede the snapshot. Should the replica fail, during subsequent recovery

  277  it will simply install the latest snapshot and then replay the truncated log to rebuild its state. Snapshots
  278  are not synchronized across replicas; each replica independently decides when to create a snapshot.
  279  This mechanism appears straightforward at first and is mentioned briefly in the literature [8]. However,
  280  it introduces a fair amount of complexity into the system: the persistent state of a replica now comprises a
  281  log and a snapshot that have to be maintained consistently. The log is fully under the framework's control,
  282  while the snapshot format is application-specific. Some aspects of the snapshot machinery are of particular
  283  interest:

  284  • The snapshot and log need to be mutually consistent. Each snapshot needs to have information about
  285  its contents relative to the fault-tolerant log. In our framework we introduced the concept of a snapshot
  286  handle for this purpose. The snapshot handle contains all the Paxos-specific information related to
  287  a particular snapshot. When creating a snapshot (which is under control of the application) the
  288  corresponding snapshot handle (provided by the framework) needs to be stored by the application as
  289  well. When recovering a snapshot, the application must return the snapshot handle to the framework,
  290  which in turn will use the information in the handle to coordinate the snapshot with the log.
  291  Note that the handle is really a snapshot of the Paxos state itself. In our system, it contains the Paxos
  292  instance number corresponding to the (log) snapshot and the group membership at that point.

  293  • Taking a snapshot takes time and in some situations we cannot afford to freeze a replica's log while it
  294  is taking a snapshot. In our framework, taking a snapshot is split into three phases. First, when the
  295  client application decides to take a snapshot, it requests a snapshot handle. Next, the client application
  296  takes its snapshot. It may block the system while taking the snapshot, or – more likely – spawn a
  297  thread that takes a snapshot while the replica continues to participate in Paxos. The snapshot must
  298  correspond to the client state at the log position when the handle was obtained. Thus if the replica
  299  continues to participate in Paxos while taking a snapshot, special precautions may have to be taken to
  300  snapshot the client's data structure while it is actively updated.5 Finally, when the snapshot has been
  301  taken, the client application informs the framework about the snapshot and passes the corresponding
  302  snapshot handle. The framework then truncates the log appropriately.

  303  • Taking a snapshot may fail. Our framework only truncates the log when it is informed that a snapshot
  304  has been taken and has received the corresponding snapshot handle. Thus, as long as the client
  305  application does not inform the framework, from the framework's viewpoint, no snapshot has been
  306  taken. This allows the client application to verify a snapshot's integrity and discard it if necessary. If
  307  there is a problem with the snapshot, the client doesn't ask the framework to truncate its log. A client
  308  application may even attempt to take several snapshots at the same time using this mechanism.

  309  • While in catch-up, a replica will attempt to obtain missing log records. If it cannot obtain them
  310  (because no replica has old-enough log entries readily available), the replica will be told to obtain a
  311  snapshot from another replica. This snapshot's handle contains information about the Paxos instance
  312  up to which the snapshot captured the state. Once the snapshot has been received and installed, under
  313  most circumstances the lagging replica will be close to the leading replica. In order to completely catchup, the lagging replica asks for and receives the remaining log records from the leading replica to bring
  314  it fully up-to-date.
  315  Note that a leading replica may even create a new snapshot while a lagging replica is installing an
  316  older snapshot – in a fault-tolerant system this cannot be avoided. In this scenario, the lagging replica
  317  may not be able to obtain any outstanding log records because the snapshot provider (and any other
  318  replicas) may have moved ahead in the meantime. The lagging replica will need to obtain a more
  319  recent snapshot.

  325  Furthermore, the leading replica may fail after sending its snapshot. The catch-up mechanism must
  326  be able to recover from such problems by having the lagging replica contact another leading replica.

  327  • We needed a mechanism to locate recent snapshots. Some applications may choose to transfer snapshots
  328  directly between leading and lagging replicas while others may ask a lagging replica to look up a
  329  snapshot on GFS. We implemented a general mechanism that allows an application to pass snapshot
  330  location information between leading and lagging replicas.

  331  5.6 Database transactions
  332  The database requirements imposed by Chubby are simple: the database needs to store key-value pairs (with
  333  keys and values being arbitrary strings), and support common operations such as insert, delete, lookup,
  334  an atomic compare and swap (cas), and iteration over all entries. We implemented a log-structured design
  335  using a snapshot of the full database, and a log of database operations to be applied to that snapshot. The
  336  log of operations is the Paxos log. The implementation periodically takes a snapshot of the database state
  337  and truncates the log accordingly.
  338  The cas operation needed to be atomic with respect to other database operations (potentially issued by
  339  a different replica). This was easily achieved by submitting all cas-related data as a single "value" to Paxos.
  340  We realized that we could extend this mechanism to provide transaction-style support without having to
  341  implement true database transactions. We describe our solution in more detail because we believe it to be
  342  useful in other contexts.
  343  Our implementation hinges around a powerful primitive which we call MultiOp. All other database
  344  operations except for iteration are implemented as a single call to MultiOp. A MultiOp is applied atomically
  345  and consists of three components:

  346  1. A list of tests called guard. Each test in guard checks a single entry in the database. It may check
  347  for the absence or presence of a value, or compare with a given value. Two different tests in the guard
  348  may apply to the same or different entries in the database. All tests in the guard are applied and
  349  MultiOp returns the results. If all tests are true, MultiOp executes t op (see item 2 below), otherwise
  350  it executes f op (see item 3 below).
  351  2. A list of database operations called t op. Each operation in the list is either an insert, delete, or
  352  lookup operation, and applies to a single database entry. Two different operations in the list may apply
  353  to the same or different entries in the database. These operations are executed6 if guard evaluates to
  354  true.
  355  3. A list of database operations called f op. Like t op, but executed if guard evaluates to false.

  356  Late in our development (and after we had implemented the database and MultiOp), we realized that we
  357  also needed epoch numbers to implement database operations for Chubby. With this additional requirement,
  358  all Chubby operations became associated with an epoch number and were required to fail if the Paxos epoch
  359  number changed. MultiOp proved useful in accomodating this new requirement. After we incorporated the
  360  Paxos epoch as a database entry, we were able to modify all previous calls to our database to include an
  361  additional guard to check for the epoch number.

  362  6 Software Engineering

  363  Fault-tolerant systems are expected to run continuously for long periods of time. Users are much less likely
  364  to tolerate bugs than in other systems. For instance, a layout bug exhibited by a document editor may be
  365  annoying to a user, but often it is possible to "work around" the issue, even though the bug is really at the
  366  6Each MultiOp operation is serialized atomically with respect to other operations. The individual operations in the list are
  367  executed sequentially on the database.

  369  core of what the software is supposed to do. A bug of similar gravity in a fault-tolerant system may make
  370  the system unusable.
  371  We adopted several software engineering methods to give us confidence in the robustness of our implementation. We describe some of the methods we used in this section.

  372  6.1 Expressing the algorithm effectively
  373  Fault-tolerant algorithms are notoriously hard to express correctly, even as pseudo-code. This problem is
  374  worse when the code for such an algorithm is intermingled with all the other code that goes into building
  375  a complete system. It becomes harder to see the core algorithm, to reason about it, or to debug it when a
  376  bug is present. It also makes it difficult to change the core algorithm in response to a requirement change.
  377  We addressed this problem by coding the core algorithm as two explicit state machines. For that purpose,
  378  we designed a simple state machine specification language and built a compiler to translate such specifications
  379  into C++. The language was designed to be terse so that a full algorithm can be rendered on a single screen.
  380  As an additional benefit, the state machine compiler also automatically generates code to log state transitions
  381  and measure code coverage to assist in debugging and testing.
  382  We believe that choosing a specification language makes it easier to reason about and modify our state
  383  machines than an explicitly coded implementation that is intermingled with the rest of the system. This is
  384  illustrated by the following experience. Towards the end of our development of the fault-tolerant log, we had
  385  to make a fundamental change in our group membership algorithm. Prior to this change, a replica roughly
  386  went through three states. Initially it waited to join the group, then it joined the group, and finally it left
  387  the group. Once a replica left the group, it was not allowed to rejoin the group. We felt this approach was
  388  best because an intermittently failing replica would not be able to join the group and disrupt it for long.
  389  Intermittent failure turned out to be more common than originally anticipated because normal replicas
  390  exhibit intermittent failures from time to time. Thus, we needed to change the algorithm to have two states.
  391  Either a replica was in the group or it was out. A replica could switch between these two states often during
  392  the lifetime of the system. It took us about one hour to make this change and three days to modify our tests
  393  accordingly. Had we intermingled our state machines with the rest of the system, this change would have
  394  been more difficult to make.

  395  6.2 Runtime consistency checking
  396  The chance for inconsistencies increases with the size of the code base, the duration of a project, and the
  397  number of people working simultaneously on the same code. We used various active self-checking mechanisms
  398  such as the liberal use of assert statements, and explicit verification code that tests data structures for
  399  consistency.
  400  For example, we used the following database consistency check. The master periodically submits a
  401  checksum request to the database log. On receipt of this request, each replica computes a checksum of its
  402  local database7. Since the Paxos log serializes all operations identically on all replicas, we expect all replicas
  403  to compute the same checksum. After the master completes a checksum computation, it sends its checksum
  404  to all replicas which compare the master's checksum with their computed checksum.
  405  We have had three database inconsistency incidents thus far:

  406  • The first incident was due to an operator error.
  407  • We have not found an explanation for the second incident. On replaying the faulty replica's log we
  408  found that it was consistent with the other replicas. Thus it is possible that this problem was caused
  409  by a random hardware memory corruption.

  410  • We suspect the third was due to an illegal memory access from errant code in the included codebase
  411  (which is of considerable size). To protect against this possibility in the future, we maintain a second
  412  database of checksums and double-check every database access against the database of checksums.
  413  7We use a shadow datastructure to handle database operations concurrently with the checksum computation.

  416  6.3 Testing
  417  Given the current state of the art, it is unrealistic to prove a real system such as ours correct. To achieve
  418  robustness, the best practical solution in addition to meticulous software engineering is to test a system
  419  thoroughly. Our system was designed to be testable from the onset and now contains an extensive suite of
  420  tests. In this section we describe two tests that take the system through a long sequence of random failures
  421  and verify that it behaves as expected. Both tests can run in one of two modes:

  422  1. Safety mode. In this mode, the test verifies that the system is consistent. However, the system is
  423  not required to make any progress. For example, it is acceptable for an operation to fail to complete
  424  or to report that the system is unavailable.
  425  2. Liveness mode. In this mode, the test verifies that the system is consistent and is making progress.
  426  All operations are expected to complete and the system is required to be consistent.

  427  Our tests start in safety mode and inject random failures into the system. After running for a predetermined period of time, we stop injecting failures and give the system time to fully recover. Then we switch
  428  the test to liveness mode. The purpose for the liveness test is to verify that the system does not deadlock
  429  after a sequence of failures.
  430  One of our tests verifies the fault-tolerant log. It simulates a distributed system consisting of a random
  431  number of replicas and takes our fault-tolerant log through a random sequence of network outages, message
  432  delays, timeouts, process crashes and recoveries, file corruptions, schedule interleavings, etc. We wanted this
  433  test to be repeatable to aid in debugging. To this end, we use a random number generator to determine
  434  the schedule of failures. The seed for the random number generator is given at the beginning of the test
  435  run. We ensure that two test runs with the same random number seed are identical by running the test
  436  in a single thread to remove unwanted non-determinism from multi-threading. This is possible because the
  437  fault-tolerant log does not create its own threads and can run in a single-threaded environment (even though
  438  it normally runs in a multi-threaded environment).
  439  Each test execution reports success or failure. If a test fails, we rerun that test with the failing random
  440  number seed and with detailed logging turned on in a debugger to determine what went wrong. This is
  441  possible because these tests are repeatable.
  442  This test proved useful in finding various subtle protocol errors, including errors in our group membership
  443  implementation, and our modifications to deal with corrupted disks. In order to measure the strength of
  444  this test, we left some protocol bugs found during code and design reviews in the system, and verified that
  445  our test system detected these bugs. After a number of bug fixes, the test became very stable. In order to
  446  improve its bug yield, we started running this test on a farm of several hundred Google machines at a time.
  447  We found additional bugs, some of which took weeks of simulated execution time (at extremely high failure
  448  rates) to find.
  449  Another test verifies robustness of the new Chubby system against lower-level system and hardware
  450  failures. We implemented several hooks in our fault-tolerant log to inject failures. The test randomly
  451  invokes these hooks and verifies that higher levels of the system can cope. Our hooks can be used to crash a
  452  replica, disconnect it from other replicas for a period of time or force a replica to pretend that it is no longer
  453  the master. This test found five subtle bugs in Chubby related to master failover in its first two weeks. In
  454  the same vein, we built a filesystem with hooks to programmatically inject failures and are using it to test
  455  our ability to deal with filesystem failures.
  456  In closing we point out a challenge that we faced in testing our system for which we have no systematic
  457  solution. By their very nature, fault-tolerant systems try to mask problems. Thus they can mask bugs or
  458  configuration problems while insidiously lowering their own fault-tolerance. For example, we have observed
  459  the following scenario. We once started a system with five replicas, but misspelled the name of one of the
  460  replicas in the initial group. The system appeared to run correctly as the four correctly configured replicas

  461  11
=== PAGE 12 ===

  462  were able to make progress. Further, the fifth replica continunously ran in catch-up mode8 and therefore
  463  appeared to run correctly as well. However in this configuration the system only tolerates one faulty replica
  464  instead of the expected two. We now have processes in place to detect this particular type of problem. We
  465  have no way of knowing if there are other bugs/misconfigurations that are masked by fault-tolerance.

  466  6.4 Concurrency
  467  At the onset of the project we were concerned about the problem of testing concurrent fault-tolerant code. In
  468  particular, we wanted our tests to be repeatable. As described earlier, our fault-tolerant log doesn't contain
  469  any of its own threads (even though it can handle concurrent requests on different threads). Threading is
  470  introduced at the edges of the code – where we receive calls from the networking layer. By making our tests
  471  repeatable, we were able to hunt down several obscure protocol errors during testing.
  472  As the project progressed, we had to make several subsystems more concurrent than we had intended
  473  and sacrifice repeatability. Chubby is multi-threaded at its core, thus we cannot run repeatable tests against
  474  the complete system. Next we had to make our database multi-threaded so it could take snapshots, compute
  475  checksums and process iterators while concurrently serving database requests. Finally, we were forced to
  476  make the code that handles the local copy of the log multi-threaded as well (the exact reason why is beyond
  477  the scope of this paper).
  478  In summary, we believe that we set ourselves the right goals for repeatability of executions by constraining
  479  concurrency. Unfortunately, as the product needs grew we were unable to adhere to these goals.

  480  7 Unexpected failures

  481  So far, our system has logged well over 100 machine years of execution in production. In this period we have
  482  witnessed the following unexpected failure scenarios:

  483  • Our first release shipped with ten times the number of worker threads as the original Chubby system.
  484  We hoped this change would enable us to handle more requests. Unfortunately, under load, the worker
  485  threads ended up starving some other key threads and caused our system to time out frequently. This
  486  resulted in rapid master failover, followed by en-masse migrations of large numbers of clients to the
  487  new master which caused the new master to be overwhelmed, followed by additional master failovers,
  488  and so on.
  489  When this problem first appeared, the precise cause was unknown and we had to protect ourselves from
  490  a potentially dangerous bug in our system. We decided to err on the side of caution and to rollback
  491  our system to the old version of Chubby (based on 3DB) in one of our data centers. At that point, the
  492  rollback mechanism was not properly documented (because we never expected to use it), its use was
  493  non-intuitive, the operator performing the roll-back had no experience with it, and when the rollback
  494  was performed, no member of the development team was present. As a result, an old snapshot was
  495  accidentally used for the rollback. By the time we discovered the error, we had lost 15 hours of data
  496  and several key datasets had to be rebuilt.

  497  • When we tried to upgrade this Chubby cell again a few months later, our upgrade script failed because
  498  we had omitted to delete files generated by the failed upgrade from the past. The cell ended up running
  499  with a months-old snapshot for a few minutes before we discovered the problem. This caused us to
  500  lose about 30 minutes of data. Fortunately all of Chubby's clients recovered from this outage.

  501  • A few months after our initial release, we realized that the semantics provided by our database were
  502  different from what Chubby expected. If Chubby submitted an operation to the database, and the
  503  database lost its master status, Chubby expected the operation to fail. With our system, a replica
  504  8In our implementation, a replica that is not (yet) a group member runs in catch-up mode to stay up-to-date. This allows
  505  us to keep a future group member "warm" so it can become an active member immediately after joining the group.

  507  could be re-installed as master during the database operation and the operation could succeed. The fix
  508  required a substantial rework of the integration layer between Chubby and our framework (we needed
  509  to implement epoch numbers). MultiOp proved to be helpful in solving this unexpected problem – an
  510  indication that MultiOp is a powerful primitive.

  511  • As mentioned before, on three occasions we discovered that one of the database replicas was different
  512  from the others in that Chubby cell. We found this problem because our system periodically takes
  513  checksums of all replicas and then compares them.

  514  • Our upgrade script which is responsible for migrating cells from the 3DB version of Chubby to the
  515  Paxos version has failed several times for a variety of reasons. For example, it once failed because a
  516  basic Google program was not installed on one of our cells.

  517  • We have encountered failures due to bugs in the underlying operating system. For example in our
  518  version of the Linux 2.4 kernel, when we try to flush a small file to disk, the call can hang for a long
  519  time if there are a lot of buffered writes to other files. This happens immediately after we write a
  520  database snapshot to disk. In this case, we observed that it could take several seconds for the kernel
  521  to flush an unrelated small write to the Paxos log. Our workaround is to write all large files in small
  522  chunks, with a flush to disk after each small chunk. While this hurts the performance of the write
  523  slightly, it protects the more critical log writes from unexpected delays.
  524  A small number of failures in 100 machine years would be considered excellent behavior for most production systems. However, we consider the current failure rate too high for Chubby and we have determined
  525  that we need to reduce it further.
  526  Three of the failures occurred during upgrade (or rollback). Every time we encountered a problem
  527  during upgrade, we updated our upgrade script accordingly. Once a cell is upgraded, this type of failure will
  528  disappear.
  529  Two of the failures were from bugs that have since been fixed. To reduce the probability of other bugs,
  530  we continue to improve and run the Chubby verification test outlined earlier.
  531  Two of our unexpected problems relate to operator error during rollout of a new release and caused us
  532  to lose data. At Google, the day-to-day monitoring and management of our systems is done by system
  533  operators. While they are very competent, they are usually not part of the development team that built the
  534  system, and therefore not familiar with its intricate details. This may lead to the occasional operator error
  535  in unforseen situations. We now rely on carefully written and well-tested scripts to automate rollout and
  536  minimize operator involvement. As a result our most recent major release of Chubby was rolled out across
  537  hundreds of machines without incident, while serving life traffic.
  538  One of the failures was due to memory corruption. Because our system is log-structured and maintains
  539  several days of log data and snapshots, it was possible to replay the database upto the exact point at which
  540  the problem appears. We were able to verify that our logs were correct and conclude that the memory
  541  corruption occurred from errant software or due to hardware problems. We added additional checksum data
  542  to detect this type of problem in the future and will crash a replica when it detects this problem.

  579  9 Summary and open problems

  580  We have described our implementation of a fault-tolerant database, based on the Paxos consensus algorithm.
  581  Despite the large body of literature in the field, algorithms dating back more then 15 years, and experience
  582  of our team (one of us has designed a similar system before and the others have built other types of complex
  583  systems in the past), it was significantly harder to build this system then originally anticipated. We attribute
  584  this to several shortcomings in the field:

  585  • There are significant gaps between the description of the Paxos algorithm and the needs of a real-world
  586  system. In order to build a real-world system, an expert needs to use numerous ideas scattered in
  587  the literature and make several relatively small protocol extensions. The cumulative effort will be
  588  substantial and the final system will be based on an unproven protocol.

  589  • The fault-tolerance computing community has not developed the tools to make it easy to implement
  590  their algorithms.

  591  • The fault-tolerance computing community has not paid enough attention to testing, a key ingredient
  592  for building fault-tolerant systems.

  593  As a result, the core algorithms work remains relatively theoretical and is not as accessible to a larger
  594  computing community as it could be. We believe that in order to make a greater impact, researchers in the
  595  field should focus on addressing these shortcomings.

  597  In contrast, consider the field of compiler construction. Though concepts in that field are complex, they
  598  have been made accessible to a wide audience. Industrial-strength parsing tools such yacc [6] appeared not
  599  too long after the theory of parsing was well-understood. Not only are there now many front-end tools such as
  600  ANTLR [15] or CoCo/R [13]; but there are also tree-rewriting tools helping with optimizations and instruction
  601  selection, assemblers helping with binary code generation, and so forth. Thus, in this area of software
  602  engineering, an entire family of tools has emerged, making the construction of a compiler significantly easier
  603  or at least less error-prone. Disciplines within the field of compiler construction, such as parsing, which
  604  were once at the cutting edge of research, are now considered "solved" and are routinely taught at the
  605  undergraduate level in many schools.
  606  It appears that the fault-tolerant distributed computing community has not developed the tools and
  607  know-how to close the gaps between theory and practice with the same vigor as for instance the compiler
  608  community. Our experience suggests that these gaps are non-trivial and that they merit attention by the
  609  research community.
