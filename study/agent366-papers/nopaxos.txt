   22  Abstract

   23  Distributed applications use replication, implemented by
   24  protocols like Paxos, to ensure data availability and transparently mask server failures. This paper presents a new
   25  approach to achieving replication in the data center without the performance cost of traditional methods. Our work
   26  carefully divides replication responsibility between the
   27  network and protocol layers. The network orders requests
   28  but does not ensure reliable delivery – using a new primitive we call ordered unreliable multicast (OUM). Implementing this primitive can be achieved with near-zero-cost
   29  in the data center. Our new replication protocol, Network-
   30  Ordered Paxos (NOPaxos), exploits network ordering to
   31  provide strongly consistent replication without coordination. The resulting system not only outperforms both
   32  latency- and throughput-optimized protocols on their respective metrics, but also yields throughput within 2%
   33  and latency within 16 µs of an unreplicated system – providing replication without the performance cost.

   34  1 Introduction
   35  Server failures are a fact of life for data center applications. To guarantee that critical services always remain
   36  available, today's applications rely on fault-tolerance techniques like state machine replication. These systems use
   37  application-level consensus protocols such as Paxos to
   38  ensure the consistency of replicas' states. Unfortunately,
   39  these protocols require expensive coordination on every
   40  request, imposing a substantial latency penalty and limiting system scalability. This paper demonstrates that replication in the data center need not impose such a cost by
   41  introducing a new replication protocol with performance
   42  within 2% of an unreplicated system.
   43  It is well known that the communication model fundamentally affects the difficulty of consensus. Completely
   44  asynchronous and unordered networks require the full
   45  complexity of Paxos; if a network could provide a totally
   46  ordered atomic broadcast primitive, ensuring replica consistency would become a trivial matter. Yet this idea has
   47  yielded few gains in practice since traditional orderedmulticast systems are themselves equivalent to consensus;
   48  they simply move the same coordination expense to a
   49  different layer.
   50  We show that a new division of responsibility between

   54  replication overhead. Our key insight is that the communication layer should provide a new ordered unreliable
   55  multicast (OUM) primitive – where all receivers are guaranteed to process multicast messages in the same order,
   56  but messages may be lost. This model is weak enough to
   57  be implemented efficiently, yet strong enough to dramatically reduce the costs of a replication protocol.
   58  The ordered unreliable multicast model enables our

   63  We describe several ways to build the OUM communications layer, all of which offer net performance benefits

   78  consistency and providing maximum performance.

   82  enough to be useful to application-level protocols yet
   83  weak enough to be implemented efficiently.

   85  in the data center by presenting three implementations:

  101  2 Separating Ordering from Reliable De-

  102  livery in State Machine Replication
  103  We consider the problem of state machine replication [56].
  104  Replication, used throughout data center applications,
  105  keeps key services consistent and available despite the inevitability of failures. For example, Google's Chubby [11]
  106  and Apache ZooKeeper [24] use replication to build a
  107  highly available lock service that is widely used to coordinate access to shared resources and configuration information. It is also used in many storage services to prevent
  108  system outages or data loss [8, 16, 54].
  109  Correctness for state machine replication requires a system to behave as a linearizable [23] entity. Assuming that
  110  the application code at the replicas is deterministic, establishing a single totally ordered set of operations ensures
  111  that all replicas remain in a consistent state. We divide
  112  this into two separate properties:
  113  1. Ordering: If some replica processes request a before
  114  b, no replica processes b before a.
  115  2. Reliable Delivery: Every request submitted by a client
  116  is either processed by all replicas or none.
  117  Our research examines the question: Can the responsibility for either of these properties be moved from the
  118  application layer into the network?
  119  State of the art. Traditional state machine replication uses consensus protocol – e.g., Paxos [33, 34] or
  120  Viewstamped Replication [42, 48] – to achieve agreement on operation order. Most deployments of Paxosbased replicated systems use the Multi-Paxos optimization [34] (equivalent to Viewstamped Replication), where

  121  one replica is the designated leader and assigns an order
  122  to requests. Its normal operation proceeds in four phases:
  123  clients submit requests to the leader; the leader assigns a
  124  sequence number and notifies the other replicas; a majority of other replicas acknowledge; and the leader executes
  125  the request and notifies the client.
  126  These protocols are designed for an asynchronous network, where there are no guarantees that packets will be
  127  received in a timely manner, in any particular order, or
  128  even delivered at all. As a result, the application-level
  129  protocol assumes responsibility for both ordering and
  130  reliability.
  131  The case for ordering without reliable delivery. If
  132  the network itself provided stronger guarantees, the full
  133  complexity of Paxos-style replication would be unnecessary. At one extreme, an atomic broadcast primitive (i.e., a
  134  virtually synchronous model) [6, 27] ensures both reliable
  135  delivery and consistent ordering, which makes replication
  136  trivial. Unfortunately, implementing atomic broadcast is a
  137  problem equivalent to consensus [14] and incurs the same
  138  costs, merely in a different layer.
  139  This paper envisions a middle ground: an ordered but
  140  unreliable network. We show that a new division of responsibility – providing ordering in the network layer but
  141  leaving reliability to the replication protocol – leads to
  142  a more efficient whole. What makes this possible is that
  143  an ordered unreliable multicast primitive can be implemented efficiently and easily in the network, yet fundamentally simplifies the task of the replication layer.
  144  We note that achieving reliable delivery despite the
  145  range of possible failures is a formidable task, and the
  146  end-to-end principle suggests that it is best left to the application [15, 55]. However, ordering without a guarantee of
  147  reliability permits a straightforward, efficient implementation: assigning sequence numbers to messages and then
  148  discarding those that arrive out of sequence number order.
  149  We show in §3 that this approach can be implemented at
  150  almost no cost in data center network hardware.
  151  At the same time, providing an ordering guarantee simplifies the replication layer dramatically. Rather than agree
  152  on which request should be executed next, it needs to ensure only all-or-nothing delivery of each message. We
  153  show that this enables a simpler replication protocol that
  154  can execute operations without inter-replica coordination
  155  in the common case when messages are not lost, yet can
  156  recover quickly from lost messages.
  157  Prior work has considered an asynchronous network
  158  that provides ordering and reliability in the common case
  159  but does not guarantee either. Fast Paxos [36] and related
  160  systems [29, 45, 50] provide agreement in one fewer message delay when requests usually arrive at replicas in the
  161  same order, but they require more replicas and/or larger
  162  quorum sizes. Speculative Paxos [53] takes this further by
  163  having replicas speculatively execute operations without

  183  3 Ordered Unreliable Multicast
  184  We have argued for a separation of concerns between ordering and reliable delivery. Towards this end, we seek to
  185  design an ordered but unreliable network. In this section,
  186  we precisely define the properties that this network provides, and show how it can be realized efficiently using
  187  in-network processing.
  188  We are not the first to argue for a network with ordered
  189  delivery semantics. Prior work has observed that some
  190  networks often deliver requests to replicas in the same
  191  order [50,58], that data center networks can be engineered
  192  to support a multicast primitive that has this property [53],
  193  and that it is possible to use this fact to design protocols
  194  that are more efficient in the common case [29, 36, 53].
  195  We contribute by demonstrating that it is possible to build
  196  a network with ordering guarantees rather than probabilistic or best-effort properties. As we show in §5, doing
  197  so can support simpler and more efficient protocols.
  198  Figure 1 shows the architecture of an OUM/NOPaxos
  199  deployment. All components reside in a single data center. OUM is implemented by components in the network along with a library, libOUM, that runs on senders
  200  and receivers. NOPaxos is a replication system that uses
  201  libOUM; clients use libOUM to send messages, and replicas use libOUM to receive clients' messages.

  202  3.1 Ordered Unreliable Multicast Properties
  203  We begin by describing the basic primitive provided by
  204  our networking layer: ordered unreliable multicast. More
  205  specifically, our model is an asynchronous, unreliable
  206  network that supports ordered multicast with multicast
  207  drop detection. These properties are defined as follows:

  208  • Asynchrony: There is no bound on the latency of message delivery.

  209  • Unreliability: The network does not guarantee that
  210  any message will ever be delivered to any recipient.

  241  3.2 OUM Sessions and the libOUM API

  245  OUM model described above. An OUM group is a set of
  246  receivers and is identified by an IP address. We explain
  247  group membership changes in §5.2.5.
  248  libOUM introduces an additional concept, sessions.
  249  For each OUM group, there are one or more sessions,
  250  which are intervals during which the OUM guarantees

  292  4 OUM Design and Implementation
  293  We implement OUM in the context of a single data center
  294  network. The basic design is straightforward: the network routes all packets destined for a given OUM group
  295  through a single sequencer, a low-latency device that
  296  serves one purpose: to add a sequence number to each
  297  packet before forwarding it to its destination. Since all
  298  packets have been marked with a sequence number, the

  299  libOUM library can ensure ordering by discarding messages that are received out of order and detect and report
  300  dropped messages by noticing gaps in the sequence number.
  301  Achieving this design poses three challenges. First, the
  302  network must serialize all requests through the sequencer;
  303  we use software-defined networking (SDN) to provide this
  304  network serialization (§4.1). Second, we must implement
  305  a sequencer capable of high throughput and low latency.
  306  We present three such implementations in §4.2: a zeroadditional-latency implementation for programmable data
  307  center switches, a middlebox-like prototype using a network processor, and a pure-software implementation. Finally, the system must remain robust to failures of network
  308  components, including the sequencer (§4.3).

  309  4.1 Network Serialization
  310  The first aspect of our design is network serialization,
  311  where all OUM packets for a particular group are routed
  312  through a sequencer on the common path. Network serialization was previously used to implement a best-effort
  313  multicast [53]; we adapt that design here.
  314  Our design targets a data center that uses softwaredefined networking, as is common today. Data center
  315  networks are engineered networks constructed with a particular topology – generally some variant of a multi-rooted
  316  tree. A traditional design calls for a three-level tree topology where many top-of-rack switches, each connecting
  317  to a few dozen server, are interconnected via aggregation
  318  switches that themselves connect through core switches.
  319  More sophisticated topologies, such as fat-tree or Clos
  320  networks [1, 22, 43, 46] extend this basic design to support
  321  large numbers of physical machines using many commodity switches and often provide full bisection bandwidth.
  322  Figure 3 shows the testbed we use, implementing a fattree network [1].
  323  Software-defined networking additionally allows the
  324  data center network to be managed by a central controller.
  325  This controller can install custom forwarding, filtering,
  326  and rewriting rules in switches. The current generation of
  327  SDN switches, e.g., OpenFlow [44], allow these rules to
  328  be installed at a per-flow granularity, matching on a fixed
  329  set of packet headers.
  330  To implement network serialization, we assign each
  331  OUM group a distinct address in the data center network
  332  that senders can use to address messages to the group. The
  333  SDN controller installs forwarding rules for this address
  334  that route messages through the sequencer, then to group
  335  members.
  336  To do this, the controller must select a sequencer for
  337  each group. In the most efficient design, switches themselves are used as sequencers (§4.2.1). In this case, the
  338  controller selects a switch that is a common ancestor of all
  339  destination nodes in the tree hierarchy to avoid increasing

  361  4.2 Implementing the Sequencer
  362  The sequencer plays a simple but critical role: assigning a
  363  sequence number to each message destined for a particular
  364  OUM group, and writing that sequence number into the
  365  packet header. This establishes a total order over packets
  366  and is the key element that elevates our design from a
  367  best-effort ordering property to an ordering guarantee.
  368  Even if packets are dropped (e.g., due to congestion or
  369  link failures) or reordered (e.g., due to multipath effects)
  370  in the network, receivers can use the sequence numbers
  371  to ensure that they process packets in order and deliver
  372  drop notifications for missing packets.
  373  Sequencers maintain one counter per OUM group. For
  374  every packet destined for that group, they increment the
  375  counter and write it into a designated field in the packet
  376  header. The counter must be incremented by 1 on each
  377  packet (as opposed to a timestamp, which monotonically
  378  increases but may have gaps). This counter lets libOUM
  379  return DROP-NOTIFICATIONs when it notices gaps in the
  380  sequence numbers of incoming messages. Sequencers
  381  also maintain and write into each packet the OUM session number that is used to handle sequencer failures; we
  382  describe its use in §4.3.
  383  Our sequencer design is general; we discuss three possible implementations here. The most efficient one targets

  405  4.2.1 In-Switch Sequencing
  406  Ideally, switches themselves could serve as sequencers.
  407  The benefit of doing so is latency: packets could be sequenced by one of the switches through which they already transit, rather than having to be redirected to a
  408  dedicated device. Moreover, switching hardware is highly
  409  optimized for low-latency packet processing, unlike endhosts.
  410  Using a switch as a sequencer is made possible by the
  411  increasing ability of data center switches to perform flexible, per-packet computations. An emerging class of switch
  412  architectures – such as Reconfigurable Match Tables [10],
  413  Intel's FlexPipe [49], and Cavium's XPliant [59] – allow
  414  the switch's behavior to be controlled on a per-packet
  415  granularity, supporting the parsing and matching of arbitrary packet fields, rewriting of packet contents, and
  416  maintaining of small amounts of state between packets.
  417  Exposed through high-level languages like P4 [9], this
  418  increased flexibility lets us consider network switches
  419  as not simply forwarding elements, but as devices with
  420  computational ability.
  421  We implemented our switch sequencing functionality
  422  in the P4 language, which allows it to be compiled and
  423  deployed to upcoming programmable switches as well as
  424  software switches. Our implementation uses the reconfigurable parser capabilities of these switches to define a
  425  custom packet header that includes the OUM sequence
  426  and session numbers. It uses stateful memory (register
  427  arrays) to store the current sequence number for every
  428  OUM group and increments it on each packet. Complete
  429  NOPaxos P4 code is available in [39].

  436  e.g., in the Arista 7150 switch) can modify packets to include the egress timestamp with zero latency cost [2, 49].
  437  We note that a network switch provides orders-ofmagnitude lower latency and greater reliability [21] than
  438  an end-host. Today's fastest cut-through switches can consistently process packets in approximately 300 ns [2],
  439  while a typical Linux server has median latency in the 10–
  440  100 µs range and 99.9th-percentile latency over 5 ms [40].
  441  This trend seems unlikely to change: even with highperformance server operating systems [3,52], NIC latency
  442  remains an important factor [20]. At the same time, the
  443  limited computational model of the switch requires a careful partitioning of functionality between the network and
  444  application. The OUM model offers such a design.
  445  4.2.2 Hardware Middlebox Prototype Sequencing
  446  Because available switches do not provide the necessary
  447  flexibility to run P4 programs, we implemented a prototype using existing OpenFlow switches and a network
  448  processor.
  449  This prototype is part of the testbed that we use to evaluate our OUM model and its uses for distributed protocols.
  450  This testbed simulates the 12-switch, 3-layer fat-tree network configuration depicted in Figure 3. We implemented
  451  it on three physical switches by using VLANs and appropriate OpenFlow forwarding rules to emulate separate
  452  virtual switches: two HP 6600 switches implement the
  453  ToR and aggregation tiers, and one Arista 7050S switch
  454  implements the core tier.
  455  We implemented the sequencer as a form of middlebox
  456  using a Cavium Octeon II CN68XX network processor.
  457  This device contains 32 MIPS64 cores and supports 10
  458  Gb/s Ethernet I/O. Users can customize network functionality by loading C binaries that match, route, drop
  459  or modify packets going through the processor. Onboard
  460  DRAM maintains per-group state. We attached the middlebox to the root switches and installed OpenFlow rules
  461  to redirect OUM packets to the middlebox.
  462  This implementation does not provide latency as low
  463  as the switch-based sequencer; routing traffic through
  464  the network processor adds latency. We measured this
  465  latency to be 8 µs in the median case and 16 µs in the
  466  99th percentile. This remains considerably lower than
  467  implementing packet processing in an end-host.
  468  4.2.3 End-host Sequencing
  469  Finally, we also implemented the sequencing functionality on a conventional server. While this incurs higher

  470  latency, it allows the OUM abstraction to be implemented
  471  without any specialized hardware. Nevertheless, using a
  472  dedicated host for network-level sequencing can still provide throughput, if not latency, benefits as we demonstrate
  473  in §6. We implemented a simple Linux program that uses
  474  raw sockets to access packet headers.
  475  4.2.4 Sequencer Scalability
  476  Since all OUM packets for a particular group go through
  477  the sequencer, a valid concern is whether the sequencer
  478  will become the performance bottleneck. Switches and
  479  network processors are designed to process packets at line
  480  rate and thus will not become the bottleneck for a single
  481  OUM group (group receivers are already limited by the
  482  link bandwidth). Previous work [28] has demonstrated
  483  that an end-host sequencer using RDMA can process
  484  close to 100 million requests per second, many more
  485  than any single OUM group can process. We note that
  486  different OUM groups need not share a sequencer, and
  487  therefore deployment of multiple OUM groups can scale
  488  horizontally.

  489  4.3 Fault Tolerance
  490  Designating a sequencer and placing it on the common
  491  path for all messages to a particular group introduces an
  492  obvious challenge: what if it fails or becomes unreachable? If link failures or failures of other switches render
  493  the sequencer unreachable, local rerouting mechanisms
  494  may be able to identify an alternate path [43]. However, if
  495  the sequencer itself fails, or local rerouting is not possible,
  496  replacing the sequencer becomes necessary.
  497  In our design, the network controller monitors the sequencer's availability. If it fails or no longer has a path
  498  to all OUM group members, the controller selects a different switch. It reconfigures the network to use this new
  499  sequencer by updating routes in other switches. During
  500  the reconfiguration period, multicast messages may not
  501  be delivered. However, failures of root switches happen
  502  infrequently [21], and rerouting can be completed within
  503  a few milliseconds [43], so this should not significantly
  504  affect system availability.
  505  We must also ensure that the ordering guarantee of
  506  multicast messages is robust to sequencer failures. This
  507  requires the continuous, ordered assignment of sequence
  508  numbers even when the network controller fails over to a
  509  new sequencer.
  510  To address this, we introduce a unique, monotonically
  511  increasing session number, incremented each time sequencer failover occurs. When the controller detects a
  512  sequencer failure, it updates the forwarding rules and
  513  contacts the new sequencer to set its local session number to the appropriate value. As a result, the total order of messages follows the lexicographical order of the
  514  ⟨session-number, sequence-number⟩ tuple, and clients

  535  5 NOPaxos

  536  NOPaxos, or Network-Ordered Paxos, is a new replication
  537  protocol which leverages the Ordered Unreliable Multicast sessions provided by the network layer.

  538  5.1 Model
  539  NOPaxos replicas communicate over an asynchronous
  540  network that provides OUM sessions (via libOUM).
  541  NOPaxos requires the network to provide ordered but
  542  unreliable delivery of multicast messages within a session. In the normal case, these messages are delivered sequentially and are not dropped; however, it remains robust to dropped packets (presented as DROPNOTIFICATION through libOUM). NOPaxos is also robust
  543  to SESSION-TERMINATED notifications that occur if the
  544  sequencer fails. These network anomalies do not affect
  545  NOPaxos's safety guarantees, and we discuss how they
  546  affect NOPaxos's performance in §6.
  547  NOPaxos assumes a crash failure model. It uses 2f +1
  548  replicas, where f replicas are allowed to fail. However, in
  549  the presence of more than f failures, the system still guarantees safety. Furthermore, NOPaxos guarantees safety
  550  even in an asynchronous network with no bound on message latency (provided the OUM guarantees continue to
  551  hold).
  552  NOPaxos provides linearizability of client requests. It
  553  provides at-most-once semantics using the standard mechanism of maintaining a table of the most recent request
  554  from each client [42].

  556  5.2 Protocol
  557  Overview. NOPaxos is built on top of the guarantees
  558  of the OUM network primitive. During a single OUM
  559  session, REQUESTs broadcast to the replicas are totally
  560  ordered but can be dropped. As a result, the replicas have
  561  to agree only on which REQUESTs to execute and which
  562  to permanently ignore, a simpler task than agreeing on
  563  the order of requests. Conceptually, this is equivalent to
  564  running multiple rounds of binary consensus. However,
  565  NOPaxos must explicitly run this consensus only when
  566  DROP-NOTIFICATIONs are received. To switch OUM sessions (in the case of sequencer failure), the replicas must
  567  agree on the contents of their shared log before they start
  568  listening to the new session.
  569  To these ends, NOPaxos uses a view-based approach:
  570  each view has a single OUM session-num and a single
  571  replica acting as leader. The leader executes requests and
  572  drives the agreement to skip a dropped request. That is,
  573  it decides which of the sequencer's REQUESTs to ignore
  574  and treat as NO-OPs. The view ID is a tuple ⟨leader-num,
  575  session-num⟩. Here, leader-num is incremented each time
  576  a new leader is needed; the current leader of any view is
  577  leader-num (mod n); and session-num is the latest session ID from libOUM. View IDs in NOPaxos are partially
  578  ordered.2 However, the IDs of all views that successfully
  579  start will be comparable.
  580  In the normal case, the replicas receive a REQUEST
  581  from libOUM. The replicas then reply to the client, the
  582  leader replying with the result of the REQUEST, so the
  583  client's REQUEST is processed in only a single round-trip.
  584  NOPaxos uses a single round-trip in the normal case because, like many speculative protocols, the client checks
  585  the durability of requests. However, unlike most speculative protocols, NOPaxos clients have a guarantee regarding ordering of operations; they need only check that the
  586  operation was received.
  587  When replicas receive a DROP-NOTIFICATION from
  588  libOUM, they first try to recover the missing REQUEST
  589  from each other. Failing that, the leader initiates a round of
  590  agreement to commit a NO-OP into the corresponding slot
  591  in the log. Finally, NOPaxos uses a view change protocol
  592  to handle leader failures and OUM session termination
  593  while maintaining consistency.

  595  • Normal Operations (§5.2.1): NOPaxos processes
  596  client REQUESTs in a single round-trip in the normal
  597  case.

  598  • Gap Agreement (§5.2.2): NOPaxos ensures correctness
  599  in the face of DROP-NOTIFICATIONs by having the

  627  5.2.1 Normal Operations
  628  In the normal case when replicas receive REQUESTs instead of DROP-NOTIFICATIONs, client requests are committed and executed in a single phase. Clients broadcast ⟨REQUEST, op, request-id⟩ to all replicas through
  629  libOUM, where op is the operation they want to execute,
  630  and request-id is a unique id used to match requests and
  631  their responses.
  632  When each replica receives the client's REQUEST, it
  633  increments session-msg-num and appends op to the log. If
  634  the replica is the leader of the current view, it executes the
  635  op (or looks up the previous result if it is a duplicate of a
  636  completed request). Each replica then replies to the client
  637  with ⟨REPLY, view-id, log-slot-num, request-id, result⟩,
  638  where log-slot-num is the index of op in the log. If the
  639  replica is the leader, it includes the result of the operation;
  640  NULL otherwise.
  641  The client waits for REPLYs to the REQUEST with
  642  matching view-ids and log-slot-nums from f +1 replicas, where one of those replicas is the leader of the view.
  643  This indicates that the request will remain persistent even
  644  across view changes. If the client does not receive the
  645  required REPLYs within a timeout, it retries the request.

  646  5.2.2 Gap Agreement
  647  NOPaxos replicas always process operations in order.
  648  When a replica receives a DROP-NOTIFICATION from
  649  libOUM (and increments its session-msg-num), it must
  650  either recover the contents of the missing request or prevent it from succeeding before moving on to subsequent
  651  requests. Non-leader replicas do this by contacting the
  652  leader for a copy of the request. If the leader itself receives a DROP-NOTIFICATION, it coordinates to commit
  653  a NO-OP operation in place of that request:

  654  1. If the leader receives a DROP-NOTIFICATION, it inserts a NO-OP into its log and sends a ⟨GAP-COMMIT,
  655  log-slot⟩ to the other replicas, where log-slot is the slot
  656  into which the NO-OP was inserted.

  657  2. When a non-leader replica receives the GAP-COMMIT
  658  and has filled all log slots up to the one specified by the
  659  leader, it inserts a NO-OP into its log at the specified location3 (possibly overwriting a REQUEST) and replies
  660  to the leader with a ⟨GAP-COMMIT-REP, log-slot⟩.

  661  3. The leader waits for f GAP-COMMIT-REPs (retrying if
  662  necessary).

  663  Clients need not be notified explicitly when a NO-OP
  664  has been committed in place of one of their requests.
  665  They simply retry their request after failing to receive a
  666  quorum of responses. Note that the retried operation will
  667  be considered a new request and will have a new slot in the
  668  replicas' logs. Replicas identify duplicate client requests
  669  by checking if they have processed another request with
  670  the same client-id and request-id, as is commonly done
  671  in other protocols.
  672  This protocol ensures correctness because clients do
  673  not consider an operation completed until they receive
  674  a response from the leader, so the leader can propose a
  675  NO-OP regardless of whether the other replicas received
  676  the REQUEST. However, before proceeding to the next
  677  sequence number, the leader must ensure that a majority
  678  of replicas have learned of its decision to commit a NOOP. When combined with the view change protocol, this
  679  ensures that the decision persists even if the leader fails.
  680  As an optimization, the leader can first try to contact
  681  the other replicas to obtain a copy of the REQUEST and initiate the gap commit protocol only if no replicas respond
  682  before a timeout. While not necessary for correctness, this
  683  reduces the number of NO-OPs.

  690  5.2.3 View Change
  691  During each view, a NOPaxos group has a particular
  692  leader and OUM session number. NOPaxos must perform
  693  view changes to ensure progress in two cases: (1) when
  694  the leader is suspected of having failed (e.g, by failing to
  695  respond to pings), or (2) when a replica detects the end
  696  of an OUM session. To successfully replace the leader
  697  or move to a new OUM session, NOPaxos runs a view
  698  change protocol. This protocol ensures that all successful
  699  operations from the old view are carried over into the new
  700  view and that all replicas start the new view in a consistent
  701  state.
  702  NOPaxos's view change protocol resembles that used
  703  in Viewstamped Replication [42]. The principal difference is that NOPaxos views serve two purposes, and so
  704  NOPaxos view IDs are therefore a tuple of ⟨leader-num,
  705  session-num⟩ rather than a simple integer. A view change
  706  can increment either one. However, NOPaxos ensures
  707  that each replica's leader-num and session-num never go
  708  backwards. This maintains a total order over all views
  709  that successfully start.

  710  1. A replica initiates a view change when: (1) it suspects that the leader in its current view has failed;
  711  (2) it receives a SESSION-TERMINATED notification
  712  from libOUM; or (3) it receives a VIEW-CHANGE or
  713  VIEW-CHANGE-REQ message from another replica
  714  with a higher leader-num or session-num. In all cases,
  715  the replica appropriately increments the leader-num
  716  and/or session-num in its view-id and sets its status
  717  to ViewChange. If the replica incremented its sessionnum, it resets its session-msg-num to 0.

  718  It then sends ⟨VIEW-CHANGE-REQ, view-id⟩ to the
  719  other replicas and ⟨VIEW-CHANGE, view-id, v0,
  720  session-msg-num, log⟩ to the leader of the new view,
  721  where v0 is the view ID of the last view in which its
  722  status was Normal. While in ViewChange status, the
  723  replica ignores all replica-to-replica messages (except
  724  START-VIEW, VIEW-CHANGE, and VIEW-CHANGEREQ).
  725  If the replica ever times out waiting for the view change
  726  to complete, it simply rebroadcasts the VIEW-CHANGE
  727  and VIEW-CHANGE-REQ messages.

  728  2. When the leader for the new view receives f +1 VIEWCHANGE messages (including one from itself) with
  729  matching view-ids, it performs the following steps:

  730  • The leader merges the logs from the most recent

  749  5.2.4 Synchronization
  750  During any view, only the leader executes operations and
  751  provides results. Thus, all successful client REQUESTs are
  752  committed on a stable log at the leader, which contains
  753  only persistent client REQUESTs. In contrast, non-leader
  754  replicas might have speculative operations throughout
  755  their logs. If the leader crashes, the view change protocol
  756  ensures that the new leader first recreates the stable log
  757  of successful operations. However, it must then execute
  758  all operations before it can process new ones. While this
  759  protocol is correct, it is clearly inefficient.
  760  Therefore, as an optimization, NOPaxos periodically
  761  executes a synchronization protocol in the background.
  762  This protocol ensures that all other replicas learn which
  763  operations have successfully completed and which the
  764  leader has replaced with NO-OPs. That is, synchronization
  765  ensures that all replicas' logs are stable up to their syncpoint and that they can safely execute all REQUESTs up
  766  to this point in the background.
  767  For brevity, we omit the details of this protocol. See
  768  [39] for the full specification.

  788  5.3 Benefits of NOPaxos
  789  NOPaxos achieves the theoretical minimum latency and
  790  maximum throughput: it can execute operations in one
  791  round-trip from the client to the replicas and does not
  792  require replicas to coordinate on each request. By relying
  793  on the network to stamp requests with sequence numbers,
  794  it requires replies only from a simple majority of replicas and uses a cheaper and rollback-free mechanism to
  795  correctly account for network anomalies.
  796  The OUM session guarantees mean that the replicas
  797  already agree on the ordering of all operations. As a consequence, clients need not wait for a superquorum of
  798  replicas to reply, as in Fast Paxos and Speculative Paxos
  799  (and as is required by any protocol that provides fewer
  800  message delays than Paxos in an asynchronous, unordered
  801  network [37]). In NOPaxos, a simple majority of replicas
  802  suffices to guarantee the durability of a REQUEST in the
  803  replicas' shared log.
  804  Additionally, the OUM guarantees enable NOPaxos
  805  to avoid expensive mechanisms needed to detect when
  806  replicas are not in the same state, such as using hashing to
  807  detect conflicting logs from replicas. To keep the replicas'
  808  logs consistent, the leader need only coordinate with the
  809  other replicas when it receives DROP-NOTIFICATIONs.
  810  Committing a NO-OP takes but a single round-trip and
  811  requires no expensive reconciliation protocol.
  812  NOPaxos also avoids rollback, which is usually necessary in speculative protocols. It does so not by coordinating on every operation, as in non-speculative protocols,
  813  but by having only the leader execute operations. Nonleader replicas do not execute requests during normal
  814  operations (except, as an optimization, when the synchronization protocol indicates it is safe to do so), so they
  815  need not rollback. The leader executes operations speculatively, without coordinating with the other replicas,
  816  but clients do not accept a leader's response unless it is
  817  supported by matching responses from f other replicas.
  818  The only rare case when a replica will execute an operation that is not eventually committed is if a functioning
  819  leader is incorrectly replaced through a view change, losing some operations it executed. Because this case is rare,
  820  it is reasonable to handle it by having the ousted leader

  821  transfer application state from another replica, rather than
  822  application-level rollback.
  823  Finally, unlike many replication protocols, NOPaxos
  824  replicas send and receive a constant number of messages
  825  for each REQUEST in the normal case, irrespective of the
  826  total number of replicas. This means that NOPaxos can be
  827  deployed with an increasing number of replicas without
  828  the typical performance degradation, allowing for greater
  829  fault-tolerance. §6.3 demonstrates that NOPaxos achieves
  830  the same throughput regardless of the number of replicas.

  831  5.4 Correctness
  832  NOPaxos guarantees linearizability: that operations submitted by multiple concurrent clients appear to be executed by a single, correct machine. In a sense, correctness in NOPaxos is a much simpler property than in
  833  other systems, such as Paxos and Viewstamped Replication [33, 48], because the replicas need not agree on the
  834  order of the REQUESTs they execute. Since the REQUEST
  835  order is already provided by the guarantees of OUM sessions, the replicas must only agree on which REQUESTs
  836  to execute and which REQUESTs to drop.

  838  NOPaxos protocol. For a full, detailed proof, see [39].

  840  NOPaxos protocol.

  844  the client receives the f +1 suitable REPLYs. We say a
  845  log is stable in view v if it will be a prefix of the log of
  846  every replica in views higher than v.

  864  NO-OPs is stable.
  865  Next, every view that starts (i.e., f +1 replicas receive
  866  the START-VIEW and enter Normal status) trivially starts
  867  with a log containing only committed REQUESTs and NOOPs. Replicas send REPLYs to a REQUEST only after all
  868  log slots before the REQUEST's slot have been filled with
  869  REQUESTs or NO-OPs; further, a replica inserts a NO-OP
  870  only if the leader already inserted that NO-OP. Therefore,
  871  if a REQUEST is committed, all previous REQUESTs and
  872  NO-OPs in the leader's log were already committed.
  873  This means that any REQUEST that is successful in
  874  a view must have been appended to a stable log at the
  875  leader, and the resulting log must also be stable, showing
  876  (1). To see that (2) is true, notice that the last entry in
  877  the combined log formed during a view change and the
  878  session-msg-num are taken from the same replica(s) and
  879  therefore must be consistent.
  880  NOPaxos also guarantees liveness given a sufficient
  881  amount of time during which the following properties
  882  hold: the network over which the replicas communicate
  883  is fair-lossy; there is some bound on the relative processing speeds of replicas; there is a quorum of replicas that stays up; there is a replica that stays up that no
  884  replica suspects of having failed; all replicas correctly suspect crashed nodes of having failed; no replica receives
  885  a DROP-NOTIFICATION or SESSION-TERMINATED from
  886  libOUM; and clients' REQUESTs eventually get delivered
  887  through libOUM.

  888  6 Evaluation

  889  We implemented the NOPaxos protocol in approximately
  890  5,000 lines of C++ code. We ran our experiments using
  891  the 3-level fat-tree network testbed shown in Figure 3.
  892  All clients and replicas ran on servers with 2.5 GHz Intel
  893  Xeon E5-2680 processors and 64GB of RAM. All experiments used five replicas (thereby tolerating two replica
  894  failures).
  895  To evaluate the performance of NOPaxos, we compared it to four other replication protocols: Paxos, Fast
  896  Paxos, Paxos with batching, and Speculative Paxos; we
  897  also evaluated it against an unreplicated system that provides no fault tolerance. Like NOPaxos, the clients in
  898  both Speculative Paxos and Fast Paxos multicast their
  899  requests to the replicas through a root serialization switch
  900  to minimize message reordering. Requests from NOPaxos
  901  clients, however, are also routed through the Cavium processor to be stamped with the sequencer's OUM session
  902  number and current request sequence number. For the
  903  batching variant of Paxos, we used a sliding-window technique where the system adaptively adjusts the batch size,
  904  keeping at least one batch in progress at all times; this
  905  approach reduces latency at low load while still providing
  906  throughput benefits at high load [13].

  937  6.1 Latency vs. Throughput
  938  To compare the latency and throughput of NOPaxos and
  939  the other four protocols, we ran each system with an
  940  increasing number of concurrent closed-loop clients. Figure 5 shows results of this experiment. NOPaxos achieves
  941  a much higher maximum throughput than Paxos and Fast
  942  Paxos (370% increases in both cases) without any additional latency. The leaders in both Paxos and Fast Paxos
  943  send and receive more messages than the other replicas,
  944  and the leaders' message processing quickly becomes the
  945  bottleneck of these systems. NOPaxos has no such inefficiency. NOPaxos also achieves higher throughput than
  946  Speculative Paxos (24% increase) because Speculative
  947  Paxos requires replicas to compute hashes of their logs
  948  for each client request.
  949  Figure 5 also shows that NOPaxos has lower latency
  950  (111 µs) than Paxos (240 µs) and Fast Paxos (193 µs) because NOPaxos requires fewer message delays in the normal case. Speculative Paxos also has higher latency than
  951  NOPaxos because clients must wait for a superquorum of
  952  replica replies instead of NOPaxos's simple quorum.
  953  Batching improves Paxos's throughput by reducing the
  954  number of messages sent by the leader. Paxos with batching is able to reach a maximum throughput equivalent
  955  to Speculative Paxos. However, batching also increases
  956  the latency of Paxos (385 µs at low load and 907 µs
  957  at maximum throughput). NOPaxos attains both higher
  958  throughput and lower latency than Paxos with batching.

  985  6.2 Resilience to Network Anomalies
  986  To test the performance of NOPaxos in an unreliable
  987  network, we randomly dropped a fraction of all packets.
  988  Figure 7 shows the maximum throughput of the five protocols and the unreplicated system with an increasing
  989  packet drop rate. Paxos's and Fast Paxos's throughput
  990  do not decrease significantly, while Paxos with batching
  991  shows a larger drop in throughput due to frequent state
  992  transfers. However, the throughput of Speculative Paxos
  993  drops substantially after 0.5% packet dropping, demonstrating NOPaxos's largest advantage over Speculative
  994  Paxos. When 1% of packets are dropped, Speculative
  995  Paxos's maximum throughput falls to that of Paxos. As
  996  discussed in §5.3, Speculative Paxos performs an expensive reconciliation protocol when messages are dropped
  997  and replica states diverge. NOPaxos is much more resilient to packet drops and reorderings. It achieves higher
  998  throughput than Paxos with batching and much higher
  999  throughput than Speculative Paxos at high drop rates.
 1000  Even with a 1% message drop rate, NOPaxos's throughput
 1001  does not drop significantly. Indeed, NOPaxos maintains
 1002  throughput roughly equivalent to an unreplicated system,
 1003  demonstrating its strong resilience to network anomalies.

 1065  7 Related Work
 1066  Our work draws on techniques from consensus protocol
 1067  design as well as network-level processing mechanisms.
 1068  Consensus protocols Many protocols have been proposed for the equivalent problems of consensus, state
 1069  machine replication, and atomic broadcast. Most closely
 1070  related is a line of work on achieving better performance
 1071  when requests typically arrive at replicas in the same order,
 1072  including Fast Paxos [36], Speculative Paxos [53], and
 1073  Optimistic Atomic Broadcast [29, 50, 51]; Zyzzyva [32]
 1074  applies a similar idea in the context of Byzantine fault
 1075  tolerant replication. These protocols can reduce consensus latency in a manner similar to NOPaxos. However,
 1076  because requests are not guaranteed to arrive in the same
 1077  order, they incur extra complexity and require supermajority quorum sizes to complete a request (either 2/3 or
 1078  3/4 of replicas rather than a simple majority). This difference is fundamental: the possibility of conflicting orders
 1079  requires either an extra message round or a larger quorum
 1080  size [37].
 1081  Another line of work aims to reduce latency and improve throughput by avoiding coordination for operations that are commutative or otherwise need not be ordered [12, 35, 45, 60]; this requires application support to
 1082  identify commutative operations. NOPaxos avoids coordination for all operations.
 1083  Ordered Unreliable Multicast is related to a long line
 1084  of work on totally ordered broadcast primitives, usually
 1085  in the context of group communication systems [6, 7].
 1086  Years ago, a great debate raged in the SOSP community
 1087  about the effectiveness and limits of this causal and totally
 1088  ordered communication support (CATOCS) [5, 15]. Our
 1089  work draws inspiration from both sides of this debate, but
 1090  occupies a new point in the design space by splitting the

 1091  USENIX Association

 1092  responsibility between an ordered but unreliable communications layer and an application-level reliability layer. In
 1093  particular, the choice to leave reliability to the application
 1094  is inspired by the end-to-end argument [15, 55].
 1095  Network-level processing NOPaxos takes advantage
 1096  of flexible network processing to implement the OUM
 1097  model. Many designs have been proposed for flexible
 1098  processing, including fully flexible, software-based designs like Click [30] and others based on network processors [57] or FPGA platforms [47]. At the other extreme,
 1099  existing software defined networking mechanisms like
 1100  OpenFlow [44] can easily achieve line-rate performance
 1101  in commodity hardware implementations but lack the flexibility to implement our multicast primitive. We use the P4
 1102  language [9], which supports several high-performance
 1103  hardware designs like Reconfigurable Match Tables [10].
 1104  These processing elements have generally been used for
 1105  classic networking tasks like congestion control or queue
 1106  management. A notable exception is SwitchKV [41],
 1107  which uses OpenFlow switches for content-based routing
 1108  and load balancing in key-value stores.
 1109  ...and their intersection Recent work on Speculative Paxos and Mostly-Ordered Multicast proposes codesigning network primitives and consensus protocols
 1110  to achieve faster performance. Our work takes the next
 1111  step in this direction. While Speculative Paxos assumes
 1112  only a best-effort ordering property, NOPaxos requires
 1113  an ordering guarantee. Achieving this guarantee requires
 1114  more sophisticated network support made possible with
 1115  a programmable data plane (Speculative Paxos's Mostly-
 1116  Ordered Multicast requires only OpenFlow support).
 1117  However, as discussed in §5.3, NOPaxos achieves a simpler and more robust protocol as a result, avoiding the
 1118  need for superquorums and speculation.
 1119  A concurrent effort, NetPaxos [18], also explores ways
 1120  to use the network layer to improve the performance of
 1121  a replication protocol. That work proposes moving the
 1122  Paxos logic into switches, with one switch serving as
 1123  a coordinator and others as Paxos acceptors. This logic
 1124  can also be implemented using P4 [17]. However, as the
 1125  authors note, this approach requires the switches to implement substantial parts of the logic, including storing
 1126  potentially large amounts of state (the results of each
 1127  consensus instance). Our work takes a more practical approach by splitting the responsibility between the OUM
 1128  network model, which can be readily implemented, and
 1129  the NOPaxos consensus protocol.
 1130  Other related work uses hardware acceleration to speed
 1131  communication between nodes in a distributed system.
 1132  FaRM [19] uses RDMA to bypass the kernel and minimize CPU involvement in remote memory accesses. Consensus in a Box [25] implements a standard atomic broadcast protocol entirely on FPGAs. NOPaxos provides more
=== PAGE 15 ===

 1133  flexible deployment options. However, its protocol could
 1134  be integrated with RDMA or other kernel-bypass networking for faster replica performance.

 1135  8 Conclusions
 1136  We presented a new approach to high-performance, faulttolerant replication, one based on dividing the responsibility for consistency between the network layer and
 1137  the replication protocol. In our approach, the network
 1138  is responsible for ordering, while the replication protocol ensures reliable delivery. "Splitting the atom" in this
 1139  way yields dramatic performance gains: network-level
 1140  ordering, while readily achievable, supports NOPaxos, a
 1141  simpler replication protocol that avoids coordination in
 1142  most cases. The resulting system outperforms state-ofthe-art replication protocols on latency, throughput, and
 1143  application-level metrics, demonstrating the power of this
 1144  approach. More significantly, it achieves both throughput
 1145  and latency equivalent to an unreplicated system, proving
 1146  that replication does not have to come with a performance
 1147  cost.
