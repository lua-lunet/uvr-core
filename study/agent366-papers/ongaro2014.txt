   15  Abstract
   16  Raft is a consensus algorithm for managing a replicated
   17  log. It produces a result equivalent to (multi-)Paxos, and
   18  it is as efficient as Paxos, but its structure is different
   19  from Paxos; this makes Raft more understandable than
   20  Paxos and also provides a better foundation for building practical systems. In order to enhance understandability, Raft separates the key elements of consensus, such as
   21  leader election, log replication, and safety, and it enforces
   22  a stronger degree of coherency to reduce the number of
   23  states that must be considered. Results from a user study
   24  demonstrate that Raft is easier for students to learn than
   25  Paxos. Raft also includes a new mechanism for changing
   26  the cluster membership, which uses overlapping majorities to guarantee safety.

   27  1 Introduction
   28  Consensus algorithms allow a collection of machines
   29  to work as a coherent group that can survive the failures of some of its members. Because of this, they play a
   30  key role in building reliable large-scale software systems.
   31  Paxos [13, 14] has dominated the discussion of consensus algorithms over the last decade: most implementations
   32  of consensus are based on Paxos or influenced by it, and
   33  Paxos has become the primary vehicle used to teach students about consensus.
   34  Unfortunately, Paxos is quite difficult to understand, in
   35  spite of numerous attempts to make it more approachable.
   36  Furthermore, its architecture requires complex changes
   37  to support practical systems. As a result, both system
   38  builders and students struggle with Paxos.
   39  After struggling with Paxos ourselves, we set out to
   40  find a new consensus algorithm that could provide a better foundation for system building and education. Our approach was unusual in that our primary goal was understandability: could we define a consensus algorithm for
   41  practical systems and describe it in a way that is significantly easier to learn than Paxos? Furthermore, we wanted
   42  the algorithm to facilitate the development of intuitions
   43  that are essential for system builders. It was important not
   44  just for the algorithm to work, but for it to be obvious why
   45  it works.
   46  The result of this work is a consensus algorithm called
   47  Raft. In designing Raft we applied specific techniques to
   48  improve understandability, including decomposition (Raft
   49  separates leader election, log replication, and safety) and
   50  state space reduction (relative to Paxos, Raft reduces the
   51  degree of nondeterminism and the ways servers can be inconsistent with each other). A user study with 43 students
   52  at two universities shows that Raft is significantly easier

   53  USENIX Association

   54  to understand than Paxos: after learning both algorithms,
   55  33 of these students were able to answer questions about
   56  Raft better than questions about Paxos.
   57  Raft is similar in many ways to existing consensus algorithms (most notably, Oki and Liskov's Viewstamped
   58  Replication [27, 20]), but it has several novel features:
   59  • Strong leader: Raft uses a stronger form of leadership
   60  than other consensus algorithms. For example, log entries only flow from the leader to other servers. This
   61  simplifies the management of the replicated log and
   62  makes Raft easier to understand.
   63  • Leader election: Raft uses randomized timers to elect
   64  leaders. This adds only a small amount of mechanism
   65  to the heartbeats already required for any consensus algorithm, while resolving conflicts simply and rapidly.
   66  • Membership changes: Raft's mechanism for changing
   67  the set of servers in the cluster uses a new joint consensus approach where the majorities of two different configurations overlap during transitions. This allows the
   68  cluster to continue operating normally during configuration changes.
   69  We believe that Raft is superior to Paxos and other consensus algorithms, both for educational purposes and as a
   70  foundation for implementation. It is simpler and more understandable than other algorithms; it is described completely enough to meet the needs of a practical system;
   71  it has several open-source implementations and is used
   72  by several companies; its safety properties have been formally specified and proven; and its efficiency is comparable to other algorithms.
   73  The remainder of the paper introduces the replicated
   74  state machine problem (Section 2), discusses the strengths
   75  and weaknesses of Paxos (Section 3), describes our general approach to understandability (Section 4), presents
   76  the Raft consensus algorithm (Sections 5–7), evaluates
   77  Raft (Section 8), and discusses related work (Section 9).
   78  A few elements of the Raft algorithm have been omitted
   79  here because of space limitations, but they are available in
   80  an extended technical report [29]. The additional material
   81  describes how clients interact with the system, and how
   82  space in the Raft log can be reclaimed.

   83  2 Replicated state machines
   84  Consensus algorithms typically arise in the context of
   85  replicated state machines [33]. In this approach, state machines on a collection of servers compute identical copies
   86  of the same state and can continue operating even if some
   87  of the servers are down. Replicated state machines are
   88  used to solve a variety of fault tolerance problems in distributed systems. For example, large-scale systems that

   97  Replicated state machines are typically implemented

   99  stores a log containing a series of commands, which its
  100  state machine executes in order. Each log contains the
  101  same commands in the same order, so each state machine processes the same sequence of commands. Since
  102  the state machines are deterministic, each computes the
  103  same state and the same sequence of outputs.
  104  Keeping the replicated log consistent is the job of the
  105  consensus algorithm. The consensus module on a server
  106  receives commands from clients and adds them to its log.
  107  It communicates with the consensus modules on other
  108  servers to ensure that every log eventually contains the
  109  same requests in the same order, even if some servers fail.
  110  Once commands are properly replicated, each server's
  111  state machine processes them in log order, and the outputs are returned to clients. As a result, the servers appear
  112  to form a single, highly reliable state machine.
  113  Consensus algorithms for practical systems typically
  114  have the following properties:
  115  • They ensure safety (never returning an incorrect result)
  116  under all non-Byzantine conditions, including network
  117  delays, partitions, and packet loss, duplication, and reordering.
  118  • They are fully functional (available) as long as any majority of the servers are operational and can communicate with each other and with clients. Thus, a typical
  119  cluster of five servers can tolerate the failure of any two
  120  servers. Servers are assumed to fail by stopping; they
  121  may later recover from state on stable storage and rejoin the cluster.
  122  • They do not depend on timing to ensure the consistency
  123  of the logs: faulty clocks and extreme message delays
  124  can, at worst, cause availability problems.

  130  3 What's wrong with Paxos?
  131  Over the last ten years, Leslie Lamport's Paxos protocol [13] has become almost synonymous with consensus:
  132  it is the protocol most commonly taught in courses, and
  133  most implementations of consensus use it as a starting
  134  point. Paxos first defines a protocol capable of reaching
  135  agreement on a single decision, such as a single replicated
  136  log entry. We refer to this subset as single-decree Paxos.
  137  Paxos then combines multiple instances of this protocol to
  138  facilitate a series of decisions such as a log (multi-Paxos).
  139  Paxos ensures both safety and liveness, and it supports
  140  changes in cluster membership. Its correctness has been
  141  proven, and it is efficient in the normal case.
  142  Unfortunately, Paxos has two significant drawbacks.
  143  The first drawback is that Paxos is exceptionally difficult to understand. The full explanation [13] is notoriously opaque; few people succeed in understanding it, and
  144  only with great effort. As a result, there have been several
  145  attempts to explain Paxos in simpler terms [14, 18, 19].
  146  These explanations focus on the single-decree subset, yet
  147  they are still challenging. In an informal survey of attendees at NSDI 2012, we found few people who were comfortable with Paxos, even among seasoned researchers.
  148  We struggled with Paxos ourselves; we were not able to
  149  understand the complete protocol until after reading several simplified explanations and designing our own alternative protocol, a process that took almost a year.
  150  We hypothesize that Paxos' opaqueness derives from
  151  its choice of the single-decree subset as its foundation.
  152  Single-decree Paxos is dense and subtle: it is divided into
  153  two stages that do not have simple intuitive explanations
  154  and cannot be understood independently. Because of this,
  155  it is difficult to develop intuitions about why the singledecree protocol works. The composition rules for multi-
  156  Paxos add significant additional complexity and subtlety.
  157  We believe that the overall problem of reaching consensus
  158  on multiple decisions (i.e., a log instead of a single entry)
  159  can be decomposed in other ways that are more direct and
  160  obvious.
  161  The second problem with Paxos is that it does not provide a good foundation for building practical implementations. One reason is that there is no widely agreedupon algorithm for multi-Paxos. Lamport's descriptions
  162  are mostly about single-decree Paxos; he sketched possible approaches to multi-Paxos, but many details are missing. There have been several attempts to flesh out and optimize Paxos, such as [24], [35], and [11], but these differ
  163  from each other and from Lamport's sketches. Systems
  164  such as Chubby [4] have implemented Paxos-like algorithms, but in most cases their details have not been pub-

  190  experiment.

  191  4 Designing for understandability
  192  We had several goals in designing Raft: it must provide
  193  a complete and practical foundation for system building,
  194  so that it significantly reduces the amount of design work
  195  required of developers; it must be safe under all conditions
  196  and available under typical operating conditions; and it
  197  must be efficient for common operations. But our most
  198  important goal—and most difficult challenge—was understandability. It must be possible for a large audience to
  199  understand the algorithm comfortably. In addition, it must
  200  be possible to develop intuitions about the algorithm, so
  201  that system builders can make the extensions that are inevitable in real-world implementations.
  202  There were numerous points in the design of Raft
  203  where we had to choose among alternative approaches.
  204  In these situations we evaluated the alternatives based on

  205  USENIX Association

  206  understandability: how hard is it to explain each alternative (for example, how complex is its state space, and does
  207  it have subtle implications?), and how easy will it be for a
  208  reader to completely understand the approach and its implications?
  209  We recognize that there is a high degree of subjectivity in such analysis; nonetheless, we used two techniques
  210  that are generally applicable. The first technique is the
  211  well-known approach of problem decomposition: wherever possible, we divided problems into separate pieces
  212  that could be solved, explained, and understood relatively
  213  independently. For example, in Raft we separated leader
  214  election, log replication, safety, and membership changes.
  215  Our second approach was to simplify the state space
  216  by reducing the number of states to consider, making the
  217  system more coherent and eliminating nondeterminism
  218  where possible. Specifically, logs are not allowed to have
  219  holes, and Raft limits the ways in which logs can become
  220  inconsistent with each other. Although in most cases we
  221  tried to eliminate nondeterminism, there are some situations where nondeterminism actually improves understandability. In particular, randomized approaches introduce nondeterminism, but they tend to reduce the state
  222  space by handling all possible choices in a similar fashion
  223  ("choose any; it doesn't matter"). We used randomization
  224  to simplify the Raft leader election algorithm.

  225  5 The Raft consensus algorithm
  226  Raft is an algorithm for managing a replicated log of
  227  the form described in Section 2. Figure 2 summarizes the
  228  algorithm in condensed form for reference, and Figure 3
  229  lists key properties of the algorithm; the elements of these
  230  figures are discussed piecewise over the rest of this section.
  231  Raft implements consensus by first electing a distinguished leader, then giving the leader complete responsibility for managing the replicated log. The leader accepts
  232  log entries from clients, replicates them on other servers,
  233  and tells servers when it is safe to apply log entries to
  234  their state machines. Having a leader simplifies the management of the replicated log. For example, the leader can
  235  decide where to place new entries in the log without consulting other servers, and data flows in a simple fashion
  236  from the leader to other servers. A leader can fail or become disconnected from the other servers, in which case
  237  a new leader is elected.

  239  • Leader election: a new leader must be chosen when an
  240  existing leader fails (Section 5.2).
  241  • Log replication: the leader must accept log entries
  242  from clients and replicate them across the cluster, forcing the other logs to agree with its own (Section 5.3).

  374  5.1 Raft basics
  375  A Raft cluster contains several servers; five is a typical
  376  number, which allows the system to tolerate two failures.
  377  At any given time each server is in one of three states:
  378  leader, follower, or candidate. In normal operation there
  379  is exactly one leader and all of the other servers are followers. Followers are passive: they issue no requests on
  380  their own but simply respond to requests from leaders
  381  and candidates. The leader handles all client requests (if
  382  a client contacts a follower, the follower redirects it to the
  383  leader). The third state, candidate, is used to elect a new
  384  leader as described in Section 5.2. Figure 4 shows the
  385  states and their transitions; the transitions are discussed
  386  below.
  387  Raft divides time into terms of arbitrary length, as
  388  shown in Figure 5. Terms are numbered with consecutive
  389  integers. Each term begins with an election, in which one
  390  or more candidates attempt to become leader as described
  391  in Section 5.2. If a candidate wins the election, then it
  392  serves as leader for the rest of the term. In some situations
  393  an election will result in a split vote. In this case the term
  394  will end with no leader; a new term (with a new election)
  395  will begin shortly. Raft ensures that there is at most one
  396  leader in a given term.
  397  Different servers may observe the transitions between
  398  terms at different times, and in some situations a server
  399  may not observe an election or even entire terms. Terms

  428  5.2 Leader election
  429  Raft uses a heartbeat mechanism to trigger leader election. When servers start up, they begin as followers. A
  430  server remains in follower state as long as it receives valid
  431  RPCs from a leader or candidate. Leaders send periodic
  432  heartbeats (AppendEntries RPCs that carry no log entries)
  433  to all followers in order to maintain their authority. If a
  434  follower receives no communication over a period of time
  435  called the election timeout, then it assumes there is no viable leader and begins an election to choose a new leader.

  500  5.3 Log replication
  501  Once a leader has been elected, it begins servicing
  502  client requests. Each client request contains a command to
  503  be executed by the replicated state machines. The leader
  504  appends the command to its log as a new entry, then issues AppendEntries RPCs in parallel to each of the other
  505  servers to replicate the entry. When the entry has been
  506  safely replicated (as described below), the leader applies
  507  the entry to its state machine and returns the result of that
  508  execution to the client. If followers crash or run slowly,
  509  or if network packets are lost, the leader retries Append-
  510  Entries RPCs indefinitely (even after it has responded to
  511  the client) until all followers eventually store all log entries.
  512  Logs are organized as shown in Figure 6. Each log entry stores a state machine command along with the term
  513  number when the entry was received by the leader. The
  514  term numbers in log entries are used to detect inconsistencies between logs and to ensure some of the properties
  515  in Figure 3. Each log entry also has an integer index identifying its position in the log.
  516  The leader decides when it is safe to apply a log entry to the state machines; such an entry is called committed. Raft guarantees that committed entries are durable
  517  and will eventually be executed by all of the available
  518  state machines. A log entry is committed once the leader
  519  that created the entry has replicated it on a majority of
  520  the servers (e.g., entry 7 in Figure 6). This also commits

  536  of ensuring safety. Raft maintains the following properties, which together constitute the Log Matching Property
  537  in Figure 3:
  538  • If two entries in different logs have the same index and
  539  term, then they store the same command.
  540  • If two entries in different logs have the same index and
  541  term, then the logs are identical in all preceding entries.
  542  The first property follows from the fact that a leader
  543  creates at most one entry with a given log index in a given
  544  term, and log entries never change their position in the
  545  log. The second property is guaranteed by a simple consistency check performed by AppendEntries. When sending an AppendEntries RPC, the leader includes the index
  546  and term of the entry in its log that immediately precedes
  547  the new entries. If the follower does not find an entry in
  548  its log with the same index and term, then it refuses the
  549  new entries. The consistency check acts as an induction
  550  step: the initial empty state of the logs satisfies the Log
  551  Matching Property, and the consistency check preserves
  552  the Log Matching Property whenever logs are extended.
  553  As a result, whenever AppendEntries returns successfully,
  554  the leader knows that the follower's log is identical to its
  555  own log up through the new entries.
  556  During normal operation, the logs of the leader and
  557  followers stay consistent, so the AppendEntries consistency check never fails. However, leader crashes can leave

  567  the followers' logs to duplicate its own. This means that
  568  conflicting entries in follower logs will be overwritten

  615  5.4 Safety
  616  The previous sections described how Raft elects leaders and replicates log entries. However, the mechanisms
  617  described so far are not quite sufficient to ensure that each
  618  state machine executes exactly the same commands in the
  619  same order. For example, a follower might be unavailable
  620  while the leader commits several log entries, then it could
  621  be elected leader and overwrite these entries with new
  622  ones; as a result, different state machines might execute
  623  different command sequences.
  624  This section completes the Raft algorithm by adding a
  625  restriction on which servers may be elected leader. The
  626  restriction ensures that the leader for any given term contains all of the entries committed in previous terms (the
  627  Leader Completeness Property from Figure 3). Given the
  628  election restriction, we then make the rules for commitment more precise. Finally, we present a proof sketch for
  629  the Leader Completeness Property and show how it leads
  630  to correct behavior of the replicated state machine.

  631  5.4.1 Election restriction
  632  In any leader-based consensus algorithm, the leader
  633  must eventually store all of the committed log entries. In
  634  some consensus algorithms, such as Viewstamped Replication [20], a leader can be elected even if it doesn't
  635  initially contain all of the committed entries. These algorithms contain additional mechanisms to identify the
  636  missing entries and transmit them to the new leader, either during the election process or shortly afterwards. Unfortunately, this results in considerable additional mechanism and complexity. Raft uses a simpler approach where
  637  it guarantees that all the committed entries from previous
  638  terms are present on each new leader from the moment of
  639  its election, without the need to transfer those entries to
  640  the leader. This means that log entries only flow in one direction, from leaders to followers, and leaders never overwrite existing entries in their logs.
  641  Raft uses the voting process to prevent a candidate from
  642  winning an election unless its log contains all committed
  643  entries. A candidate must contact a majority of the cluster
  644  in order to be elected, which means that every committed
  645  entry must be present in at least one of those servers. If the
  646  candidate's log is at least as up-to-date as any other log
  647  in that majority (where "up-to-date" is defined precisely
  648  below), then it will hold all the committed entries. The
  649  RequestVote RPC implements this restriction: the RPC
  650  includes information about the candidate's log, and the
  651  voter denies its vote if its own log is more up-to-date than
  652  that of the candidate.
  653  Raft determines which of two logs is more up-to-date
  654  by comparing the index and term of the last entries in the

  750  5.5 Follower and candidate crashes
  751  Until this point we have focused on leader failures. Follower and candidate crashes are much simpler to handle than leader crashes, and they are both handled in the
  752  same way. If a follower or candidate crashes, then future RequestVote and AppendEntries RPCs sent to it will
  753  fail. Raft handles these failures by retrying indefinitely;
  754  if the crashed server restarts, then the RPC will complete
  755  successfully. If a server crashes after completing an RPC
  756  but before responding, then it will receive the same RPC
  757  again after it restarts. Raft RPCs are idempotent, so this
  758  causes no harm. For example, if a follower receives an
  759  AppendEntries request that includes log entries already
  760  present in its log, it ignores those entries in the new request.

  761  5.6 Timing and availability
  762  One of our requirements for Raft is that safety must
  763  not depend on timing: the system must not produce incorrect results just because some event happens more quickly
  764  or slowly than expected. However, availability (the ability
  765  of the system to respond to clients in a timely manner)
  766  must inevitably depend on timing. For example, if message exchanges take longer than the typical time between
  767  server crashes, candidates will not stay up long enough to
  768  win an election; without a steady leader, Raft cannot make
  769  progress.
  770  Leader election is the aspect of Raft where timing is
  771  most critical. Raft will be able to elect and maintain a
  772  steady leader as long as the system satisfies the following timing requirement:
  773  broadcastTime ≪ electionTimeout ≪ MTBF
  774  In this inequality broadcastTime is the average time it
  775  takes a server to send RPCs in parallel to every server
  776  in the cluster and receive their responses; electionTimeout is the election timeout described in Section 5.2; and
  777  MTBF is the average time between failures for a single

  778  2014 USENIX Annual Technical Conference 313
=== PAGE 11 ===

  779  server. The broadcast time should be an order of magnitude less than the election timeout so that leaders can
  780  reliably send the heartbeat messages required to keep followers from starting elections; given the randomized approach used for election timeouts, this inequality also
  781  makes split votes unlikely. The election timeout should be
  782  a few orders of magnitude less than MTBF so that the system makes steady progress. When the leader crashes, the
  783  system will be unavailable for roughly the election timeout; we would like this to represent only a small fraction
  784  of overall time.
  785  The broadcast time and MTBF are properties of the underlying system, while the election timeout is something
  786  we must choose. Raft's RPCs typically require the recipient to persist information to stable storage, so the broadcast time may range from 0.5ms to 20ms, depending on
  787  storage technology. As a result, the election timeout is
  788  likely to be somewhere between 10ms and 500ms. Typical
  789  server MTBFs are several months or more, which easily
  790  satisfies the timing requirement.

  791  6 Cluster membership changes
  792  Up until now we have assumed that the cluster configuration (the set of servers participating in the consensus
  793  algorithm) is fixed. In practice, it will occasionally be necessary to change the configuration, for example to replace
  794  servers when they fail or to change the degree of replication. Although this can be done by taking the entire cluster
  795  off-line, updating configuration files, and then restarting
  796  the cluster, this would leave the cluster unavailable during the changeover. In addition, if there are any manual
  797  steps, they risk operator error. In order to avoid these issues, we decided to automate configuration changes and
  798  incorporate them into the Raft consensus algorithm.
  799  For the configuration change mechanism to be safe,
  800  there must be no point during the transition where it
  801  is possible for two leaders to be elected for the same
  802  term. Unfortunately, any approach where servers switch
  803  directly from the old configuration to the new configuration is unsafe. It isn't possible to atomically switch all of
  804  the servers at once, so the cluster can potentially split into
  805  two independent majorities during the transition (see Figure 10).
  806  In order to ensure safety, configuration changes must
  807  use a two-phase approach. There are a variety of ways
  808  to implement the two phases. For example, some systems
  809  (e.g., [20]) use the first phase to disable the old configuration so it cannot process client requests; then the second
  810  phase enables the new configuration. In Raft the cluster
  811  first switches to a transitional configuration we call joint
  812  consensus; once the joint consensus has been committed,
  813  the system then transitions to the new configuration. The
  814  joint consensus combines both the old and new configurations:

  882  fromCnew). Before this point, it may be the case that only
  883  a server fromCold can be elected leader.
  884  The third issue is that removed servers (those not in
  885  Cnew) can disrupt the cluster. These servers will not receive heartbeats, so they will time out and start new elections. They will then send RequestVote RPCs with new
  886  term numbers, and this will cause the current leader to
  887  revert to follower state. A new leader will eventually be
  888  elected, but the removed servers will time out again and
  889  the process will repeat, resulting in poor availability.
  890  To prevent this problem, servers disregard RequestVote
  891  RPCs when they believe a current leader exists. Specifically, if a server receives a RequestVote RPC within
  892  the minimum election timeout of hearing from a current leader, it does not update its term or grant its vote.

  893  USENIX Association

  894  This does not affect normal elections, where each server
  895  waits at least a minimum election timeout before starting
  896  an election. However, it helps avoid disruptions from removed servers: if a leader is able to get heartbeats to its
  897  cluster, then it will not be deposed by larger term numbers.

  905  8 Implementation and evaluation
  906  We have implemented Raft as part of a replicated
  907  state machine that stores configuration information for
  908  RAMCloud [30] and assists in failover of the RAMCloud
  909  coordinator. The Raft implementation contains roughly
  910  2000 lines of C++ code, not including tests, comments, or
  911  blank lines. The source code is freely available [21]. There
  912  are also about 25 independent third-party open source implementations [31] of Raft in various stages of development, based on drafts of this paper. Also, various companies are deploying Raft-based systems [31].

  914  criteria: understandability, correctness, and performance.

  998  8.2 Correctness
  999  We have developed a formal specification and a proof
 1000  of safety for the consensus mechanism described in Section 5. The formal specification [28] makes the information summarized in Figure 2 completely precise using the
 1001  TLA+ specification language [15]. It is about 400 lines
 1002  long and serves as the subject of the proof. It is also useful on its own for anyone implementing Raft. We have
 1003  mechanically proven the Log Completeness Property using the TLA proof system [6]. However, this proof relies
 1004  on invariants that have not been mechanically checked
 1005  (for example, we have not proven the type safety of the
 1006  specification). Furthermore, we have written an informal
 1007  proof [28] of the State Machine Safety property which
 1008  is complete (it relies on the specification alone) and relatively precise (it is about 3500 words long).

 1009  8.3 Performance
 1010  Raft's performance is similar to other consensus algorithms such as Paxos. The most important case for performance is when an established leader is replicating new
 1011  log entries. Raft achieves this using the minimal number

 1013  cluster). It is also possible to further improve Raft's performance. For example, it easily supports batching and
 1014  pipelining requests for higher throughput and lower latency. Various optimizations have been proposed in the
 1015  literature for other algorithms; many of these could be applied to Raft, but we leave this to future work.

 1079  9 Related work
 1080  There have been numerous publications related to consensus algorithms, many of which fall into one of the following categories:
 1081  • Lamport's original description of Paxos [13], and attempts to explain it more clearly [14, 18, 19].
 1082  • Elaborations of Paxos, which fill in missing details and
 1083  modify the algorithm to provide a better foundation for
 1084  implementation [24, 35, 11].
 1085  • Systems that implement consensus algorithms, such as
 1086  Chubby [2, 4], ZooKeeper [9, 10], and Spanner [5]. The
 1087  algorithms for Chubby and Spanner have not been published in detail, though both claim to be based on Paxos.
 1088  ZooKeeper's algorithm has been published in more detail, but it is quite different from Paxos.
 1089  • Performance optimizations that can be applied to
 1090  Paxos [16, 17, 3, 23, 1, 25].
 1091  • Oki and Liskov's Viewstamped Replication (VR), an
 1092  alternative approach to consensus developed around the
 1093  same time as Paxos. The original description [27] was
 1094  intertwined with a protocol for distributed transactions,
 1095  but the core consensus protocol has been separated in
 1096  a recent update [20]. VR uses a leader-based approach
 1097  with many similarities to Raft.
 1098  The greatest difference between Raft and Paxos is
 1099  Raft's strong leadership: Raft uses leader election as an
 1100  essential part of the consensus protocol, and it concentrates as much functionality as possible in the leader. This
 1101  approach results in a simpler algorithm that is easier to
 1102  understand. For example, in Paxos, leader election is orthogonal to the basic consensus protocol: it serves only as
 1103  a performance optimization and is not required for achieving consensus. However, this results in additional mechanism: Paxos includes both a two-phase protocol for basic
 1104  consensus and a separate mechanism for leader election.
 1105  In contrast, Raft incorporates leader election directly into
 1106  the consensus algorithm and uses it as the first of the two
 1107  phases of consensus. This results in less mechanism than
 1108  in Paxos.
 1109  Like Raft, VR and ZooKeeper are leader-based and
 1110  therefore share many of Raft's advantages over Paxos.
 1111  However, Raft has less mechanism that VR or ZooKeeper
 1112  because it minimizes the functionality in non-leaders. For
 1113  example, log entries in Raft flow in only one direction:
 1114  outward from the leader in AppendEntries RPCs. In VR

 1115  2014 USENIX Annual Technical Conference 317
=== PAGE 15 ===

 1116  log entries flow in both directions (leaders can receive
 1117  log entries during the election process); this results in
 1118  additional mechanism and complexity. The published description of ZooKeeper also transfers log entries both to
 1119  and from the leader, but the implementation is apparently
 1120  more like Raft [32].
 1121  Raft has fewer message types than any other algorithm
 1122  for consensus-based log replication that we are aware of.
 1123  For example, VR and ZooKeeper each define 10 different message types, while Raft has only 4 message types
 1124  (two RPC requests and their responses). Raft's messages
 1125  are a bit more dense than the other algorithms', but they
 1126  are simpler collectively. In addition, VR and ZooKeeper
 1127  are described in terms of transmitting entire logs during
 1128  leader changes; additional message types will be required
 1129  to optimize these mechanisms so that they are practical.
 1130  Several different approaches for cluster membership changes have been proposed or implemented in
 1131  other work, including Lamport's original proposal [13],
 1132  VR [20], and SMART [22]. We chose the joint consensus
 1133  approach for Raft because it leverages the rest of the consensus protocol, so that very little additional mechanism
 1134  is required for membership changes. Lamport's α-based
 1135  approach was not an option for Raft because it assumes
 1136  consensus can be reached without a leader. In comparison
 1137  to VR and SMART, Raft's reconfiguration algorithm has
 1138  the advantage that membership changes can occur without limiting the processing of normal requests; in contrast, VR stops all normal processing during configuration changes, and SMART imposes an α-like limit on the
 1139  number of outstanding requests. Raft's approach also adds
 1140  less mechanism than either VR or SMART.

 1141  10 Conclusion
 1142  Algorithms are often designed with correctness, efficiency, and/or conciseness as the primary goals. Although
 1143  these are all worthy goals, we believe that understandability is just as important. None of the other goals can be
 1144  achieved until developers render the algorithm into a practical implementation, which will inevitably deviate from
 1145  and expand upon the published form. Unless developers
 1146  have a deep understanding of the algorithm and can create intuitions about it, it will be difficult for them to retain
 1147  its desirable properties in their implementation.
 1148  In this paper we addressed the issue of distributed consensus, where a widely accepted but impenetrable algorithm, Paxos, has challenged students and developers for
 1149  many years. We developed a new algorithm, Raft, which
 1150  we have shown to be more understandable than Paxos.
 1151  We also believe that Raft provides a better foundation
 1152  for system building. Using understandability as the primary design goal changed the way we approached the design of Raft; as the design progressed we found ourselves
 1153  reusing a few techniques repeatedly, such as decomposing
 1154  the problem and simplifying the state space. These tech-

 1155  318 2014 USENIX Annual Technical Conference

 1156  niques not only improved the understandability of Raft
 1157  but also made it easier to convince ourselves of its correctness.
