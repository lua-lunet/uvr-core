    7  Abstract
    8  Reconfigurable state machine replication is an important enabler of elasticity for replicated cloud
    9  services, which must be able to dynamically adjust their size as a function of changing load and resource
   10  availability. We introduce a new generic framework to allow the reconfigurable state machine implementation to be derived from a collection of arbitrary non-reconfigurable state machines. Our reduction
   11  framework follows the black box approach, and does not make any assumptions with respect to its execution environment apart from reliable channels. It allows higher-level services to leverage speculative
   12  command execution to ensure uninterrupted progress during the reconfiguration periods as well as in situations where failures prevent the reconfiguration agreement from being reached in a timely fashion. We
   13  apply our framework to obtain a reconfigurable speculative state machine from the non-reconfigurable
   14  Paxos implementation, and analyze its performance on a realistic distributed testbed. Our results show
   15  that our framework incurs negligible overheads in the absence of reconfiguration, and allows steady
   16  throughput to be maintained throughout the reconfiguration periods.

   19  1 Introduction

   20  Replicated state machine, or RSM [11], is an important tool for maintaining integrity of distributed applications and services in failure-prone data center and cloud computing environments. In these settings, the
   21  infrastructure needs to adapt to changing resource availability, load fluctuations, variable power consumption, and data locality constraints. In order to meet these requirements, RSM must support reconfiguration,
   22  i.e., dynamic changes to replica set, or quorum system. It is essential to ensure that reconfiguration incurs
   23  minimum disruption to availability and performance, in order to enable building truly elastic services. The
   24  ability to perform reconfigurations in a non-disruptive fashion provides system designers with a powerful
   25  paradigm that can enable many optimizations. This includes proactive replacement of suspected or slow
   26  nodes at low cost, adapting to the changing environment characteristics (e.g. network delay, or diurnal load
   27  fluctuations, and many others).
   28  Reconfigurable RSM has been proposed in multiple contexts (e.g., [14, 15, 10]). Each solution implemented a slightly different set of requirements, and all proved nontrivial. Designing this functionality for a
   29  realistic environment with minimal impact on performance is even more challenging. Na¨ıve constructions
   30  follow the "brick-wall approach" in which the flow of user commands is stalled until the new configuration
   31  is installed and the state is transferred to it. Ideally, systems should strive to avoid this, and favor implementations with near-seamless hand-on, that maintain steady throughput and latency during the transition
   32  periods.
   33  This paper introduces a framework for constructing reconfigurable state machines from collections of
   34  non-reconfigurable ones. We follow the black box approach that assumes nothing about the execution environment except the existence of reliable communication channels. Our reduction is both simple and generic,
   35  i.e., the underlying RSM implementation is completely opaque to the framework. Furthermore, it does
   36  not compromise efficiency, incurring negligible overhead in the absence of reconfigurations and avoiding
   37  system stalls upon reconfiguration.
   38  The main ideas underlying our framework are as follows. Each newly proposed configuration is associated with its own instance of RSM, and all active RSM are executed concurrently to each other. The
   39  globally consistent trunk of commands is created by gluing together the totally ordered command sequences
   40  produced by each RSM. When switching from one RSM to another, the latter is chosen based on the outcome of the configuration agreement in the former. Our framework also relieves RSMs of state transfer
   41  responsibilities by ensuring that the latest trunk is transferred to the new configuration concurrently with
   42  the RSM execution. This way, each newly created RSM is completely independent from its predecessor,
   43  and in particular, can start executing from its initial state. We leverage this capability in our Paxos-based
   44  reconfigurable RSM implementation to supply each newly created Paxos instance with the identifier of a
   45  deterministically chosen leader thus eschewing the first phase of Paxos if the configured leader does not fail.
   46  Another important optimization made possible by the RSM independence is the ability to speculatively
   47  overlap their execution with the reconfiguration protocol, thus considerably reducing the command latency
   48  during reconfiguration periods. Specifically, each RSM is made available for accepting commands for the
   49  new configuration as soon as it is proposed, and without waiting for it to be agreed upon by the parent RSM.
   50  The proposals associated with the new configuration proceed to be ordered concurrently with the reconfiguration agreement, and are added to the trunk as soon as the configuration is agreed upon. This way, our
   51  framework allows unbounded degree of parallelism in the command execution during the transition periods
   52  avoiding the performance problems of the "brick-wall" solutions. In addition, the benefits of speculation become more substantial as the network delay grows thus making speculative solutions attractive in wide-area
   53  network settings.

   55  The modularity of our framework enables a range of additional features useful in practical settings. One
   56  such feature is supporting rolling software upgrades: i.e., the implementation of the deployed RSMs can
   57  be replaced with the newer one without stopping the system. Likewise, misbehaving or buggy RSMs can
   58  be restarted or replaced on-the-fly with the minimum impact on the system operation as per the recoveryoriented computing (ROC) [19] guidelines.
   59  We used our framework to implement a full-fledged reconfigurable replication platform using nonreconfigurable Paxos [12, 13] as its underlying non-reconfigurable RSM, and experimentally studied its
   60  performance. The results demonstrate that our system achieves high throughput and low latency in the absence of reconfigurations, which stay almost unchanged under highly dynamic reconfiguration scenarios.
   61  Specifically, the throughput is unaffected in the runs with reconfiguration rate of 5 per second, and degrades
   62  only by 20% when reconfiguration rate achieves that of 20 per second. In addition, our study indicates that
   63  the command latency in the vicinity of reconfiguration stays the same as that in the absence thereof.
   64  In summary, the contributions made by our work are as follows:
   65  • new generic framework for constructing reconfigurable RSM from non-reconfigurable ones, which is
   66  simple, modular, and efficient;
   67  • new speculative approach to enable overlapping command ordering with the reconfiguration protocol
   68  thus reducing the reconfiguration latencies;
   69  • practical implementation that leverages our framework to transform Paxos-based RSM to a highly
   70  efficient reconfigurable RSM;
   71  • detailed experimental study of the implementation above demonstrating the benefits of our framework
   72  in practical settings.
   73  The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Related work is discussed in Section 2. System model
   74  is presented in Section 3. Section 4 introduces non-reconfigurable and reconfigurable variants of RSM,
   75  and rigorously specify their properties. In Section 5, we present our reduction algorithm, and argue its
   76  correctness. The replication platform built on top of our framework, and its performance are discussed in
   77  Section 6. Section 7 concludes the paper.

   78  2 Related work

   79  Modular approaches to specifying RSM and their constituent building blocks have been addressed in several
   80  prior works. Boichat et al. [5] introduced modular decomposition of Paxos into weak leader election and
   81  round-based consensus abstraction, and [7] studied the abstraction of ranked register capturing the essence of
   82  the Paxos consensus algorithm. These papers however, do not address reconfiguration. Stoppable Paxos [10]
   83  presents a framework for combining non-reconfigurable Paxos instances into a reconfigurable RSM, but does
   84  not attempt to abstract away the internals of the underlying Paxos implementation. In addition, [10] does
   85  not support concurrent execution of the individual Paxos instances.
   86  Several approaches to alleviating reconfiguration bottleneck in reconfigurable state machines have been
   87  proposed. The original idea by Lamport, described in [12, 13, 14], and implemented in SMART [16], was
   88  to delay the effect of the configuration agreed in a specific consensus instance by a fixed number α of
   89  successive consensus instances. If the configuration must take effect immediately, the remaining instances
   90  can be skipped by passing a special "window closure" decree consisting of α consecutive noop instances.
   91  Although this approach allows up to α consecutive commands to be executed concurrently, choosing the

   92  2
=== PAGE 4 ===

   93  right value of α is nontrivial. On the one hand, choosing α to be too small may under-utilize the available
   94  resources. On the other hand, large values of α may not match the actual service reconfiguration rate
   95  resulting in too frequent invocations of the window closure decrees (which must complete synchronously).
   96  Chubby [6] and ZooKeeper [9] expose high-level synchronization primitives (respectively, locks and
   97  watches) that can be used to implement a reconfigurable state machine within the client groups. The solutions based on this approach are however, vulnerable to timing failures, and therefore, either restrict their
   98  failure model [25], or rely on additional synchronization protocols within the replication groups themselves
   99  to maintain consistency [15, 21].
  100  Vertical Paxos [15] removes the configuration agreement overhead from the critical path by delegating it
  101  to an auxiliary "configuration master". The reconfiguration involves an extra step of synchronizing with the
  102  read quorums of all preceding configurations causing throughput degradation. In addition, the configuration
  103  master itself is implemented using the α-based reconfigurable Paxos protocol, and therefore, suffers from
  104  the limitations similar to those discussed above.
  105  Dynamic reconfiguration has been extensively studied in the context of virtually synchronous group
  106  communication [22, 4, 8, 20], and reconfigurable read/write registers [17, 1]. The reconfiguration protocols described in these papers do not aim to support consistency semantics as strong as those of state
  107  machine replication, and therefore do not directly apply in our context. Birman et al. [3] present a replication framework unifying reconfigurable state machine and virtual synchrony in which the normal operation
  108  is suspended during the reconfiguration periods.
  109  Optimistic and speculative approaches to mask the coordination latency have been extensively studied
  110  in the past in a variety of contexts (such as e.g., group communication [23], and database replication [2]).
  111  However, to the best of our knowledge, speculative reconfigurable state machine replication studied in this
  112  paper has not yet been addressed in the prior work.

  113  3 System Model

  114  We consider an asynchronous message-passing system consisting of the (possibly infinite) set of processes
  115  P. Each pair of processes is connected by a point-to-point FIFO channel. For simplicity, we assume the
  116  crash failure model: i.e., the process experiencing a crash failure stops executing any further instructions
  117  forever. A process that never crashes throughout the system execution is called correct. The channels are
  118  reliable in the sense that a message sent by a correct process p to another correct process q is eventually
  119  received by q. Extending our techniques to support stronger benign failure models (such as crash/recovery,
  120  message omission, and network partitions and merges) is straightforward, and our prototype implementation
  121  described in Section 6 is capable of doing that.

  417  5.3 Speculation
  418  One important property of our R-RSM implementation is that the non-reconfigurable state machine for each
  419  configuration C is completely independent from the state machines for other configurations, and could proceed ordering the commands and configurations submitted under C concurrently with them. In particular,
  420  since the members of C are notified of the new configuration immediately upon receiving its corresponding reconfiguration request (line 1.27– 1.29), this also applies to the commands submitted under C while
  421  the successor of its parent configuration is still being chosen. These commands will be available for incorporating into the trunk and subsequent learning as soon as C becomes the chosen successor of its parent
  422  configuration, thus reducing (or eliminating altogether) reconfiguration delays.
  423  The precise degree of the performance improvement depends on the choice of the underlying nonreconfigurable state machine implementation, and the network parameters (primarily, the communication

  427  6 Implementation and Evaluation

  428  We applied our reduction framework to implement a full-fledged reconfigurable replication platform using
  429  non-reconfigurable Paxos [12, 13] as its underlying NR-RSM implementation.
  430  To shield the users from intricacies of selecting viable configurations, our replication platform augments
  431  R-RSM with two additional modules: Configuration Manager (CM) and Command Queue (CQ). CM implements the logic to detect failures, and produce new configurations, which are then submitted to R-RSM
  432  through the recon requests. The configuration membership is based on the failure suspicions and user policies. An example policy may ask for every configuration to include sufficiently many healthy nodes. CQ is
  433  responsible for associating user commands with configurations and submitting them to the platform through
  434  the propose requests. The configurations are chosen based on the ready notifications thus leveraging the
  435  speculation capabilities of R-RSM to reduce reconfiguration delays.
  436  Each Paxos instance created by R-RSM is supplied with the identifier of a deterministically chosen leader
  437  thus avoiding the overheads of the first phase of Paxos if the configured leader does not fail. Thus, normally,
  438  only the second phase of Paxos is needed to complete each individual agreement instance resulting in a
  439  single round-trip delay to order a command.
  440  We studied the performance of our implementation experimentally using the testbed comprised of 4
  441  IBM HS22 blades equipped with Intel Xeon X5670 processor with 24 2.93GHz cores and 64GB RAM.
  442  Each machine was equipped with 1GB network card, and ran Red Hat Linux. A single replica was hosted
  443  on each machine.
  444  The replicas were subjected to request streams generated in either synchronous or asynchronous fashion.
  445  In the synchronous mode, the requests were issued from multiple threads each of which was waiting for the
  446  response to the previously submitted request to arrive before submitting the new one. The synchronous mode
  447  allowed us to exercise high degree of control over the offered system load by varying the number of simultaneously executed synchronous threads. In contrast, in the asynchronous mode, each thread was submitting
  448  requests as they were generated without waiting for the prior requests completion. The asynchronous mode
  449  was used to drive the system to its maximum utilization.
  450  In the first experiment, we studied the throughput and latency as a function of the offered system load
  451  in the absence of reconfiguration using a single configuration consisting of 3 replicas. The results (see
  452  Figure 4) show that our system is capable of achieving the sustained throughput of 12K request/second
  453  before the latency starts to rapidly grow.
  454  In the next experiment, we studied the impact of our speculative reconfiguration mechanism on the
  455  command throughput. For that, the system was subjected to a series of reconfiguration requests submitted at
  456  varied frequency. The normal commands were submitted using 10 synchronous threads, which corresponds
  457  to the maximum sustained system load of 12K requests/second (see Figure 4). The measurements are
  458  depicted (see Figure 5) as absolute command throughput in the presence of reconfiguration, and percentage
  459  of degradation relative to the maximum sustained throughput (12K). The results show that the throughput
  460  in the presence of reconfigurations is almost identical to that achieved in the absence thereof, reaching
  461  maximum 20% of degradation when the system is reconfigured 20 times/second.
  462  In the last experiment, we studied the degree of improvement produced by speculation in terms of the
  463  latencies of the commands submitted in close proximity of the reconfiguration requests. The results (see

  506  7 Conclusions

  507  We have introduced a modular framework for transforming a collection of arbitrary non-reconfigurable
  508  replicated state machines (RSM) into the reconfigurable RSM implementation. Our framework follows
  509  the black box approach, and does not make any assumptions with respect to its execution environment
  510  apart from reliable channels. The individual state machines instantiated by our implementation can be
  511  executed concurrently to one another, in particular, overlap each other execution in a speculative fashion
  512  to mask the reconfiguration delays. We have applied our framework to build a prototype of an end-to-end
  513  dynamic replication platform using non-reconfigurable Paxos as its underlying building block, and studied
