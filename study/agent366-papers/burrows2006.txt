    1  The Chubby lock service for loosely-coupled distributed systems

    2  OSDI '06: 7th USENIX Symposium on Operating Systems Design and Implementation

    3  Mike Burrows

    4  Abstract

    5  We describe our experiences with the Chubby lock service, which is intended to provide coarse-grained locking as well as reliable (though low-volume) storage for
    6  a loosely-coupled distributed system. Chubby provides
    7  an interface much like a distributed file system with advisory locks, but the design emphasis is on availability
    8  and reliability, as opposed to high performance. Many
    9  instances of the service have been used for over a year,
   10  with several of them each handling a few tens of thousands of clients concurrently. The paper describes the
   11  initial design and expected use, compares it with actual
   12  use, and explains how the design had to be modified to
   13  accommodate the differences.

   14  1 Introduction

   15  This paper describes a lock service called Chubby. It is
   16  intended for use within a loosely-coupled distributed system consisting of moderately large numbers of small machines connected by a high-speed network. For example,
   17  a Chubby instance (also known as a Chubby cell) might
   18  serve ten thousand 4-processor machines connected by
   19  1Gbit/s Ethernet. Most Chubby cells are confined to a
   20  single data centre or machine room, though we do run
   21  at least one Chubby cell whose replicas are separated by
   22  thousands of kilometres.
   23  The purpose of the lock service is to allow its clients
   24  to synchronize their activities and to agree on basic information about their environment. The primary goals
   25  included reliability, availability to a moderately large set
   26  of clients, and easy-to-understand semantics; throughput and storage capacity were considered secondary.
   27  Chubby's client interface is similar to that of a simple file
   28  system that performs whole-file reads and writes, augmented with advisory locks and with notification of various events such as file modification.
   29  We expected Chubby to help developers deal with
   30  coarse-grained synchronization within their systems, and
   31  in particular to deal with the problem of electing a leader
   32  from among a set of otherwise equivalent servers. For

   33  USENIX Association

   34  , Google Inc.

   35  example, the Google File System [7] uses a Chubby lock
   36  to appoint a GFS master server, and Bigtable [3] uses
   37  Chubby in several ways: to elect a master, to allow the
   38  master to discover the servers it controls, and to permit
   39  clients to find the master. In addition, both GFS and
   40  Bigtable use Chubby as a well-known and available location to store a small amount of meta-data; in effect they
   41  use Chubby as the root of their distributed data structures. Some services use locks to partition work (at a
   42  coarse grain) between several servers.
   43  Before Chubby was deployed, most distributed systems at Google used ad hoc methods for primary election (when work could be duplicated without harm), or
   44  required operator intervention (when correctness was essential). In the former case, Chubby allowed a small saving in computing effort. In the latter case, it achieved a
   45  significant improvement in availability in systems that no
   46  longer required human intervention on failure.
   47  Readers familiar with distributed computing will recognize the election of a primary among peers as an instance of the distributed consensus problem, and realize
   48  we require a solution using asynchronous communication; this term describes the behaviour of the vast majority of real networks, such as Ethernet or the Internet,
   49  which allow packets to be lost, delayed, and reordered.
   50  (Practitioners should normally beware of protocols based
   51  on models that make stronger assumptions on the environment.) Asynchronous consensus is solved by the
   52  Paxos protocol [12, 13]. The same protocol was used by
   53  Oki and Liskov (see their paper on viewstamped replication [19, §4]), an equivalence noted by others [14, §6].
   54  Indeed, all working protocols for asynchronous consensus we have so far encountered have Paxos at their core.
   55  Paxos maintains safety without timing assumptions, but
   56  clocks must be introduced to ensure liveness; this overcomes the impossibility result of Fischer et al. [5, §1].
   57  Building Chubby was an engineering effort required
   58  to fill the needs mentioned above; it was not research.
   59  We claim no new algorithms or techniques. The purpose
   60  of this paper is to describe what we did and why, rather
   61  than to advocate it. In the sections that follow, we describe Chubby's design and implementation, and how it

   67  2 Design

   68  2.1 Rationale

   69  One might argue that we should have built a library embodying Paxos, rather than a library that accesses a centralized lock service, even a highly reliable one. A client
   70  Paxos library would depend on no other servers (besides
   71  the name service), and would provide a standard framework for programmers, assuming their services can be
   72  implemented as state machines. Indeed, we provide such
   73  a client library that is independent of Chubby.
   74  Nevertheless, a lock service has some advantages over
   75  a client library. First, our developers sometimes do not
   76  plan for high availability in the way one would wish. Often their systems start as prototypes with little load and
   77  loose availability guarantees; invariably the code has not
   78  been specially structured for use with a consensus protocol. As the service matures and gains clients, availability
   79  becomes more important; replication and primary election are then added to an existing design. While this
   80  could be done with a library that provides distributed
   81  consensus, a lock server makes it easier to maintain existing program structure and communication patterns. For
   82  example, to elect a master which then writes to an existing file server requires adding just two statements and
   83  one RPC parameter to an existing system: One would
   84  acquire a lock to become master, pass an additional integer (the lock acquisition count) with the write RPC, and
   85  add an if-statement to the file server to reject the write if
   86  the acquisition count is lower than the current value (to
   87  guard against delayed packets). We have found this technique easier than making existing servers participate in
   88  a consensus protocol, and especially so if compatibility
   89  must be maintained during a transition period.
   90  Second, many of our services that elect a primary
   91  or that partition data between their components need a
   92  mechanism for advertising the results. This suggests that
   93  we should allow clients to store and fetch small quantities of data—that is, to read and write small files. This
   94  could be done with a name service, but our experience
   95  has been that the lock service itself is well-suited for this
   96  task, both because this reduces the number of servers on
   97  which a client depends, and because the consistency features of the protocol are shared. Chubby's success as
   98  a name server owes much to its use of consistent client
   99  caching, rather than time-based caching. In particular,
  100  we found that developers greatly appreciated not having

  101  336

  102  to choose a cache timeout such as the DNS time-to-live
  103  value, which if chosen poorly can lead to high DNS load,
  104  or long client fail-over times.
  105  Third, a lock-based interface is more familiar to our
  106  programmers. Both the replicated state machine of Paxos
  107  and the critical sections associated with exclusive locks
  108  can provide the programmer with the illusion of sequential programming. However, many programmers have
  109  come across locks before, and think they know to use
  110  them. Ironically, such programmers are usually wrong,
  111  especially when they use locks in a distributed system;
  112  few consider the effects of independent machine failures on locks in a system with asynchronous communications. Nevertheless, the apparent familiarity of locks
  113  overcomes a hurdle in persuading programmers to use a
  114  reliable mechanism for distributed decision making.
  115  Last, distributed-consensus algorithms use quorums to
  116  make decisions, so they use several replicas to achieve
  117  high availability. For example, Chubby itself usually has
  118  five replicas in each cell, of which three must be running for the cell to be up. In contrast, if a client system
  119  uses a lock service, even a single client can obtain a lock
  120  and make progress safely. Thus, a lock service reduces
  121  the number of servers needed for a reliable client system
  122  to make progress. In a loose sense, one can view the
  123  lock service as a way of providing a generic electorate
  124  that allows a client system to make decisions correctly
  125  when less than a majority of its own members are up.
  126  One might imagine solving this last problem in a different way: by providing a "consensus service", using a
  127  number of servers to provide the "acceptors" in the Paxos
  128  protocol. Like a lock service, a consensus service would
  129  allow clients to make progress safely even with only one
  130  active client process; a similar technique has been used to
  131  reduce the number of state machines needed for Byzantine fault tolerance [24]. However, assuming a consensus
  132  service is not used exclusively to provide locks (which
  133  reduces it to a lock service), this approach solves none of
  134  the other problems described above.
  135  These arguments suggest two key design decisions:
  136  • We chose a lock service, as opposed to a library or
  137  service for consensus, and
  138  • we chose to serve small-files to permit elected primaries to advertise themselves and their parameters,
  139  rather than build and maintain a second service.
  140  Some decisions follow from our expected use and
  141  from our environment:
  142  • A service advertising its primary via a Chubby file
  143  may have thousands of clients. Therefore, we must
  144  allow thousands of clients to observe this file, preferably without needing many servers.
  145  • Clients and replicas of a replicated service may wish
  146  to know when the service's primary changes. This

  150  be useful to avoid polling.
  151  • Even if clients need not poll files periodically, many
  152  will; this is a consequence of supporting many developers. Thus, caching of files is desirable.
  153  • Our developers are confused by non-intuitive caching

  156  security mechanisms, including access control.
  157  A choice that may surprise some readers is that we
  158  do not expect lock use to be fine-grained, in which they
  159  might be held only for a short duration (seconds or less);
  160  instead, we expect coarse-grained use. For example, an
  161  application might use a lock to elect a primary, which
  162  would then handle all access to that data for a considerable time, perhaps hours or days. These two styles of use
  163  suggest different requirements from a lock server.
  164  Coarse-grained locks impose far less load on the lock
  165  server. In particular, the lock-acquisition rate is usually only weakly related to the transaction rate of the
  166  client applications. Coarse-grained locks are acquired
  167  only rarely, so temporary lock server unavailability delays clients less. On the other hand, the transfer of a lock
  168  from client to client may require costly recovery procedures, so one would not wish a fail-over of a lock server
  169  to cause locks to be lost. Thus, it is good for coarsegrained locks to survive lock server failures, there is little
  170  concern about the overhead of doing so, and such locks
  171  allow many clients to be adequately served by a modest
  172  number of lock servers with somewhat lower availability.
  173  Fine-grained locks lead to different conclusions. Even
  174  brief unavailability of the lock server may cause many
  175  clients to stall. Performance and the ability to add new
  176  servers at will are of great concern because the transaction rate at the lock service grows with the combined
  177  transaction rate of clients. It can be advantageous to reduce the overhead of locking by not maintaining locks
  178  across lock server failure, and the time penalty for dropping locks every so often is not severe because locks are
  179  held for short periods. (Clients must be prepared to lose
  180  locks during network partitions, so the loss of locks on
  181  lock server fail-over introduces no new recovery paths.)
  182  Chubby is intended to provide only coarse-grained
  183  locking. Fortunately, it is straightforward for clients to
  184  implement their own fine-grained locks tailored to their
  185  application. An application might partition its locks into
  186  groups and use Chubby's coarse-grained locks to allocate
  187  these lock groups to application-specific lock servers.
  188  Little state is needed to maintain these fine-grain locks;
  189  the servers need only keep a non-volatile, monotonicallyincreasing acquisition counter that is rarely updated.
  190  Clients can learn of lost locks at unlock time, and if a
  191  simple fixed-length lease is used, the protocol can be
  192  simple and efficient. The most important benefits of this

  210  2.2 System structure

  211  Chubby has two main components that communicate
  212  via RPC: a server, and a library that client applications
  213  link against; see Figure 1. All communication between
  214  Chubby clients and the servers is mediated by the client
  215  library. An optional third component, a proxy server, is
  216  discussed in Section 3.1.
  217  A Chubby cell consists of a small set of servers (typically five) known as replicas, placed so as to reduce the
  218  likelihood of correlated failure (for example, in different
  219  racks). The replicas use a distributed consensus protocol
  220  to elect a master; the master must obtain votes from a
  221  majority of the replicas, plus promises that those replicas
  222  will not elect a different master for an interval of a few
  223  seconds known as the master lease. The master lease is
  224  periodically renewed by the replicas provided the master
  225  continues to win a majority of the vote.
  226  The replicas maintain copies of a simple database, but
  227  only the master initiates reads and writes of this database.
  228  All other replicas simply copy updates from the master,
  229  sent using the consensus protocol.
  230  Clients find the master by sending master location
  231  requests to the replicas listed in the DNS. Non-master
  232  replicas respond to such requests by returning the identity of the master. Once a client has located the master,
  233  the client directs all requests to it either until it ceases
  234  to respond, or until it indicates that it is no longer the
  235  master. Write requests are propagated via the consensus
  236  protocol to all replicas; such requests are acknowledged
  237  when the write has reached a majority of the replicas in
  238  the cell. Read requests are satisfied by the master alone;
  239  this is safe provided the master lease has not expired, as
  240  no other master can possibly exist. If a master fails, the
  241  other replicas run the election protocol when their master
  242  leases expire; a new master will typically be elected in a
  243  few seconds. For example, two recent elections took 6s
  244  and 4s, but we see values as high as 30s (§4.1).

  261  2.3 Files, directories, and handles

  262  Chubby exports a file system interface similar to, but
  263  simpler than that of UNIX [22]. It consists of a strict
  264  tree of files and directories in the usual way, with name
  265  components separated by slashes. A typical name is:
  266  /ls/foo/wombat/pouch
  267  The ls prefix is common to all Chubby names, and
  268  stands for lock service. The second component (foo) is
  269  the name of a Chubby cell; it is resolved to one or more
  270  Chubby servers via DNS lookup. A special cell name
  271  local indicates that the client's local Chubby cell should
  272  be used; this is usually one in the same building and
  273  thus the one most likely to be accessible. The remainder of the name, /wombat/pouch, is interpreted within
  274  the named Chubby cell. Again following UNIX, each directory contains a list of child files and directories, while
  275  each file contains a sequence of uninterpreted bytes.
  276  Because Chubby's naming structure resembles a file
  277  system, we were able to make it available to applications
  278  both with its own specialized API, and via interfaces
  279  used by our other file systems, such as the Google File
  280  System. This significantly reduced the effort needed to
  281  write basic browsing and name space manipulation tools,
  282  and reduced the need to educate casual Chubby users.
  283  The design differs from UNIX in a ways that ease distribution. To allow the files in different directories to be
  284  served from different Chubby masters, we do not expose
  285  operations that can move files from one directory to another, we do not maintain directory modified times, and
  286  we avoid path-dependent permission semantics (that is,
  287  access to a file is controlled by the permissions on the
  288  file itself rather than on directories on the path leading to
  289  the file). To make it easier to cache file meta-data, the
  290  system does not reveal last-access times.
  291  The name space contains only files and directories,
  292  collectively called nodes. Every such node has only one
  293  name within its cell; there are no symbolic or hard links.

  294  338

  295  Nodes may be either permanent or ephemeral. Any
  296  node may be deleted explicitly, but ephemeral nodes are
  297  also deleted if no client has them open (and, for directories, they are empty). Ephemeral files are used as temporary files, and as indicators to others that a client is alive.
  298  Any node can act as an advisory reader/writer lock; these
  299  locks are described in more detail in Section 2.4.
  300  Each node has various meta-data, including three
  301  names of access control lists (ACLs) used to control
  302  reading, writing and changing the ACL names for the
  303  node. Unless overridden, a node inherits the ACL names
  304  of its parent directory on creation. ACLs are themselves
  305  files located in an ACL directory, which is a well-known
  306  part of the cell's local name space. These ACL files consist of simple lists of names of principals; readers may be
  307  reminded of Plan 9's groups [21]. Thus, if file F's write
  308  ACL name is foo, and the ACL directory contains a file
  309  foo that contains an entry bar, then user bar is permitted to write F. Users are authenticated by a mechanism
  310  built into the RPC system. Because Chubby's ACLs are
  311  simply files, they are automatically available to other services that wish to use similar access control mechanisms.
  312  The per-node meta-data includes four monotonicallyincreasing 64-bit numbers that allow clients to detect
  313  changes easily:
  314  • an instance number; greater than the instance number
  315  of any previous node with the same name.
  316  • a content generation number (files only); this increases when the file's contents are written.
  317  • a lock generation number; this increases when the
  318  node's lock transitions from free to held.
  319  • an ACL generation number; this increases when the
  320  node's ACL names are written.
  321  Chubby also exposes a 64-bit file-content checksum so
  322  clients may tell whether files differ.
  323  Clients open nodes to obtain handles that are analogous to UNIX file descriptors. Handles include:
  324  • check digits that prevent clients from creating or
  325  guessing handles, so full access control checks need
  326  be performed only when handles are created (compare with UNIX, which checks its permissions bits at
  327  open time, but not at each read/write because file descriptors cannot be forged).
  328  • a sequence number that allows a master to tell whether
  329  a handle was generated by it or by a previous master.
  330  • mode information provided at open time to allow the
  331  master to recreate its state if an old handle is presented
  332  to a newly restarted master.

  333  2.4 Locks and sequencers

  334  Each Chubby file and directory can act as a reader-writer
  335  lock: either one client handle may hold the lock in exclusive (writer) mode, or any number of client handles may

  336  USENIX Association
=== PAGE 5 ===

  337  OSDI '06: 7th USENIX Symposium on Operating Systems Design and Implementation

  338  hold the lock in shared (reader) mode. Like the mutexes
  339  known to most programmers, locks are advisory. That
  340  is, they conflict only with other attempts to acquire the
  341  same lock: holding a lock called F neither is necessary
  342  to access the file F, nor prevents other clients from doing so. We rejected mandatory locks, which make locked
  343  objects inaccessible to clients not holding their locks:
  344  • Chubby locks often protect resources implemented by
  345  other services, rather than just the file associated with
  346  the lock. To enforce mandatory locking in a meaningful way would have required us to make more extensive modification of these services.
  347  • We did not wish to force users to shut down applications when they needed to access locked files for
  348  debugging or administrative purposes. In a complex
  349  system, it is harder to use the approach employed on
  350  most personal computers, where administrative software can break mandatory locks simply by instructing
  351  the user to shut down his applications or to reboot.
  352  • Our developers perform error checking in the conventional way, by writing assertions such as "lock X is
  353  held", so they benefit little from mandatory checks.
  354  Buggy or malicious processes have many opportunities to corrupt data when locks are not held, so we find
  355  the extra guards provided by mandatory locking to be
  356  of no significant value.
  357  In Chubby, acquiring a lock in either mode requires write
  358  permission so that an unprivileged reader cannot prevent
  359  a writer from making progress.
  360  Locking is complex in distributed systems because
  361  communication is typically uncertain, and processes may
  362  fail independently. Thus, a process holding a lock L may
  363  issue a request R, but then fail. Another process may acquire L and perform some action before R arrives at its
  364  destination. If R later arrives, it may be acted on without
  365  the protection of L, and potentially on inconsistent data.
  366  The problem of receiving messages out of order has been
  367  well studied; solutions include virtual time [11], and virtual synchrony [1], which avoids the problem by ensuring
  368  that messages are processed in an order consistent with
  369  the observations of every participant.
  370  It is costly to introduce sequence numbers into all
  371  the interactions in an existing complex system. Instead,
  372  Chubby provides a means by which sequence numbers
  373  can be introduced into only those interactions that make
  374  use of locks. At any time, a lock holder may request a sequencer, an opaque byte-string that describes the state of
  375  the lock immediately after acquisition. It contains the
  376  name of the lock, the mode in which it was acquired
  377  (exclusive or shared), and the lock generation number.
  378  The client passes the sequencer to servers (such as file
  379  servers) if it expects the operation to be protected by the
  380  lock. The recipient server is expected to test whether
  381  the sequencer is still valid and has the appropriate mode;

  382  USENIX Association

  383  if not, it should reject the request. The validity of a
  384  sequencer can be checked against the server's Chubby
  385  cache or, if the server does not wish to maintain a session with Chubby, against the most recent sequencer that
  386  the server has observed. The sequencer mechanism requires only the addition of a string to affected messages,
  387  and is easily explained to our developers.
  388  Although we find sequencers simple to use, important
  389  protocols evolve slowly. Chubby therefore provides an
  390  imperfect but easier mechanism to reduce the risk of delayed or re-ordered requests to servers that do not support sequencers. If a client releases a lock in the normal
  391  way, it is immediately available for other clients to claim,
  392  as one would expect. However, if a lock becomes free
  393  because the holder has failed or become inaccessible,
  394  the lock server will prevent other clients from claiming
  395  the lock for a period called the lock-delay. Clients may
  396  specify any lock-delay up to some bound, currently one
  397  minute; this limit prevents a faulty client from making a
  398  lock (and thus some resource) unavailable for an arbitrarily long time. While imperfect, the lock-delay protects
  399  unmodified servers and clients from everyday problems
  400  caused by message delays and restarts.

  401  2.5 Events

  402  Chubby clients may subscribe to a range of events when
  403  they create a handle. These events are delivered to the
  404  client asynchronously via an up-call from the Chubby library. Events include:
  405  • file contents modified—often used to monitor the location of a service advertised via the file.
  406  • child node added, removed, or modified—used to implement mirroring (§2.12). (In addition to allowing
  407  new files to be discovered, returning events for child
  408  nodes makes it possible to monitor ephemeral files
  409  without affecting their reference counts.)
  410  • Chubby master failed over—warns clients that other
  411  events may have been lost, so data must be rescanned.
  412  • a handle (and its lock) has become invalid—this typically suggests a communications problem.
  413  • lock acquired—can be used to determine when a primary has been elected.
  414  • conflicting lock request from another client—allows
  415  the caching of locks.
  416  Events are delivered after the corresponding action has
  417  taken place. Thus, if a client is informed that file contents
  418  have changed, it is guaranteed to see the new data (or data
  419  that is yet more recent) if it subsequently reads the file.
  420  The last two events mentioned are rarely used, and
  421  with hindsight could have been omitted. After primary
  422  election for example, clients typically need to communicate with the new primary, rather than simply know
  423  that a primary exists; thus, they wait for a file modifi-

  433  2.6 API

  434  Clients see a Chubby handle as a pointer to an opaque
  435  structure that supports various operations. Handles are
  436  created only by Open(), and destroyed with Close().
  437  Open() opens a named file or directory to produce a
  438  handle, analogous to a UNIX file descriptor. Only this
  439  call takes a node name; all others operate on handles.
  440  The name is evaluated relative to an existing directory
  441  handle; the library provides a handle on "/" that is always
  442  valid. Directory handles avoid the difficulties of using a
  443  program-wide current directory in a multi-threaded program that contains many layers of abstraction [18].
  444  The client indicates various options:
  445  • how the handle will be used (reading; writing and
  446  locking; changing the ACL); the handle is created
  447  only if the client has the appropriate permissions.
  448  • events that should be delivered (see §2.5).
  449  • the lock-delay (§2.4).
  450  • whether a new file or directory should (or must) be
  451  created. If a file is created, the caller may supply initial contents and initial ACL names. The return value
  452  indicates whether the file was in fact created.
  453  Close() closes an open handle. Further use of the handle is not permitted. This call never fails. A related call
  454  Poison() causes outstanding and subsequent operations
  455  on the handle to fail without closing it; this allows a client
  456  to cancel Chubby calls made by other threads without
  457  fear of deallocating the memory being accessed by them.
  458  The main calls that act on a handle are:
  459  GetContentsAndStat() returns both the contents and
  460  meta-data of a file. The contents of a file are read atomically and in their entirety. We avoided partial reads and
  461  writes to discourage large files. A related call GetStat()
  462  returns just the meta-data, while ReadDir() returns the
  463  names and meta-data for the children of a directory.
  464  SetContents() writes the contents of a file. Optionally, the client may provide a content generation number
  465  to allow the client to simulate compare-and-swap on a
  466  file; the contents are changed only if the generation number is current. The contents of a file are always written
  467  atomically and in their entirety. A related call SetACL()
  468  performs a similar operation on the ACL names associated with the node.

  469  340

  470  Delete() deletes the node if it has no children.
  471  Acquire(), TryAcquire(), Release() acquire and
  472  release locks.
  473  GetSequencer() returns a sequencer (§2.4) that describes any lock held by this handle.
  474  SetSequencer() associates a sequencer with a handle.
  475  Subsequent operations on the handle fail if the sequencer
  476  is no longer valid.
  477  CheckSequencer() checks whether a sequencer is
  478  valid (see §2.4).
  479  Calls fail if the node has been deleted since the handle was created, even if the file has been subsequently
  480  recreated. That is, a handle is associated with an instance
  481  of a file, rather than with a file name. Chubby may apply access control checks on any call, but always checks
  482  Open() calls (see §2.3).
  483  All the calls above take an operation parameter in addition to any others needed by the call itself. The operation parameter holds data and control information that
  484  may be associated with any call. In particular, via the
  485  operation parameter the client may:
  486  • supply a callback to make the call asynchronous,
  487  • wait for the completion of such a call, and/or
  488  • obtain extended error and diagnostic information.
  489  Clients can use this API to perform primary election
  490  as follows: All potential primaries open the lock file and
  491  attempt to acquire the lock. One succeeds and becomes
  492  the primary, while the others act as replicas. The primary
  493  writes its identity into the lock file with SetContents()
  494  so that it can be found by clients and replicas, which
  495  read the file with GetContentsAndStat(), perhaps in
  496  response to a file-modification event (§2.5). Ideally,
  497  the primary obtains a sequencer with GetSequencer(),

  499  they should confirm with CheckSequencer() that it is
  500  still the primary. A lock-delay may be used with services
  501  that cannot check sequencers (§2.4).

  502  2.7 Caching

  503  To reduce read traffic, Chubby clients cache file data
  504  and node meta-data (including file absence) in a consistent, write-through cache held in memory. The cache is
  505  maintained by a lease mechanism described below, and
  506  kept consistent by invalidations sent by the master, which
  507  keeps a list of what each client may be caching. The protocol ensures that clients see either a consistent view of
  508  Chubby state, or an error.
  509  When file data or meta-data is to be changed, the modification is blocked while the master sends invalidations
  510  for the data to every client that may have cached it; this
  511  mechanism sits on top of KeepAlive RPCs, discussed
  512  more fully in the next section. On receipt of an invalidation, a client flushes the invalidated state and acknowl-

  513  USENIX Association
=== PAGE 7 ===

  514  OSDI '06: 7th USENIX Symposium on Operating Systems Design and Implementation

  515  edges by making its next KeepAlive call. The modification proceeds only after the server knows that each
  516  client has invalidated its cache, either because the client
  517  acknowledged the invalidation, or because the client allowed its cache lease to expire.
  518  Only one round of invalidations is needed because the
  519  master treats the node as uncachable while cache invalidations remain unacknowledged. This approach allows
  520  reads always to be processed without delay; this is useful
  521  because reads greatly outnumber writes. An alternative
  522  would be to block calls that access the node during invalidation; this would make it less likely that over-eager
  523  clients will bombard the master with uncached accesses
  524  during invalidation, at the cost of occasional delays. If
  525  this were a problem, one could imagine adopting a hybrid
  526  scheme that switched tactics if overload were detected.
  527  The caching protocol is simple: it invalidates cached
  528  data on a change, and never updates it. It would be just
  529  as simple to update rather than to invalidate, but updateonly protocols can be arbitrarily inefficient; a client that
  530  accessed a file might receive updates indefinitely, causing an unbounded number of unnecessary updates.
  531  Despite the overheads of providing strict consistency,
  532  we rejected weaker models because we felt that programmers would find them harder to use. Similarly, mechanisms such as virtual synchrony that require clients to
  533  exchange sequence numbers in all messages were considered inappropriate in an environment with diverse preexisting communication protocols.
  534  In addition to caching data and meta-data, Chubby
  535  clients cache open handles. Thus, if a client opens a file
  536  it has opened previously, only the first Open() call necessarily leads to an RPC to the master. This caching is restricted in minor ways so that it never affects the semantics observed by the client: handles on ephemeral files
  537  cannot be held open if the application has closed them;
  538  and handles that permit locking can be reused, but cannot be used concurrently by multiple application handles.
  539  This last restriction exists because the client may use
  540  Close() or Poison() for their side-effect of cancelling
  541  outstanding Acquire() calls to the master.
  542  Chubby's protocol permits clients to cache locks—that
  543  is, to hold locks longer than strictly necessary in the hope
  544  that they can be used again by the same client. An event
  545  informs a lock holder if another client has requested a
  546  conflicting lock, allowing the holder to release the lock
  547  just when it is needed elsewhere (see §2.5).

  548  2.8 Sessions and KeepAlives

  549  A Chubby session is a relationship between a Chubby
  550  cell and a Chubby client; it exists for some interval of
  551  time, and is maintained by periodic handshakes called
  552  KeepAlives. Unless a Chubby client informs the master

  553  USENIX Association

  554  otherwise, the client's handles, locks, and cached data all
  555  remain valid provided its session remains valid. (However, the protocol for session maintenance may require
  556  the client to acknowledge a cache invalidation in order to
  557  maintain its session; see below.)
  558  A client requests a new session on first contacting the
  559  master of a Chubby cell. It ends the session explicitly
  560  either when it terminates, or if the session has been idle
  561  (with no open handles and no calls for a minute).
  562  Each session has an associated lease—an interval of
  563  time extending into the future during which the master
  564  guarantees not to terminate the session unilaterally. The
  565  end of this interval is called the session lease timeout.
  566  The master is free to advance this timeout further into
  567  the future, but may not move it backwards in time.
  568  The master advances the lease timeout in three circumstances: on creation of the session, when a master fail-over occurs (see below), and when it responds
  569  to a KeepAlive RPC from the client. On receiving a
  570  KeepAlive, the master typically blocks the RPC (does
  571  not allow it to return) until the client's previous lease interval is close to expiring. The master later allows the
  572  RPC to return to the client, and thus informs the client of
  573  the new lease timeout. The master may extend the timeout by any amount. The default extension is 12s, but an
  574  overloaded master may use higher values to reduce the
  575  number of KeepAlive calls it must process. The client
  576  initiates a new KeepAlive immediately after receiving
  577  the previous reply. Thus, the client ensures that there
  578  is almost always a KeepAlive call blocked at the master.
  579  As well as extending the client's lease, the KeepAlive
  580  reply is used to transmit events and cache invalidations
  581  back to the client. The master allows a KeepAlive to
  582  return early when an event or invalidation is to be delivered. Piggybacking events on KeepAlive replies ensures
  583  that clients cannot maintain a session without acknowledging cache invalidations, and causes all Chubby RPCs
  584  to flow from client to master. This simplifies the client,
  585  and allows the protocol to operate through firewalls that
  586  allow initiation of connections in only one direction.
  587  The client maintains a local lease timeout that is a conservative approximation of the master's lease timeout. It
  588  differs from the master's lease timeout because the client
  589  must make conservative assumptions both of the time its
  590  KeepAlive reply spent in flight, and the rate at which the
  591  master's clock is advancing; to maintain consistency, we
  592  require that the server's clock advance no faster than a
  593  known constant factor faster than the client's.
  594  If a client's local lease timeout expires, it becomes unsure whether the master has terminated its session. The
  595  client empties and disables its cache, and we say that its
  596  session is in jeopardy. The client waits a further interval
  597  called the grace period, 45s by default. If the client and
  598  master manage to exchange a successful KeepAlive be-

  736  code, which is exercised far less often than other parts of
  737  the system, has been a rich source of interesting bugs.

  738  2.10 Database implementation

  739  The first version of Chubby used the replicated version
  740  of Berkeley DB [20] as its database. Berkeley DB provides B-trees that map byte-string keys to arbitrary byte-

  741  USENIX Association

  742  string values. We installed a key comparison function
  743  that sorts first by the number of components in a path
  744  name; this allows nodes to by keyed by their path name,
  745  while keeping sibling nodes adjacent in the sort order.
  746  Because Chubby does not use path-based permissions, a
  747  single lookup in the database suffices for each file access.
  748  Berkeley DB's uses a distributed consensus protocol
  749  to replicate its database logs over a set of servers. Once
  750  master leases were added, this matched the design of
  751  Chubby, which made implementation straightforward.
  752  While Berkeley DB's B-tree code is widely-used and
  753  mature, the replication code was added recently, and has
  754  fewer users. Software maintainers must give priority to
  755  maintaining and improving their most popular product
  756  features. While Berkeley DB's maintainers solved the
  757  problems we had, we felt that use of the replication code
  758  exposed us to more risk than we wished to take. As a result, we have written a simple database using write ahead
  759  logging and snapshotting similar to the design of Birrell et al. [2]. As before, the database log is distributed
  760  among the replicas using a distributed consensus protocol. Chubby used few of the features of Berkeley DB,
  761  and so this rewrite allowed significant simplification of
  762  the system as a whole; for example, while we needed
  763  atomic operations, we did not need general transactions.

  764  2.11 Backup

  765  Every few hours, the master of each Chubby cell writes
  766  a snapshot of its database to a GFS file server [7] in a
  767  different building. The use of a separate building ensures
  768  both that the backup will survive building damage, and
  769  that the backups introduce no cyclic dependencies in the
  770  system; a GFS cell in the same building potentially might
  771  rely on the Chubby cell for electing its master.
  772  Backups provide both disaster recovery and a means
  773  for initializing the database of a newly replaced replica
  774  without placing load on replicas that are in service.

  775  2.12 Mirroring

  776  Chubby allows a collection of files to be mirrored from
  777  one cell to another. Mirroring is fast because the files
  778  are small and the event mechanism (§2.5) informs the
  779  mirroring code immediately if a file is added, deleted,
  780  or modified. Provided there are no network problems,
  781  changes are reflected in dozens of mirrors world-wide in
  782  well under a second. If a mirror is unreachable, it remains unchanged until connectivity is restored. Updated
  783  files are then identified by comparing their checksums.
  784  Mirroring is used most commonly to copy configuration files to various computing clusters distributed
  785  around the world. A special cell, named global, contains a subtree/ls/global/master that is mirrored to the

  797  3 Mechanisms for scaling

  798  Chubby's clients are individual processes, so Chubby
  799  must handle more clients than one might expect; we
  800  have seen 90,000 clients communicating directly with a
  801  Chubby master—far more than the number of machines
  802  involved. Because there is just one master per cell, and
  803  its machine is identical to those of the clients, the clients
  804  can overwhelm the master by a huge margin. Thus, the
  805  most effective scaling techniques reduce communication
  806  with the master by a significant factor. Assuming the
  807  master has no serious performance bug, minor improvements in request processing at the master have little effect. We use several approaches:
  808  • We can create an arbitrary number of Chubby cells;
  809  clients almost always use a nearby cell (found with
  810  DNS) to avoid reliance on remote machines. Our typical deployment uses one Chubby cell for a data centre
  811  of several thousand machines.
  812  • The master may increase lease times from the default
  813  12s up to around 60s when it is under heavy load, so
  814  it need process fewer KeepAlive RPCs. (KeepAlives
  815  are by far the dominant type of request (see 4.1), and
  816  failure to process them in time is the typical failure
  817  mode of an overloaded server; clients are largely insensitive to latency variation in other calls.)
  818  • Chubby clients cache file data, meta-data, the absence
  819  of files, and open handles to reduce the number of
  820  calls they make on the server.
  821  • We use protocol-conversion servers that translate the
  822  Chubby protocol into less-complex protocols such as
  823  DNS and others. We discuss some of these below.
  824  Here we describe two familiar mechanisms, proxies
  825  and partitioning, that we expect will allow Chubby to
  826  scale further. We do not yet use them in production,
  827  but they are designed, and may be used soon. We have
  828  no present need to consider scaling beyond a factor of
  829  five: First, there are limits on the number of machines
  830  one would wish to put in a data centre or make reliant on
  831  a single instance of a service. Second, because we use
  832  similar machines for Chubby clients and servers, hardware improvements that increase the number of clients
  833  per machine also increase the capacity of each server.

  834  344

  835  3.1 Proxies

  836  Chubby's protocol can be proxied (using the same protocol on both sides) by trusted processes that pass requests from other clients to a Chubby cell. A proxy
  837  can reduce server load by handling both KeepAlive and
  838  read requests; it cannot reduce write traffic, which passes
  839  through the proxy's cache. But even with aggressive
  840  client caching, write traffic constitutes much less than
  841  one percent of Chubby's normal workload (see §4.1),
  842  so proxies allow a significant increase in the number of
  843  clients. If a proxy handles Nproxy clients, KeepAlive
  844  traffic is reduced by a factor of Nproxy, which might be
  845  10 thousand or more. A proxy cache can reduce read
  846  traffic by at most the mean amount of read-sharing—a
  847  factor of around 10 (§4.1). But because reads constitute
  848  under 10% of Chubby's load at present, the saving in
  849  KeepAlive traffic is by far the more important effect.
  850  Proxies add an additional RPC to writes and first-time
  851  reads. One might expect proxies to make the cell temporarily unavailable at least twice as often as before, because each proxied client depends on two machines that
  852  may fail: its proxy and the Chubby master.
  853  Alert readers will notice that the fail-over strategy described in Section 2.9, is not ideal for proxies. We discuss this problem in Section 4.4.

  854  3.2 Partitioning

  855  As mentioned in Section 2.3, Chubby's interface was
  856  chosen so that the name space of a cell could be partitioned between servers. Although we have not yet
  857  needed it, the code can partition the name space by directory. If enabled, a Chubby cell would be composed of
  858  N partitions, each of which has a set of replicas and a
  859  master. Every node D/C in directory D would be stored
  860  on the partition P(D/C) = hash(D) mod N. Note that
  861  the meta-data for D may be stored on a different partition
  862  P(D) = hash(D0) mod N, where D0 is the parent of D.
  863  Partitioning is intended to enable large Chubby cells
  864  with little communication between the partitions. Although Chubby lacks hard links, directory modifiedtimes, and cross-directory rename operations, a few operations still require cross-partition communication:
  865  • ACLs are themselves files, so one partition may use
  866  another for permissions checks. However, ACL files
  867  are readily cached; only Open() and Delete() calls
  868  require ACL checks; and most clients read publicly
  869  accessible files that require no ACL.
  870  • When a directory is deleted, a cross-partition call may
  871  be needed to ensure that the directory is empty.
  872  Because each partition handles most calls independently
  873  of the others, we expect this communication to have only
  874  a modest impact on performance or availability.

  875  USENIX Association
=== PAGE 11 ===

  876  OSDI '06: 7th USENIX Symposium on Operating Systems Design and Implementation

  877  Unless the number of partitions N is large, one would
  878  expect that each client would contact the majority of the
  879  partitions. Thus, partitioning reduces read and write traffic on any given partition by a factor of N but does not
  880  necessarily reduce KeepAlive traffic. Should it be necessary for Chubby to handle more clients, our strategy
  881  involves a combination of proxies and partitioning.

  928  Now we briefly describe the typical causes of outages
  929  in our cells. If we assume (optimistically) that a cell is
  930  "up" if it has a master that is willing to serve, on a sample of our cells we recorded 61 outages over a period of
  931  a few weeks, amounting to 700 cell-days of data in total. We excluded outages due to maintenance that shut
  932  down the data centre. All other causes are included: network congestion, maintenance, overload, and errors due
  933  to operators, software, and hardware. Most outages were
  934  15s or less, and 52 were under 30s; most of our applications are not affected significantly by Chubby outages
  935  under 30s. The remaining nine outages were caused by
  936  network maintenance (4), suspected network connectivity problems (2), software errors (2), and overload (1).
  937  In a few dozen cell-years of operation, we have lost
  938  data on six occasions, due to database software errors
  939  (4) and operator error (2); none involved hardware error. Ironically, the operational errors involved upgrades
  940  to avoid the software errors. We have twice corrected
  941  corruptions caused by software in non-master replicas.
  942  Chubby's data fits in RAM, so most operations are
  943  cheap. Mean request latency at our production servers
  944  is consistently a small fraction of a millisecond regardless of cell load until the cell approaches overload,
  945  when latency increases dramatically and sessions are
  946  dropped. Overload typically occurs when many sessions
  947  (> 90, 000) are active, but can result from exceptional
  948  conditions: when clients made millions of read requests
  949  simultaneously (described in Section 4.3), and when a
  950  mistake in the client library disabled caching for some
  951  reads, resulting in tens of thousands of requests per second. Because most RPCs are KeepAlives, the server can
  952  maintain a low mean request latency with many active
  953  clients by increasing the session lease period (see §3).
  954  Group commit reduces the effective work done per request when bursts of writes arrive, but this is rare.
  955  RPC read latencies measured at the client are limited
  956  by the RPC system and network; they are under 1ms for
  957  a local cell, but 250ms between antipodes. Writes (which
  958  include lock operations) are delayed a further 5-10ms by
  959  the database log update, but by up to tens of seconds if a
  960  recently-failed client cached the file. Even this variability in write latency has little effect on the mean request
  961  latency at the server because writes are so infrequent.
  962  Clients are fairly insensitive to latency variation provided sessions are not dropped. At one point, we added
  963  artificial delays in Open() to curb abusive clients (see
  964  §4.5); developers noticed only when delays exceeded ten
  965  seconds and were applied repeatedly. We have found that
  966  the key to scaling Chubby is not server performance; reducing communication to the server can have far greater

  969  impact. No significant effort has been applied to tuning
  970  read/write server code paths; we checked that no egregious bugs were present, then focused on the scaling
  971  mechanisms that could be more effective. On the other

  973  the local Chubby cache, which a client may read thousands of times per second.

  974  4.2 Java clients

  975  Google's infrastructure is mostly in C++, but a growing
  976  number of systems are being written in Java [8]. This
  977  trend presented an unanticipated problem for Chubby,
  978  which has a complex client protocol and a non-trivial
  979  client-side library.
  980  Java encourages portability of entire applications at
  981  the expense of incremental adoption by making it somewhat irksome to link against other languages. The usual
  982  Java mechanism for accessing non-native libraries is
  983  JNI [15], but it is regarded as slow and cumbersome. Our
  984  Java programmers so dislike JNI that to avoid its use they
  985  prefer to translate large libraries into Java, and commit to
  986  supporting them.
  987  Chubby's C++ client library is 7000 lines (comparable
  988  with the server), and the client protocol is delicate. To
  989  maintain the library in Java would require care and expense, while an implementation without caching would
  990  burden the Chubby servers. Thus our Java users run
  991  copies of a protocol-conversion server that exports a simple RPC protocol that corresponds closely to Chubby's
  992  client API. Even with hindsight, it is not obvious how
  993  we might have avoided the cost of writing, running and
  994  maintaining this additional server.

  995  4.3 Use as a name service

  996  Even though Chubby was designed as a lock service, we
  997  found that its most popular use was as a name server.
  998  Caching within the normal Internet naming system,
  999  the DNS, is based on time. DNS entries have a timeto-live (TTL), and DNS data are discarded when they
 1000  have not been refreshed within that period. Usually it
 1001  is straightforward to pick a suitable TTL value, but if
 1002  prompt replacement of failed services is desired, the TTL
 1003  can become small enough to overload the DNS servers.
 1004  For example, it is common for our developers to
 1005  run jobs involving thousands of processes, and for each
 1006  process to communicate with every other, leading to a
 1007  quadratic number of DNS lookups. We might wish to
 1008  use a TTL of 60s; this would allow misbehaving clients
 1009  to be replaced without excessive delay and is not considered an unreasonably short replacement time in our
 1010  environment. In that case, to maintain the DNS caches

 1011  346

 1012  of a single job as small as 3 thousand clients would require 150 thousand lookups per second. (For comparison, a 2-CPU 2.6GHz Xeon DNS server might handle 50
 1013  thousand requests per second.) Larger jobs create worse
 1014  problems, and several jobs many be running at once. The
 1015  variability in our DNS load had been a serious problem
 1016  for Google before Chubby was introduced.
 1017  In contrast, Chubby's caching uses explicit invalidations so a constant rate of session KeepAlive requests
 1018  can maintain an arbitrary number of cache entries indefinitely at a client, in the absence of changes. A 2-CPU
 1019  2.6GHz Xeon Chubby master has been seen to handle
 1020  90 thousand clients communicating directly with it (no
 1021  proxies); the clients included large jobs with communication patterns of the kind described above. The ability
 1022  to provide swift name updates without polling each name
 1023  individually is so appealing that Chubby now provides
 1024  name service for most of the company's systems.
 1025  Although Chubby's caching allows a single cell to sustain a large number of clients, load spikes can still be
 1026  a problem. When we first deployed the Chubby-based
 1027  name service, starting a 3 thousand process job (thus
 1028  generating 9 million requests) could bring the Chubby
 1029  master to its knees. To resolve this problem, we chose to
 1030  group name entries into batches so that a single lookup
 1031  would return and cache the name mappings for a large
 1032  number (typically 100) of related processes within a job.
 1033  The caching semantics provided by Chubby are more
 1034  precise than those needed by a name service; name
 1035  resolution requires only timely notification rather than
 1036  full consistency. As a result, there was an opportunity
 1037  for reducing the load on Chubby by introducing a simple protocol-conversion server designed specifically for
 1038  name lookups. Had we foreseen the use of Chubby as a
 1039  name service, we might have chosen to implement full
 1040  proxies sooner than we did in order to avoid the need for
 1041  this simple, but nevertheless additional server.
 1042  One further protocol-conversion server exists: the
 1043  Chubby DNS server. This makes the naming data stored
 1044  within Chubby available to DNS clients. This server is
 1045  important both for easing the transition from DNS names
 1046  to Chubby names, and to accommodate existing applications that cannot be converted easily, such as browsers.

 1047  4.4 Problems with fail-over

 1048  The original design for master fail-over (§2.9) requires
 1049  the master to write new sessions to the database as they
 1050  are created. In the Berkeley DB version of the lock
 1051  server, the overhead of creating sessions became a problem when many processes were started at once. To avoid
 1052  overload, the server was modified to store a session in the
 1053  database not when it was first created, but instead when it
 1054  attempted its first modification, lock acquisition, or open

 1055  USENIX Association
=== PAGE 13 ===

 1056  OSDI '06: 7th USENIX Symposium on Operating Systems Design and Implementation

 1057  of an ephemeral file. In addition, active sessions were
 1058  recorded in the database with some probability on each
 1059  KeepAlive. Thus, the writes for read-only sessions were
 1060  spread out in time.
 1061  Though it was necessary to avoid overload, this optimization has the undesirable effect that young read-only
 1062  sessions may not be recorded in the database, and so may
 1063  be discarded if a fail-over occurs. Although such sessions hold no locks, this is unsafe; if all the recorded
 1064  sessions were to check in with the new master before the
 1065  leases of discarded sessions expired, the discarded sessions could then read stale data for a while. This is rare
 1066  in practice; in a large system it is almost certain that some
 1067  session will fail to check in, and thus force the new master to await the maximum lease time anyway. Nevertheless, we have modified the fail-over design both to avoid
 1068  this effect, and to avoid a complication that the current
 1069  scheme introduces to proxies.
 1070  Under the new design, we avoid recording sessions in
 1071  the database at all, and instead recreate them in the same
 1072  way that the master currently recreates handles (§2.9,¶8).
 1073  A new master must now wait a full worst-case lease timeout before allowing operations to proceed, since it cannot know whether all sessions have checked in (§2.9,¶6).
 1074  Again, this has little effect in practice because it is likely
 1075  that not all sessions will check in.
 1076  Once sessions can be recreated without on-disc state,
 1077  proxy servers can manage sessions that the master is not
 1078  aware of. An extra operation available only to proxies
 1079  allows them to change the session that locks are associated with. This permits one proxy to take over a client
 1080  from another when a proxy fails. The only further change
 1081  needed at the master is a guarantee not to relinquish locks
 1082  or ephemeral file handles associated with proxy sessions
 1083  until a new proxy has had a chance to claim them.

 1084  4.5 Abusive clients

 1085  Google's project teams are free to set up their own
 1086  Chubby cells, but doing so adds to their maintenance burden, and consumes additional hardware resources. Many
 1087  services therefore use shared Chubby cells, which makes
 1088  it important to isolate clients from the misbehaviour of
 1089  others. Chubby is intended to operate within a single company, and so malicious denial-of-service attacks
 1090  against it are rare. However, mistakes, misunderstandings, and the differing expectations of our developers
 1091  lead to effects that are similar to attacks.
 1092  Some of our remedies are heavy-handed. For example,
 1093  we review the ways project teams plan to use Chubby,
 1094  and deny access to the shared Chubby name space until
 1095  review is satisfactory. A problem with this approach is
 1096  that developers are often unable to predict how their services will be used in the future, and how use will grow.

 1097  USENIX Association

 1098  Readers will note the irony of our own failure to predict
 1099  how Chubby itself would be used.
 1100  The most important aspect of our review is to determine whether use of any of Chubby's resources (RPC
 1101  rate, disc space, number of files) grows linearly (or
 1102  worse) with number of users or amount of data processed
 1103  by the project. Any linear growth must be mitigated by
 1104  a compensating parameter that can be adjusted to reduce
 1105  the load on Chubby to reasonable bounds. Nevertheless
 1106  our early reviews were not thorough enough.
 1107  A related problem is the lack of performance advice in
 1108  most software documentation. A module written by one
 1109  team may be reused a year later by another team with
 1110  disastrous results. It is sometimes hard to explain to interface designers that they must change their interfaces
 1111  not because they are bad, but because other developers
 1112  may be less aware of the cost of an RPC.
 1113  Below we list some problem cases we encountered.
 1114  Lack of aggressive caching Originally, we did not appreciate the critical need to cache the absence of files,
 1115  nor to reuse open file handles. Despite attempts at education, our developers regularly write loops that retry
 1116  indefinitely when a file is not present, or poll a file by
 1117  opening it and closing it repeatedly when one might expect they would open the file just once.
 1118  At first we countered these retry-loops by introducing exponentially-increasing delays when an application
 1119  made many attempts to Open() the same file over a short
 1120  period. In some cases this exposed bugs that developers acknowledged, but often it required us to spend yet
 1121  more time on education. In the end it was easier to make
 1122  repeated Open() calls cheap.
 1123  Lack of quotas Chubby was never intended to be used
 1124  as a storage system for large amounts of data, and so it
 1125  has no storage quotas. In hindsight, this was na¨ıve.
 1126  One of Google's projects wrote a module to keep track
 1127  of data uploads, storing some meta-data in Chubby. Such
 1128  uploads occurred rarely and were limited to a small set of
 1129  people, so the space was bounded. However, two other
 1130  services started using the same module as a means for
 1131  tracking uploads from a wider population of users. Inevitably, these services grew until the use of Chubby was
 1132  extreme: a single 1.5MByte file was being rewritten in
 1133  its entirety on each user action, and the overall space
 1134  used by the service exceeded the space needs of all other
 1135  Chubby clients combined.
 1136  We introduced a limit on file size (256kBytes), and
 1137  encouraged the services to migrate to more appropriate storage systems. But it is difficult to make significant changes to production systems maintained by busy
 1138  people—it took approximately a year for the data to be
 1139  migrated elsewhere.
 1140  Publish/subscribe There have been several attempts
 1141  to use Chubby's event mechanism as a publish/subscribe

 1143  OSDI '06: 7th USENIX Symposium on Operating Systems Design and Implementation

 1144  system in the style of Zephyr [6]. Chubby's heavyweight
 1145  guarantees and its use of invalidation rather than update
 1146  in maintaining cache consistency make it a slow and inefficient for all but the most trivial publish/subscribe examples. Fortunately, all such uses have been caught before
 1147  the cost of redesigning the application was too large.

 1148  4.6 Lessons learned

 1149  Here we list lessons, and miscellaneous design changes
 1150  we might make if we have the opportunity:
 1151  Developers rarely consider availability We find that
 1152  our developers rarely think about failure probabilities,
 1153  and are inclined to treat a service like Chubby as though
 1154  it were always available. For example, our developers once built a system employing hundred of machines
 1155  that initiated recovery procedures taking tens of minutes
 1156  when Chubby elected a new master. This magnified the
 1157  consequences of a single failure by a factor of a hundred
 1158  both in time and the number of machines affected. We
 1159  would prefer developers to plan for short Chubby outages, so that such an event has little or no affect on their
 1160  applications. This is one of the arguments for coarsegrained locking, discussed in Section 2.1.
 1161  Developers also fail to appreciate the difference between a service being up, and that service being available
 1162  to their applications. For example, the global Chubby
 1163  cell (see §2.12), is almost always up because it is rare for
 1164  more than two geographically distant data centres to be
 1165  down simultaneously. However, its observed availability for a given client is usually lower than the observed
 1166  availability of the client's local Chubby cell. First, the local cell is less likely to be partitioned from the client, and
 1167  second, although the local cell may be down often due to
 1168  maintenance, the same maintenance affects the client directly, so Chubby's unavailability is not observed by the
 1169  client.
 1170  Our API choices can also affect the way developers
 1171  chose to handle Chubby outages. For example, Chubby
 1172  provides an event that allows clients to detect when a
 1173  master fail-over has taken place. The intent was for
 1174  clients to check for possible changes, as other events
 1175  may have been lost. Unfortunately, many developers
 1176  chose to crash their applications on receiving this event,
 1177  thus decreasing the availability of their systems substantially. We might have done better to send redundant "file
 1178  change" events instead, or even to ensure that no events
 1179  were lost during a fail-over.
 1180  At present we use three mechanisms to prevent developers from being over-optimistic about Chubby availability, especially that of the global cell. First, as previously mentioned (§4.5), we review how project teams
 1181  plan to use Chubby, and advise them against techniques
 1182  that would tie their availability too closely to Chubby's.

 1183  348

 1184  Second, we now supply libraries that perform some highlevel tasks so that developers are automatically isolated
 1185  from Chubby outages. Third, we use the post-mortem
 1186  of each Chubby outage as a means not only of eliminating bugs in Chubby and our operational procedures, but
 1187  of reducing the sensitivity of applications to Chubby's
 1188  availability—both can lead to better availability of our
 1189  systems overall.
 1190  Fine-grained locking could be ignored At the end of
 1191  Section 2.1 we sketched a design for a server that clients
 1192  could run to provide fine-grained locking. It is perhaps
 1193  a surprise that so far we have not needed to write such
 1194  a server; our developers typically find that to optimize
 1195  their applications, they must remove unnecessary communication, and that often means finding a way to use
 1196  coarse-grained locking.
 1197  Poor API choices have unexpected affects For the
 1198  most part, our API has evolved well, but one mistake
 1199  stands out. Our means for cancelling long-running calls
 1200  are the Close() and Poison() RPCs, which also discard
 1201  the server state for the handle. This prevents handles
 1202  that can acquire locks from being shared, for example,
 1203  by multiple threads. We may add a Cancel() RPC to
 1204  allow more sharing of open handles.
 1205  RPC use affects transport protocols KeepAlives are
 1206  used both for refreshing the client's session lease, and for
 1207  passing events and cache invalidations from the master
 1208  to the client. This design has the automatic and desirable
 1209  consequence that a client cannot refresh its session lease
 1210  without acknowledging cache invalidations.
 1211  This would seem ideal, except that it introduced a tension in our choice of protocol. TCP's back off policies
 1212  pay no attention to higher-level timeouts such as Chubby
 1213  leases, so TCP-based KeepAlives led to many lost sessions at times of high network congestion. We were
 1214  forced to send KeepAlive RPCs via UDP rather than
 1215  TCP; UDP has no congestion avoidance mechanisms, so
 1216  we would prefer to use UDP only when high-level timebounds must be met.
 1217  We may augment the protocol with an additional TCPbased GetEvent() RPC which would be used to communicate events and invalidations in the normal case,
 1218  used in the same way KeepAlives. The KeepAlive reply would still contain a list of unacknowledged events
 1219  so that events must eventually be acknowledged.

 1220  5 Comparison with related work

 1221  Chubby is based on well-established ideas. Chubby's
 1222  cache design is derived from work on distributed file systems [10]. Its sessions and cache tokens are similar in behaviour to those in Echo [17]; sessions reduce the overhead of leases [9] in the V system. The idea of exposing a general-purpose lock service is found in VMS [23],

 1223  USENIX Association
=== PAGE 15 ===

 1224  OSDI '06: 7th USENIX Symposium on Operating Systems Design and Implementation

 1225  though that system initially used a special-purpose highspeed interconnect that permitted low-latency interactions. Like its caching model, Chubby's API is based
 1226  on a file-system model, including the idea that a filesystem-like name space is convenient for more than just
 1227  files [18, 21, 22].
 1228  Chubby differs from a distributed file system such as
 1229  Echo or AFS [10] in its performance and storage aspirations: Clients do not read, write, or store large amounts
 1230  of data, and they do not expect high throughput or even
 1231  low-latency unless the data is cached. They do expect consistency, availability, and reliability, but these
 1232  attributes are easier to achieve when performance is less
 1233  important. Because Chubby's database is small, we are
 1234  able to store many copies of it on-line (typically five
 1235  replicas and a few backups). We take full backups multiple times per day, and via checksums of the database
 1236  state, we compare replicas with one another every few
 1237  hours. The weakening of the normal file system performance and storage requirements allows us to serve tens
 1238  of thousands of clients from a single Chubby master. By
 1239  providing a central point where many clients can share
 1240  information and co-ordinate activities, we have solved a
 1241  class of problems faced by our system developers.
 1242  The large number of file systems and lock servers described in the literature prevents an exhaustive comparison, so we provide details on one: we chose to compare
 1243  with Boxwood's lock server [16] because it was designed
 1244  recently, it too is designed to run in a loosely-coupled environment, and yet its design differs in various ways from
 1245  Chubby, some interesting and some incidental.
 1246  Chubby implements locks, a reliable small-file storage
 1247  system, and a session/lease mechanism in a single service. In contrast, Boxwood separates these into three:
 1248  a lock service, a Paxos service (a reliable repository
 1249  for state), and a failure detection service respectively.
 1250  The Boxwood system itself uses these three components
 1251  together, but another system could use these building
 1252  blocks independently. We suspect that this difference
 1253  in design stems from a difference in target audience.
 1254  Chubby was intended for a diverse audience and application mix; its users range from experts who create new
 1255  distributed systems, to novices who write administration
 1256  scripts. For our environment, a large-scale shared service with a familiar API seemed attractive. In contrast,
 1257  Boxwood provides a toolkit that (to our eyes, at least) is
 1258  appropriate for a smaller number of more sophisticated
 1259  developers working on projects that may share code but
 1260  need not be used together.
 1261  In many cases, Chubby provides a higher-level interface than Boxwood. For example, Chubby combines
 1262  the lock and file names spaces, while Boxwood's lock
 1263  names are simple byte sequences. Chubby clients cache
 1264  file state by default; a client of Boxwood's Paxos service

 1265  USENIX Association

 1266  could implement caching via the lock service, but would
 1267  probably use the caching provided by Boxwood itself.
 1268  The two systems have markedly different default parameters, chosen for different expectations: Each Boxwood failure detector is contacted by each client every 200ms with a timeout of 1s; Chubby's default lease
 1269  time is 12s and KeepAlives are exchanged every 7s.
 1270  Boxwood's subcomponents use two or three replicas to
 1271  achieve availability, while we typically use five replicas per cell. However, these choices alone do not suggest a deep design difference, but rather an indication of
 1272  how parameters in such systems must be adjusted to accommodate more client machines, or the uncertainties of
 1273  racks shared with other projects.
 1274  A more interesting difference is the introduction of
 1275  Chubby's grace period, which Boxwood lacks. (Recall that the grace period allows clients to ride out long
 1276  Chubby master outages without losing sessions or locks.
 1277  Boxwood's "grace period" is the equivalent of Chubby's
 1278  "session lease", a different concept.) Again, this difference is the result of differing expectations about scale
 1279  and failure probability in the two systems. Although
 1280  master fail-overs are rare, a lost Chubby lock is expensive for clients.
 1281  Finally, locks in the two systems are intended for different purposes. Chubby locks are heavier-weight, and
 1282  need sequencers to allow externals resources to be protected safely, while Boxwood locks are lighter-weight,
 1283  and intended primarily for use within Boxwood.

 1284  6 Summary

 1285  Chubby is a distributed lock service intended for coarsegrained synchronization of activities within Google's
 1286  distributed systems; it has found wider use as a name
 1287  service and repository for configuration information.
 1288  Its design is based on well-known ideas that have
 1289  meshed well: distributed consensus among a few replicas
 1290  for fault tolerance, consistent client-side caching to reduce server load while retaining simple semantics, timely
 1291  notification of updates, and a familiar file system interface. We use caching, protocol-conversion servers, and
 1292  simple load adaptation to allow it scale to tens of thousands of client processes per Chubby instance. We expect to scale it further via proxies and partitioning.
 1293  Chubby has become Google's primary internal name
 1294  service; it is a common rendezvous mechanism for systems such as MapReduce [4]; the storage systems GFS
 1295  and Bigtable use Chubby to elect a primary from redundant replicas; and it is a standard repository for files that
 1296  require high availability, such as access control lists.
