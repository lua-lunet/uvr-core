    8  Abstract

    9  We introduce optimistic crash consistency, a new approach to crash consistency in journaling file systems.
   10  Using an array of novel techniques, we demonstrate how
   11  to build an optimistic commit protocol that correctly
   12  recovers from crashes and delivers high performance.
   13  We implement this optimistic approach within a Linux
   14  ext4 variant which we call OptFS. We introduce two
   15  new file-system primitives, osync() and dsync(), that
   16  decouple ordering of writes from their durability. We
   17  show through experiments that OptFS improves performance for many workloads, sometimes by an order of
   18  magnitude; we confirm its correctness through a series
   19  of robustness tests, showing it recovers to a consistent
   20  state after crashes. Finally, we show that osync() and
   21  dsync() are useful in atomic file system and database
   22  update scenarios, both improving performance and meeting application-level consistency demands.

   23  1 Introduction

   24  Modern storage devices present a seemingly innocuous
   25  interface to clients. To read a block, one simply issues a
   26  low-level read command and specifies the address of the
   27  block (or set of blocks) to read; when the disk finishes
   28  the read, it is transferred into memory and any awaiting
   29  clients notified of the completion. A similar process is
   30  followed for writes.
   31  Unfortunately, the introduction of write buffering [28]
   32  in modern disks greatly complicates this apparently simple process. With write buffering enabled, disk writes
   33  may complete out of order, as a smart disk scheduler may
   34  reorder requests for performance [13, 24, 38]; further, the
   35  notification received after a write issue implies only that

   49  Out-of-order write completion, in particular, greatly
   50  complicates known techniques for recovering from system crashes. For example, modern journaling file systems such as Linux ext3, XFS, and NTFS all carefully
   51  orchestrate a sequence of updates to ensure that writes to
   52  main file-system structures and the journal reach disk in a
   53  particular order [22]; copy-on-write file systems such as
   54  LFS, btrfs, and ZFS also require ordering when updating
   55  certain structures. Without ordering, most file systems
   56  cannot ensure that state can be recovered after a crash [6].
   57  Write ordering is achieved in modern drives via expensive cache flush operations [30]; such flushes cause
   58  all buffered dirty data in the drive to be written to the
   59  surface (i.e., persisted) immediately. To ensure A is written before B, a client issues the write to A, and then a
   60  cache flush; when the flush returns, the client can safely
   61  assume that A reached the disk; the write to B can then
   62  be safely issued, knowing it will be persisted after A.
   63  Unfortunately, cache flushing is expensive, sometimes
   64  prohibitively so. Flushes make I/O scheduling less efficient, as the disk has fewer requests to choose from. A
   65  flush also unnecessarily forcesall previous writes to disk,
   66  whereas the requirements of the client may be less stringent. In addition, during a large cache flush, disk reads
   67  may exhibit extremely long latencies as they wait for
   68  pending writes to complete [26]. Finally, flushing conflates ordering and durability; if a client simply wishes
   69  to order one write before another, forcing the first write
   70  to disk is an expensive manner in which to achieve such
   71  an end. In short, the classic approach of flushing is pessimistic; it assumes a crash will occur and goes to great
   72  lengths to ensure that the disk is never in an inconsistent state via flush commands. The poor performance
   73  that results from pessimism has led some systems to disable flushing, apparently sacrificing correctness for performance; for example, the Linux ext3 default configuration did not flush caches for many years [8].
   74  Disabling flushes does not necessarily lead to file system inconsistency, but rather introduces it as a possibility. We refer to such an approach as probabilistic crash
   75  consistency, in which a crash might lead to file system inconsistency, depending on many factors, including workload, system and disk parameters, and the exact timing of

   76  the crash or power loss. In this paper, one of our first contributions is the careful study of probabilistic crash consistency, wherein we show which exact factors affect the
   77  odds that a crash will leave the file system inconsistent
   78  (§3). We show that for some workloads, the probabilistic
   79  approach rarely leaves the file system inconsistent.
   80  Unfortunately, a probabilistic approach is insufficient
   81  for many applications, where certainty in crash recovery is desired. Indeed, we also show, for some workloads, that the chances of inconsistency are high; to
   82  realize higher-level application-level consistency (i.e.,
   83  something a DBMS might desire), the file system must
   84  provide something more than probability and chance.
   85  Thus, in this paper, we introduce optimistic crash consistency, a new approach to building a crash-consistent
   86  journaling file system (§4). This optimistic approach
   87  takes advantage of the fact that in many cases, ordering
   88  can be achieved through other means and that crashes
   89  are rare events (similar to optimistic concurrency control [12, 16]). However, realizing consistency in an optimistic fashion is not without challenge; we thus develop
   90  a range of novel techniques, including a new extension of
   91  the transactional checksum [23] to detect data/metadata
   92  inconsistency, delayed reuse of blocks to avoid incorrect
   93  dangling pointers, and a selective data journaling technique to handle block overwrite correctly. The combination of these techniques leads to both high performance
   94  and deterministic consistency; in the rare event that a
   95  crash does occur, optimistic crash consistency either
   96  avoids inconsistency by design or ensures that enough
   97  information is present on the disk to detect and discard
   98  improper updates during recovery.
   99  We demonstrate the power of optimistic crash consistency through the design, implementation, and analysis
  100  of the optimistic file system (OptFS). OptFS builds upon
  101  the principles of optimistic crash consistency to implement optimistic journaling, which ensures that the file
  102  system is kept consistent despite crashes. Optimistic
  103  journaling is realized as a set of modifications to the
  104  Linux ext4 file system, but also requires a slight change
  105  in the disk interface to provide what we refer to as asynchronous durability notification, i.e., a notification when
  106  a write is persisted in addition to when the write has simply been received by the disk. We describe the details of
  107  our implementation (§5) and study its performance (§6),
  108  showing that for a range of workloads, OptFS significantly outperforms classic Linux ext4 with pessimistic
  109  journaling, and showing that OptFS performs almost
  110  identically to Linux ext4 with probabilistic journaling
  111  while ensuring crash consistency.
  112  Central to the performance benefits of OptFS is
  113  the separation of ordering and durability. By allowing applications to order writes without incurring a
  114  disk flush, and request durability when needed, OptFS

  115  enables application-level consistency at high performance. OptFS introduces two new file-system primitives: osync(), which ensures ordering between writes
  116  but only eventual durability, and dsync(), which ensures immediate durability as well as ordering.
  117  We show how these primitives provide a useful base
  118  on which to build higher-level application consistency
  119  semantics (§7). Specifically, we show how a document
  120  editing application can use osync() to implement the
  121  atomic update of a file (via a create and then atomic rename), and how the SQLite database management system can use file-system provided ordering to implement
  122  ordered transactions with eventual durability. We show
  123  that these primitives are sufficient for realizing useful
  124  application-level consistency at high performance.
  125  Of course, the optimistic approach, while useful in
  126  many scenarios, is not a panacea. If an application
  127  requires immediate, synchronous durability (instead of
  128  eventual, asynchronous durability with consistent ordering), an expensive cache flush is still required. In this
  129  case, applications can use dsync() to request durability (as well as ordering). However, by decoupling the
  130  durability of writes from their ordering, OptFS provides
  131  a useful middle ground, thus realizing high performance
  132  and meaningful crash consistency for many applications.

  133  2 Pessimistic Crash Consistency

  134  To understand the optimistic approach to journaling, we
  135  first describe standard pessimistic crash consistency in
  136  journaling file systems. To do so, we describe the necessary disk support (i.e., cache-flushing commands) and
  137  details on how such crash consistency operates. We then
  138  demonstrate the negative performance impact of cache
  139  flushing during pessimistic journaling.

  140  2.1 Disk Interface
  141  For the purposes of this discussion, we assume the presence of a disk-level cache flush command. In the ATA
  142  family of drives, this is referred to as the "flush cache"
  143  command; in SCSI drives, it is known as "synchronize
  144  cache". Both operations have similar semantics, forcing
  145  all pending dirty writes in the disk to be written to the
  146  surface. Note that a flush can be issued as a separate request, or as part of a write to a given block D; in the latter
  147  case, pending writes are flushed before the write to D.
  148  Some finer-grained controls also exist. For example, "force unit access" (FUA) commands read or write
  149  around the cache entirely. FUAs are often used in tandem with flush commands; for example, to write A before B, but to also ensure that both A and B are durable, a
  150  client might write A, then write B with both cache flushing and FUA enabled; this ensures that when B reaches
  151  the drive, A (and other dirty data) will be forced to disk;
  152  subsequently, B will be forced to disk due to the FUA.

  153  2.2 Pessimistic Journaling
  154  Given the disk interface described above, we now describe how a journaling file system safely commits data
  155  to disk in order to maintain consistency in the event of a
  156  system crash. We base our discussion on ordered-mode
  157  Linux ext3 and ext4 [34, 35], though much of what we
  158  say is applicable to other journaling file systems such as
  159  SGI XFS [32], Windows NTFS [27], and IBM JFS [3].
  160  In ordered mode, file-system metadata is journaled to
  161  maintain its consistency; data is not journaled, as writing
  162  each data block twice reduces performance substantially.
  163  When an application updates file-system state, either
  164  metadata, user data, or (often) both need to be updated in
  165  a persistent manner. For example, when a user appends
  166  a block to a file, a new data block (D) must be written to
  167  disk (at some point); in addition, various pieces of metadata (M) must be updated as well, including the file's
  168  inode and a bitmap marking the block as allocated.
  169  We refer to the atomic update of metadata to the journal as a transaction. Before committing a transaction T x
  170  to the journal, the file system first writes any data blocks
  171  (D) associated with the transaction to their final destinations; writing data before transaction commit ensures
  172  that committed metadata does not point to garbage. After these data writes complete, the file system uses the
  173  journal to log metadata updates; we refer to these journal
  174  writes as JM. After these writes are persisted, the file system issues a write to a commit block (JC ); when the disk
  175  persists JC, the transaction T x is said to be committed.
  176  Finally, after the commit, the file system is free to update
  177  the metadata blocks in place (M); if a crash occurs during
  178  this checkpointing process, the file system can recover
  179  simply by scanning the journal and replaying committed
  180  transactions. Details can be found elsewhere [22, 35].
  181  We thus have the following set of ordered writes that
  182  must take place: D before JM before JC before M, or
  183  more simply: D → JM → JC → M. Note that D, JM ,
  184  and M can represent more than a single block (in larger
  185  transactions), whereas JC is always a single sector (for
  186  the sake of write atomicity). To achieve this ordering,
  187  the file system issues a cache flush wherever order is required (i.e., where there is a → symbol).
  188  Optimizations to this protocol have been suggested in
  189  the literature, some of which have been realized in Linux
  190  ext4. For example, some have noted that the ordering
  191  between data and journaled metadata (D → JM) is superfluous; removing that ordering can sometimes improve
  192  performance (D|JM → JC → M) [22].
  193  Others have suggested a "transactional checksum" [23] which can be used to remove the ordering between the journal metadata and journal commit (JM and
  194  JC). In the normal case, the file system cannot issue JM
  195  and JC together, because the drive might reorder them; in
  196  that case, JC might hit the disk first, at which point a system crash (or power loss) would leave that transaction
  197  in a seemingly committed state but with garbage contents. By computing a checksum over the entire transaction and placing its value in JC, the writes to JM and JC
  198  can be issued together, improving performance; with the
  199  checksum present, crash recovery can avoid replay of improperly committed transactions. With this optimization,
  200  the ordering is D → JM|JC → M (where the bar over the
  201  journal updates indicates their protection via checksum).
  202  Interestingly, these two optimizations do not combine,
  203  i.e., D|JM |JC → M is not correct; if the file system issues D, JM , and JC together, it is possible that JM and
  204  JC reach the disk first. In this case, the metadata commits before the data; if a crash occurs before the data
  205  is written, an inode (or indirect block) in the committed transaction could end up pointing to garbage data.
  206  Oddly, ext4 allows this situation with the "right" set of
  207  mount options.
  208  We should note that one other important ordering exists among updates, specifically the order between transactions; journaling file systems assume transactions are
  209  committed to disk in order (i.e., T xi → T xi+1) [35]. Not
  210  following this ordering could lead to odd results during
  211  crash recovery. For example, a block B could have been
  212  freed in T xi, and then reused in T xi+1; in this case, a
  213  crash after T xi+1 committed but before T xi did would
  214  lead to a state where B is allocated to two files.
  215  Finally, and most importantly, we draw attention to the
  216  pessimistic nature of this approach. Whenever ordering
  217  is required, an expensive cache flush is issued, thus forcing all pending writes to disk, when perhaps only a subset of them needed to be flushed. In addition, the flushes
  218  are issued even though the writes may have gone to disk
  219  in the correct order anyhow, depending on scheduling,
  220  workload, and other details; flushes induce extra work
  221  that may not be necessary. Finally, and perhaps most
  222  harmful, is the fact that the burden of flushes is added despite the fact that crashes are rare, thus exacting a heavy
  223  cost in anticipation of an extremely occasional event.

  256  3 Probabilistic Crash Consistency

  257  Given the potential performance gains, practitioners
  258  sometimes forgo the safety provided by correct implementations that issue flushes and choose to disable
  259  flushes [8]. In this fast mode, a risk of file-system inconsistency is introduced; if a crash occurs at an untimely
  260  point in the update sequence, and blocks have been reordered across ordering points, crash recovery as run by
  261  the file system will result in an inconsistent file system.
  262  In some cases, practitioners observed that skipping
  263  flush commands sometimes did not lead to observable inconsistency, despite the presence of (occasional) crashes.
  264  Such commentary led to a debate within the Linux community as to underlying causes. Long-time kernel developer Theodore Ts'o hypothesized why such consistency
  265  was often achieved despite the lack of ordering enforcement by the file system [33]:
  266  I suspect the real reason why we get away with
  267  it so much with ext3 is that the journal is usually contiguous on disk, hence, when you write
  268  to the journal, it's highly unlikely that commit
  269  block will be written and the blocks before the
  270  commit block have not. ... The most important
  271  reason, though, is that the blocks which are
  272  dirty don't get flushed out to disk right away!

  274  orderings: JM → JC and JC → M. In the first case, Ts'o
  275  notes that the disk is likely to commit JC to disk after
  276  JM even without an intervening flush (note that this is
  277  without the presence of transactional checksums) due to
  278  layout and scheduling; disks are simply unlikely to reorder two writes that are contiguous. In the second case,
  279  Ts'o notes that JC → M often holds without a flush due

  305  3.1 Quantifying Probabilistic Consistency
  306  Unfortunately, probabilistic consistency is not well understood. To shed light on this issue, we quantify how
  307  often inconsistency arises without flushing via simulation. To do so, we disable flushing in Linux ext4 and
  308  extract block-level traces underneath ext4 across a range
  309  of workloads. We analyze the traces carefully to determine the chances of an inconsistency occurring due to
  310  a crash. Our analysis is done via a simulator built atop
  311  DiskSim [4], which enables us to model complex disk
  312  behaviors (e.g., scheduling, caching).
  313  The main output of our simulations is a determination
  314  of when a window of vulnerability (W ) arises, and for
  315  how long such windows last. Such a window occurs due
  316  to reordering. For example, if A should be written to
  317  disk before B, but B is written at time t1 and A written at
  318  t2, the state of the system is vulnerable to inconsistency
  319  in the time period between, W = t2 −t1. If the system
  320  crashes during this window, the file system will be left
  321  in an inconsistent state; conversely, once the latter block
  322  (A) is written, there is no longer any concern.
  323  Given a workload and a disk model, it is thus possible
  324  to quantify the probability of inconsistency (Pinc) by dividing the total time spent in windows of vulnerability by
  325  the total run time of the workload (Pinc =∪Wi/tworkload);
  326  Figure 2 shows an example. Note that when a workload
  327  is run on a file system with cache-flushing enabled, Pinc
  328  is always zero.

  448  3.3 Summary

  449  The classic approach to journaling is overly pessimistic,
  450  forcing writes to persistent storage often when only ordering is desired. As a result, users have sometimes
  451  turned to probabilistic journaling, taking their chances
  452  with consistency in order to gain more performance. We
  453  have carefully studied which factors affect the consistency of the probabilistic approach, and shown that for
  454  some workloads, it works fairly well; unfortunately, for
  455  other workloads with a high number of random-write
  456  I/Os, or where the application itself forces traffic to disk,
  457  the probability of inconsistency becomes high. As devices become more sophisticated, and can handle a large
  458  number of outstanding requests, the odds that a crash will
  459  cause inconsistency increases. Thus, to advance beyond
  460  the probabilistic approach, a system must include machinery to either avoid situations that lead to inconsistency, or be able to detect and recover when such occurrences arise. We now describe one such approach:
  461  optimistic crash consistency.

  462  4 Optimistic Crash Consistency

  463  Given that journaling with probabilistic consistency often gives consistent results even in the presence of system crashes, we note a new opportunity. The goal of
  464  optimistic crash consistency, as realized in an optimistic
  465  journaling system, is to commit transactions to persistent storage in a manner that maintains consistency to the
  466  same extent as pessimistic journaling, but with nearly the
  467  same performance as with probabilistic consistency. Optimistic journaling requires minimal changes to current
  468  disk interfaces and the journaling layer; in particular, our
  469  approach does not require changes to file-system structures outside of the journal (e.g., backpointers [6]).
  470  To describe optimistic crash consistency and journaling, we begin by describing the intuition behind optimistic techniques. Optimistic crash consistency is based
  471  on two main ideas. First, checksums can remove the need
  472  for ordering writes. Optimistic crash consistency eliminates the need for ordering during transaction commit
  473  by generalizing metadata transactional checksums [23]
  474  to include data blocks. During recovery, transactions are
  475  discarded upon checksum mismatch.
  476  Second, asynchronous durability notifications are used
  477  to delay checkpointing a transaction until it has been
  478  committed durably. Fortunately, this delay does not affect application performance, as applications block until
  479  the transaction is committed, not until it is checkpointed.
  480  Additional techniques are required for correctness in scenarios such as block reuse and overwrite.
  481  We first propose an additional notification that disk
  482  drives should expose. We then explain how optimistic
  483  journaling provides different properties to preserve the
  484  consistency semantics of ordered journaling. We show
  485  that these properties can be achieved using a combination
  486  of optimistic techniques. We also describe an additional
  487  optimistic technique which enables optimistic journaling
  488  to provide consistency equivalent to data journaling.

  489  4.1 Asynchronous Durability Notification
  490  The current interface to the disk for ensuring that write
  491  operations are performed in a specified order is pessimistic: the upper-level file system tells the lower-level
  492  disk when it must flush its cache (or certain blocks) and
  493  the disk must then promptly do so. However, the actual
  494  ordering and durability of writes to the platter does not
  495  matter, unless there is a crash. Therefore, the current interface is overly constraining and limits I/O performance.
  496  Rather than requiring the disk to obey ordering and
  497  durability commands from the layer above, we propose
  498  that the disk be freed to perform reads and writes in
  499  the order that optimizes its scheduling and performance.
  500  Thus, the performance of the disk is optimized for the
  501  common case in which there is no crash.
=== PAGE 7 ===

  502  Given that the file system must still be able to guarantee consistency and durability in the event of a crash, we
  503  propose a minimal extension to the disk interface. With
  504  an asynchronous durability notification the disk informs
  505  the upper-level client that a specific write request has
  506  completed and is now guaranteed to be durable. Thus
  507  there will be two notifications from the disk: first when
  508  the disk has received the write and later when the write
  509  has been persisted. Some interfaces, such as Forced Unit
  510  Access (FUA), provide a single, synchronous durability
  511  notification: the drive receives the request and indicates
  512  completion when the request has been persisted [14, 37].
  513  Tagged Queuing allows a limited number of requests to
  514  be outstanding at a given point of time [15, 37]. Unfortunately, many drives do not implement tagged queuing
  515  and FUA reliably [18]. Furthermore, a request tagged
  516  with FUA also implies urgency, prompting some implementations to force the request to disk immediately.
  517  While a correct implementation of tagged queuing and
  518  FUA may suffice for optimistic crash consistency, we
  519  feel that an interface that decouples request acknowledgement from persistence enables higher levels of I/O
  520  concurrency and thus provides a better foundation on
  521  which to build OptFS.

  522  4.2 Optimistic Consistency Properties
  523  As described in Section 2.2, ordered journaling mode
  524  involves the following writes for each transaction: data
  525  blocks, D, to in-place locations on disk; metadata blocks
  526  to the journal, JM; a commit block to the journal, JC; and
  527  finally, a checkpoint of the metadata to its in-place location, M. We refer to writes belonging to a particular
  528  transaction i with the notation :i; for example, the journaled metadata of transaction i is denoted JM :i.
  529  Ordered journaling mode ensures several properties.
  530  First, metadata written in transaction T x:i+1 cannot be
  531  observed unless metadata from transaction T x:i is also
  532  observed. Second, it is not possible for metadata to point
  533  to invalid data. These properties are maintained by the
  534  recovery process and how writes are ordered. If a crash
  535  occurs after the transaction is properly committed (i.e.,
  536  D, JM , and JC are all durably written), but before M is
  537  written, then the recovery process can replay the transaction so that M is written to its in-place location. If a crash
  538  occurs before the transaction is completed, then ordered
  539  journaling ensures that no in-place metadata related to
  540  this transaction was updated.
  541  Optimistic journaling allows the disk to perform writes
  542  in any order it chooses, but ensures that in the case of a
  543  crash, the necessary consistency properties are upheld for
  544  ordered transactions. To give the reader some intuition
  545  for why particular properties are sufficient for ordered
  546  journaling semantics, we walk through the example in

  600  4.3 Optimistic Techniques
  601  The behavior of optimistic journaling described above
  602  can be ensured with a set of optimistic techniques: inorder journal recovery and release, checksums, background writes after notification, reuse after notification,
  603  and selective data journaling. We now describe each.
  604  4.3.1 In-Order Journal Recovery
  605  The most basic technique for preserving the correct ordering of writes after a crash occurs during the journal
  606  recovery process itself. The recovery process reads the
  607  journal to observe which transactions were made durable
  608  and it simply discards or ignores any write operations
  609  that occurred out of the desired ordering.
  610  The correction that optimistic journaling applies is to
  611  ensure that if any part of a transaction T x:i was not correctly or completely made durable, then neither transaction T x:i nor any following transaction T x: j where j >i
  612  is left durable. Thus, journal recovery must proceed inorder, sequentially scanning the journal and performing
  613  checkpoints in-order, stopping at the first transaction that
  614  is not complete upon disk. The in-order recovery process
  615  will use the checksums described below to determine if
  616  a transaction is written correctly and completely.
  617  4.3.2 In-Order Journal Release
  618  Given that completed, durable journal transactions define
  619  the write operations that are durable on disk, optimistic
  620  journaling must ensure that journal transactions are not
  621  freed (or overwritten) until all corresponding checkpoint
  622  writes (of metadata) are confirmed as durable.
  623  Thus, optimistic journaling must wait until it has been
  624  notified by the disk that the checkpoint writes corresponding to this transaction are durable. At this point,
  625  optimistic journaling knows that the transaction need not
  626  be replayed if the system crashes; therefore, the transaction can be released. To preserve the property that
  627  T x:i+1 is made durable only if T x:i is durable, transactions must be freed in order.
  628  4.3.3 Checksums
  629  Checksums are a well-known technique for detecting
  630  data corruption and lost writes [20, 31]. A checksum can
  631  also be used to detect whether or not a write related to

  632  a specific transaction has occurred. Specifically, checksums can optimistically "enforce" two orderings: that
  633  the journal commit block (JC ) persists only after metadata to the journal (JM ) and after data blocks to their inplace location (D). This technique for ensuring metadata
  634  is durably written to the journal in its entirety has been
  635  referred to as transactional checksumming [23]; in this
  636  approach, a checksum is calculated over JM and placed
  637  in JC. If a crash occurs during the commit process, the
  638  recovery procedure can reliably detect the mismatch between JM and the checksum in JC and not replay that
  639  transaction (or any transactions following). To identify
  640  this particular instance of transactional checksumming
  641  we refer to it as metadata transactional checksumming.
  642  A similar, but more involved, version of data transactional checksumming can be used to ensure that data
  643  blocks D are written in their entirety as part of the transaction. Collecting these data blocks and calculating their
  644  checksums as they are dirtied in main memory is more
  645  involved than performing the checksums over JM , but the
  646  basic idea is the same. With the data checksums and their
  647  on-disk block addresses stored in JC, the journal recovery process can abort transactions upon mismatch. Thus,
  648  data transactional checksums enable optimistic journaling to ensure that metadata is not checkpointed if the
  649  corresponding data was not durably written.
  650  4.3.4 Background Write after Notification
  651  An important optimistic technique ensures that the
  652  checkpoint of the metadata (M) occurs after the preceding writes to the data and the journal (i.e., D, JM , and
  653  JC ). While pessimistic journaling guaranteed this behavior with a flush after JC, optimistic journaling explicitly
  654  postpones the checkpoint write of metadata M until it
  655  has been notified that all previous transactions have been
  656  durably completed. Note that it is not sufficient for M to
  657  occur after only JC; D and JM must precede M as well
  658  since optimistic journaling must ensure that the entire
  659  transaction is valid and can be replayed if any of the inplace metadata M is written. Similarly, M:i+1 must be
  660  postponed until all transactions T x:i have been durably
  661  committed to ensure that M:i +1 is not durable if M:i
  662  cannot be replayed. We note that M:i+1 does not need
  663  to wait for M:i to complete, but must instead wait for the
  664  responsible transaction T x:i to be durable.
  665  Checkpointing is one of the few points in the optimistic journaling protocol where the file system must
  666  wait to issue a particular write until a specific set of
  667  writes have completed. However, this particular waiting is not likely to impact performance because checkpointing occurs in the background. Subsequent writes
  668  by applications will be placed in later transactions and
  669  these journal updates can be written independently of any
  670  other outstanding writes; journal writes do not need to
=== PAGE 9 ===

  671  wait for previous checkpoints or transactions. Therefore,
  672  even applications waiting for journal writes (e.g., by calling fsync()) will not observe the checkpoint latency.
  673  For this reason, waiting for the asynchronous durability notification before a background checkpoint is fundamentally more powerful than the pessimistic approach of
  674  sending an ordering command to the disk (i.e., a cache
  675  flush). With a traditional ordering command, the disk
  676  is not able to postpone checkpoint writes across multiple transactions. On the other hand, the asynchronous
  677  durability notification command does not artificially constrain the ordering of writes and gives more flexibility to
  678  the disk so that it can best cache and schedule writes; the
  679  command also provides the needed information to the file
  680  system so that it can allow independent writes to proceed
  681  while appropriately holding back dependent writes.
  682  4.3.5 Reuse after Notification
  683  The preceding techniques were sufficient for handling
  684  the cases where blocks were not reused across transactions. The difficulty occurs with ordered journaling because data writes are performed to their in-place locations, potentially overwriting data on disk that is still referenced by durable metadata from previous transactions.
  685  Therefore, additional optimistic techniques are needed
  686  to ensure that durable metadata from earlier transactions
  687  never points to incorrect data blocks changed in later
  688  transactions. This is a security issue: if user A deletes
  689  their file, and then the deleted block becomes part of user
  690  B's file, a crash should not lead to user A being able to
  691  view user B's data. This is also one of the update dependency rules required for Soft Updates [25], but optimistic
  692  journaling enforces this rule with a different technique:
  693  reuse after notification.
  694  To understand the motivation for this technique, consider the steps when a data block DA is freed from one
  695  file MA and allocated to another file, MB and rewritten
  696  with the contents DB. Depending on how writes are reordered to disk, a durable version of MA may point to the
  697  erroneous content of DB.
  698  This problem can be fixed with transactions as follows.
  699  First, the freeing of DA and update to MA, denoted MA′ ,
  700  is written as part of a transaction JM ′ :i; the allocation
  701  A
  702  of DB to MB is written in a later transaction as DB:i+1
  703  and JM :i+1. Pessimistic journaling ensures that JM ′ :i
  704  BA
  705  occurs before DB:i +1 with a traditional flush between
  706  every transaction. The optimistic techniques introduced
  707  so far are not sufficient to provide this guarantee because
  708  the writes to DB:i+1 in their in-place locations cannot
  709  be recovered or rolled back if M′ is lost (even if there is
  710  A
  711  a checksum mismatch and transactions T x:i or T x:i+1
  712  are found to be incomplete).
  713  Optimistic journaling guarantees that JM ′ :i occurs beA
  714  fore DB:i +1 by ensuring that data block DA is not reallocated to another file until the file system has been notified by the disk that JM ′ :i has been durably written; at
  715  A
  716  this point, the data block DA is "durably free." When the
  717  file MB must be allocated a new data block, the optimistic
  718  file system allocates a "durably-free" data block that is
  719  known to not be referenced by any other files; finding a
  720  durably-free data block is straight-forward given the proposed asynchronous durability notification from disks.
  721  Performing reuse only after notification is unlikely to
  722  cause the file system to wait or to harm performance.
  723  Unless the file system is nearly 100% full, there should
  724  always exist a list of data blocks that are known to be
  725  durably free; under normal operating conditions, the file
  726  system is unlikely to need to wait to be informed by the
  727  disk that a particular data block is available.
  728  4.3.6 Selective Data Journaling
  729  Our final optimistic technique selectively journals data
  730  blocks that have been overwritten. This technique allows
  731  optimistic journaling to provide data journaling consistency semantics instead of ordered journaling semantics.
  732  A special case of an update dependency occurs when
  733  a data block is overwritten in a file and the metadata for
  734  that file (e.g., size) must be updated consistently. Optimistic journaling could handle this using reuse after notification: a new durably-free data block is allocated to the
  735  file and written to disk (DB: j), and then the new metadata is journaled (JM : j). The drawback of this approach
  736  B
  737  is that the file system takes on the behavior of a copy-onwrite file system and loses some of the locality benefits of
  738  an update-in-place file system [21]; since optimistic journaling forces a durably-free data block to be allocated,
  739  a file that was originally allocated contiguously and provided high sequential read and write throughput may lose
  740  its locality for certain random-update workloads.
  741  If update-in-place is desired for performance, a different technique can be used: selective data journaling.
  742  Data journaling places both metadata and data in the
  743  journal and both are then updated in-place at checkpoint
  744  time. The attractive property of data journaling is that inplace data blocks are not overwritten until the transaction
  745  is checkpointed; therefore, data blocks can be reused if
  746  their metadata is also updated in the same transaction.
  747  The disadvantage of data journaling is that every data
  748  block is written twice (once in the journal, JD, and once
  749  in its checkpointed in-place location, D) and therefore often has worse performance than ordered journaling [22].
  750  Selective data journaling allows ordered journaling to
  751  be used for the common case and data journaling only
  752  when data blocks are repeatedly overwritten within the
  753  same file and the file needs to maintain its original layout
  754  on disk. In selective data journaling, the checkpoint of
  755  both D and M simply waits for durable notification of all
  756  the journal writes (JD, JM, and JC ).

  789  4.4 Durability vs. Consistency
  790  Optimistic journaling uses an array of novel techniques
  791  to ensure that writes to disk are properly ordered, or that
  792  enough information exists on disk to recover from an untimely crash when writes are issued out of order; the
  793  result is file-system consistency and proper ordering of
  794  writes, but without guarantees of durability. However,
  795  some applications may wish to force writes to stable storage for the sake of durability, not ordering. In this case,
  796  something more than optimistic ordering is needed; the
  797  file system must either wait for such writes to be persisted (via an asynchronous durability notification) or issue flushes to force the issue. To separate these cases, we
  798  believe two calls should be provided; an "ordering" sync,
  799  osync(), guarantees ordering between writes, while a
  800  "durability" sync, dsync(), ensures when it returns that
  801  pending writes have been persisted.
  802  We now define and compare the guarantees given by
  803  osync() and dsync(). Assume the user makes a series of writes W1,W2, ...,Wn. If no osync() or dsync()
  804  calls are made, there is no guarantee as to file-system
  805  state after a crash: any or all of the updates may be lost,
  806  and updates may be applied out of order, i.e., W2 may be
  807  applied without W1.
  808  Now consider when every write is followed by
  809  dsync(), i.e., W1,d1,W2,d2, ...,Wn,dn. If a crash happens after di, the file system will recover to a state with
  810  W1,W2, ...,Wi applied.
  811  If every write was followed by osync(), i.e.,
  812  W1,o1,W2,o2, ...,Wn,on, and a crash happens after oi, the
  813  file system will recover to a state with W1,W2, ...,Wi−k
  814  applied, where the last k writes had not been made
  815  durable before the crash. We term this eventual durability. Thus osync() provides prefix semantics [36], ensuring that users always see a consistent version of the
  816  file system, though the data may be stale. Prior research
  817  indicates that this is useful in many domains [7].

  818  5 Implementation of OptFS

  819  We have implemented the Optimistic File System
  820  (OptFS) inside Linux 3.2, based on the principles outlined before (§4), as a variant of the ext4 file system, with
  821  additional changes to the JBD2 journaling layer and virtual memory subsystem.

  822  5.1 Asynchronous Durability Notifications
  823  Since current disks do not implement the proposed asynchronous durability notification interface, OptFS uses an
  824  approximation: durability timeouts. Durability timeouts
  825  represent the maximum time interval that the disk can
  826  delay committing a write request to the non-volatile platter. When a write for block A is received at time T by
  827  the disk, the block must be made durable by time T+
  828  TD. The value of TD is specific to each disk, and must be
  829  exported by the disk to higher levels in the storage stack.
  830  Upon expiration of the time interval TD, OptFS considers the block to be durable; this is equivalent to the disk
  831  notifying OptFS after TD seconds. Note that to account
  832  for other delays in the I/O subsystem, TD is measured
  833  from the time the write is acknowledged by the disk, and
  834  not from the time the write is issued by the file system.
  835  This approximation is limiting; TD might overestimate
  836  the durability interval, leading to performance problems
  837  and memory pressure; TD might underestimate the durability interval, comprising consistency. OptFS errs towards safety and sets TD to be 30 seconds.
=== PAGE 11 ===

  838  5.2 Handling Data Blocks
  839  OptFS does not journal all data blocks: newly allocated
  840  data blocks are only checksummed; their contents are not
  841  stored in the journal. This complicates journal recovery as data-block contents may change after the checksum was recorded. Due to selective data journaling, a
  842  data block that is not journaled (as it is newly allocated)
  843  in one transaction might be overwritten in the following
  844  transaction and therefore journaled. For example, consider data block D with content A belonging to T x1. The
  845  checksum A will be recorded in T x1. D is overwritten by
  846  T x2, with content B. Though this sequence is valid, the
  847  checksum A in T x1 will not match the content B in D.
  848  This necessitates individual block checksums, since
  849  checksum mismatch of a single block is not a problem
  850  if the block belongs to a later valid transaction. In contrast, since the frozen state of metadata blocks are stored
  851  in the journal, checksumming over the entire set is sufficient for metadata transactional checksums. We explain
  852  how OptFS handles this during recovery shortly.
  853  OptFS does not immediately write out checksummed
  854  data blocks; they are collected in memory and written in
  855  large batches upon transaction commit. This increases
  856  performance in some workloads.

  857  5.3 Optimistic Techniques
  858  We now describe the implementation of the optimistic
  859  journaling techniques. We also describe how OptFS reverts to more traditional mechanisms in some circumstances (e.g., when the journal runs out of space).
  860  In-Order Journal Recovery: OptFS recovers transactions in the order they were committed to the journal. A
  861  transaction can be replayed only if all its data blocks belong to valid transactions, and the checksum computed
  862  over metadata blocks matches that in the commit block.
  863  OptFS performs recovery in two passes: the first pass
  864  linearly scans the journal, compiling a list of data blocks
  865  with checksum mismatches and the first journal transaction that contained each block. If a later valid transaction
  866  matches the block checksum, the block is deleted from
  867  the list. At the end of the scan, the earliest transaction in
  868  the list is noted. The next pass performs journal recovery
  869  until the faulting transaction, thus ensuring consistency.
  870  OptFS journal recovery might take longer than ext4
  871  recovery since OptFS might need to read data blocks off
  872  non-contiguous disk locations while ext4 only needs to
  873  read the contiguous journal to perform recovery.
  874  In-Order Journal Release: When the virtual memory
  875  (VM) subsystem informs OptFS that checkpoint blocks
  876  have been acknowledged at time T, OptFS sets the transaction cleanup time as T +TD, after which it is freed from
  877  the journal.
  878  When the journal is running out of space, it may not
  879  be optimal to wait for the durability timeout interval before freeing up journal blocks. Under memory pressure,
  880  OptFS may need to free memory buffers of checkpoint
  881  blocks that have been issued to the disk and are waiting
  882  for durability timeouts. In such cases, OptFS issues a
  883  disk flush, ensuring the durability of checkpoint blocks
  884  that have been acknowledged by the disk. This allows
  885  OptFS to clean journal blocks belonging to some checkpointed transactions and free associated memory buffers.
  886  Checksums: OptFS checksums data blocks using the
  887  same CRC32 algorithm used for metadata. A tag is created for each data block which stores the block number
  888  and its checksum. Data tags are stored in the descriptor
  889  blocks along with tags for metadata checksums.
  890  Background Write after Notification: OptFS uses the
  891  VM subsystem to perform background writes. Checkpoint metadata blocks are marked as dirty and the
  892  expiry field of each block is set to be T+ TD (the disk
  893  acknowledged the commit block at T). T will reflect the
  894  time that the entire transaction has been acknowledged
  895  because the commit is not issued until the disk acknowledges data and metadata writes. The blocks are then
  896  handed off to the VM subsystem.
  897  During periodic background writes, the VM subsystem checks if each dirty block has expired: if so,
  898  the block is written out; otherwise the VM subsystem
  899  rechecks the block on its next periodic write-out cycle.
  900  Reuse after Notification: Upon transaction commit,
  901  OptFS adds deleted data blocks to a global in-memory
  902  list of blocks that will be freed after the durability timeout, TD. A background thread periodically frees blocks
  903  with expired durability timeouts. Upon file-system unmount, all list blocks are freed.
  904  When the file system runs out of space, if the reuse list
  905  contains blocks, a disk flush is issued; this ensures the
  906  durability of transactions which freed the blocks in the
  907  list. These blocks are then set to be free. We expect that
  908  these "safety" flushes will be used infrequently.
  909  Selective Data Journaling: Upon a block write, the
  910  block allocation information (which is reflected in New
  911  state of the buffer head) is used to determine whether
  912  the block was newly allocated. If the write is an overwrite, the block is journaled as if it was metadata.

 1112  8 Related Work
 1113  A number of approaches to building higher performing
 1114  file systems relate to our work on OptFS and optimistic
 1115  crash consistency. For example, Soft Updates [11] shows
 1116  how to carefully order disk updates so as to never leave
 1117  an on-disk structure in an inconsistent form. In contrast
 1118  with OptFS, FreeBSD Soft Updates issues flushes to implement fsync() and ordering (although the original
 1119  work modified the SCSI driver to avoid issuing flushes).
 1120  Given the presence of asynchronous durability notifications, Soft Updates could be modified to take advantage of such signals. We believe doing so would
 1121  be more challenging than modifying journaling file systems; while journaling works at the abstraction level of
 1122  metadata and data, Soft Updates works directly with filesystem structures, significantly increasing its complexity.
 1123  Our work is similar to that of Frost et al.'s work on
 1124  Featherstitch [10], which provides a generalized framework to order file-system updates, in either a softupdating or journal-based approach. Our work instead
 1125  focuses on delayed ordering for journal commits; some
 1126  of our techniques could increase the journal performance
 1127  observed in their work.
 1128  Featherstitch provides similar primitives for ordering
 1129  and durability: pg depend() is similar to osync(),
 1130  while pg sync() is similar to dsync(). The main difference lies in the amount of work required from application developers: Featherstitch requires developers
 1131  to explicitly encapsulate sets of file-system operations
 1132  into units called patchgroups and define dependencies
 1133  between them. Since osync() builds upon the familiar semantics of fsync(), we believe it will be easier
 1134  for application developers to use.
 1135  Other work on "rethinking the sync" [19] has a similar flavor to our work. In that work, the authors cleverly note that disk writes only need to become durable
 1136  when some external entity can observe said durability;
 1137  thus, by delaying persistence until such externalization
 1138  occurs, huge gains in performance can be realized. Our
 1139  work is complimentary, in that it reduces the number of
 1140  such durability events, instead enforcing a weaker (and
 1141  higher performance) ordering among writes, but avoiding the complexity of implementing dependency tracking
 1142  within the OS. In cases where durability is required (i.e.,
 1143  applications use dsync() and not osync()), optimistic
 1144  journaling does not provide much gain; thus, Nightingale
 1145  et al.'s work still can be of benefit therein.
 1146  More recently, Chidambaram et al. implement
 1147  NoFS [6], which removes the need for any ordering to
 1148  disk at all, thus providing excellent performance. However, a lack of ordered writes means certain kinds of
 1149  crashes can lead to a recovered file system that is consistent, but that contains data from partially completed
 1150  operations. As a result, NoFS cannot implement atomic
 1151  actions, such as rename(). Atomic rename is critical
 1152  in the following update sequence: create temporary file;
 1153  write temporary file with the entire contents of the old
 1154  file, plus updates; persist temporary file via fsync();
 1155  atomically rename temporary file over old file. At the
 1156  end of this sequence, the file should exist in either the old
 1157  state or the new state with all the updates. If rename()
 1158  is not atomic, or operations can be re-ordered, the entire file could be lost due to an inopportune crash (something that has been observed in deployment [9]). OptFS,
 1159  in contrast, realizes many of the benefits of the NoFS
 1160  approach but still delivers meaningful semantics upon
 1161  which higher-level application semantics can be built.
 1162  Some real systems provide different flavors of
 1163  fsync() to applications. For example, on Mac OS X,
 1164  fsync() only ensures that writes have been issued to
 1165  the disk from main memory; as the man page states, if
 1166  an application desires durability, it should call fcntl()
 1167  with the F FULLSYNC flag. While the latter is identical
 1168  to dsync(), the former is not equivalent to osync();
 1169  importantly, simply flushing a write to the disk cache
 1170  does not provide any higher-level ordering guarantees
 1171  and thus does not serve as a suitable primitive atop which
 1172  to build application consistency; in contrast, as we have
 1173  shown, osync() is quite apt for this usage scenario.
 1174  Prior work in distributed systems aims to measure
 1175  eventual consistency (among replicas) and understand

 1219  flush-free, they cannot force writes to disk, and hence
 1220  have eventual durability. Pessimistic approaches like
 1221  journaling and copy-on-write employ flushes to provide
 1222  transactional support and high levels of consistency and
 1223  durability; however, they cannot implement ordering
 1224  without flushes, and hence offer reduced performance on
 1225  workloads with many ordering points. Due to osync()
 1226  and dsync(), optimistic crash consistency can persist
 1227  writes on demand (leading to immediate durability), and
 1228  yet remain flush-free when only ordering is required.

 1229  9 Conclusion

 1230  We present optimistic crash consistency, a new approach
 1231  to crash consistency in journaling file systems that uses
 1232  a range of novel techniques to obtain both a high level
 1233  of consistency and excellent performance in the common case. We introduce two new file-system primitives,
 1234  osync() and dsync(), which decouple ordering from
 1235  durability, and show that they can provide both high performance and meaningful semantics for application developers. We believe that such decoupling holds the key
 1236  to resolving the constant tension between consistency
 1237  and performance in file systems.
 1238  The source code for OptFS can be obtained at:
 1239  http://www.cs.wisc.edu/adsl/Software/optfs.
 1240  We hope that this will encourage adoption of the
 1241  optimistic approach to consistency.
