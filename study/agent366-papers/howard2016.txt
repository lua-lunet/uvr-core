   24  arXiv:1608.06696v1 [cs.DC] 24 Aug 2016 1 Introduction

   25  Distributed consensus is the problem of reaching agreement in the face of failures.
   26  It is a common problem in modern distributed systems and its applications range
   27  from distributed locking and atomic broadcast to strongly consistent key value
   28  stores and state machine replication [35]. Lamport's Paxos algorithm [18, 19] is
   29  one such solution to this problem and since its publication it has been widely
   30  built upon in teaching, research and practice.
   31  At its core, Paxos uses two phases, each requires agreement from a subset of
   32  participants (known as a quorum) to proceed. The safety and liveness of Paxos
   33  is based on the guarantee that any two quorums will intersect. To satisfy this

   34  1
=== PAGE 2 ===

   35  requirement, quorums are typically composed of any majority from a fixed set
   36  of participants, although other quorum schemes have been proposed.
   37  In practice, we usually wish to reach agreement over a sequence of values,
   38  known as Multi-Paxos [19]. We use the first phase of Paxos to establish one
   39  participant as a leader and the second phase of Paxos to propose a series of
   40  values. To commit a value, the leader must always communicate with at least a
   41  quorum of participants and wait for them to accept the value.
   42  In this paper, we weaken the requirement in the original protocol that all
   43  quorums intersect to require only that quorums from different phases intersect.
   44  Within each of the phases of Paxos, it is safe to use disjoint quorums and majority
   45  quorums are not necessary. We will refer to this new formulation as Flexible
   46  Paxos (FPaxos) as it allows developers the flexibility to choose quorums for
   47  the two phases, provided they meet the above requirement. FPaxos is strictly
   48  more general than Paxos and FPaxos with intersecting quorums is equivalent to
   49  Paxos.
   50  Given that Multi-Paxos and its variants are widely deployed, such a result has
   51  wide reaching practical applications. Since the second phase of Paxos (replication)
   52  is far more common than the first phase (leader election), we can use FPaxos
   53  to reduce the size of commonly used second phase quorums. For example, in a
   54  system of 10 nodes, we can safely allow only 3 nodes to participate in replication,
   55  provided that we require 8 nodes to participate when recovering from leader
   56  failure. This strategy, decreasing phase 2 quorums at the cost of increasing phase
   57  1 quorums, is referred to in the body of the paper as simple quorums.
   58  The simple quorum system reduces latency, as leaders will no longer be
   59  required to wait for a majority of participants to accept proposals. Likewise, it
   60  improves steady state throughput as disjoint sets of participants can now accept
   61  proposals, enabling better utilization of participants and decreased network load.
   62  The price we pay for this is reduced availability as the system can tolerate fewer
   63  failures whilst recovering from leader failure.
   64  Later, we will illustrate that surprisingly, it is not always necessary to
   65  compromise availability for steady state performance. Examples include reducing
   66  the size of second phase quorums by one when the number of acceptors is even,
   67  and utilizing quorum systems such as grid quorums, which decrease the quorum
   68  sizes of both phases.
   69  In the following section we outline the basic Paxos algorithm using the
   70  standard terminology. Readers who are already familiar with the algorithm
   71  should proceed directly to the next section. In §3 we describe the observation
   72  in detail and then in §4 motivate why such flexibility is useful in practice. §5
   73  gives an informal description of why it is safe to weaken Paxos's assumption on
   74  quorum intersection. In §6 we evaluate a na¨ıve implementation of FPaxos and
   75  demonstrate its usefulness. §7 outlines how to dynamically choose quorums and
   76  §8 relates FPaxos to the existing work in the field. The appendix includes a
   77  TLA+ [20] specification of the FPaxos algorithm which has been model checked
   78  against our safety assumption.

   80  2 Paxos

   81  We wish to decide a single value v between a set of processes. The system is
   82  asynchronous, each process may fail and the messages passed between them may
   83  be lost. Each process has one or more roles. We have three roles: the proposer,
   84  a process who wishes to have a particular value chosen, the acceptor, a process
   85  which agrees and persists decided values or the learner, a process wishing to
   86  learn the decided value.
   87  A proposer who has a candidate value will try to propose the value to the
   88  acceptors. If a value has already been chosen, the proposer will instead learn
   89  it. The process of proposing a value has two stages: phase 1 and phase 2, each
   90  phase requires a majority of acceptors to agree in order to proceed. We will now
   91  look at each of these stages in details:
   92  Phase 1 - Prepare & Promise
   93  i A proposer selects a unique proposal number p and sends prepare(p) to the
   94  acceptors.
   95  ii Each acceptor receives prepare(p). If p is the highest proposal number
   96  promised, then p is written to persistent storage and the acceptor replies
   97  with promise(p',v'). (p',v') is the last accepted proposal (if present) where p'
   98  is the proposal number and v' is the corresponding proposed value.
   99  iii Once the proposer receives promise from the majority of acceptors, it proceeds
  100  to phase two. Otherwise, it may try again with higher proposal number.

  101  Phase 2 - Propose & Accept
  102  i The proposer must now select a value v. If more than one proposal was
  103  returned in phase 1 then it must choose the value associated with the highest
  104  proposal number. If no proposals were returned, then the proposer can
  105  choose its own value for v. The proposer then sends propose(p,v) to the
  106  acceptors.
  107  ii Each acceptor receives a propose(p,v). If p is equal to or greater than the
  108  highest promised proposal number, then the promised proposal number and
  109  accepted proposal is written to persistent storage and the acceptor replies
  110  with accept(p).
  111  iii Once the proposer receives accept(p) from the majority of acceptors, it learns
  112  that the value v is decided. Otherwise, it may try phase 1 again with a
  113  higher proposal number.
  114  Paxos guarantees that once a value is decided, the decision is final and
  115  no different value can be chosen. Paxos will reach agreement provided that
  116  bn/2c + 1 acceptors out of n acceptors are up and are able to communicate.
  117  Proving progress requires us to make some assumptions about the synchrony of
  118  the system, as we cannot guarantee progress in a truly asynchronous systems [7].

  133  Usually, we wish to reach agreement over a sequence of values, which we will
  134  refer to as slots. We could use distinct instances of Paxos to decide each value in
  135  the sequence i.e. the ⟩th slot is decided by the ⟩th instance of Paxos. In practice
  136  however, we can do much better and this is referred to as Multi-Paxos.
  137  The first phase of Paxos is independent of the value proposed for any given
  138  instance, therefore phase 1 can be executed prior to knowledge of which value to
  139  propose. Furthermore, we can aggregate phase 1 over a series of slots. We refer
  140  to a proposer who has completed phase 1 as a leader. To avoid loss of generality,
  141  we introduce another agent, the client who is the origin of values for proposal.
  142  Clients may be external to the system or co-located with other processes such as
  143  the proposers.
  144  Figure 1 illustrates how Multi-Paxos performs in practice. The x-axis shows
  145  the number of replicas in the system, each replica performs the roles of proposer,
  146  acceptor and learner. The blue line indicates the commit latency observed by the
  147  client and the red line indicates the average request throughput. As we would
  148  expect, increasing the number of replicas will increase latency and decrease
  149  throughput. These findings are consistent with previous studies [29, 26].
  150  A quorum system is the method by which we choose which sets of acceptors are
  151  able to form valid quorums. It has been observed that Paxos can be generalized
  152  to replace majority quorums with any quorum system which guarantees that
  153  any two quorums will have a non-empty intersection [19, 21]. The fundamental
  154  theorem of quorum intersection states that its resilience is inversely proportional
  155  to the load on (hence the throughput of) participants [31]. Therefore, with
  156  Paxos and its intersecting quorums, one can only hope to increase throughput
  157  by reducing the resilience, or vice versa. In the rest of this paper, we show that
  158  by weakening the quorum intersection requirement, we can break away from the
  159  inherent trade off between resilience and performance.

  161  3 FPaxos

  162  In this section, we observe that the usual description of Paxos (as given in §2)
  163  is more conservative than is necessary. To explain this observation, we will
  164  differentiate between the quorum used by the first phase of Paxos, which we will
  165  refer to as Q1 and the quorum for second phase, referred to as Q2.
  166  Paxos uses majority quorums of acceptors for both Q1 and Q2. By requiring
  167  that quorums contain at least a majority of acceptors we can guarantee that
  168  there will be at least one acceptor in common between any two quorums. Paxos's
  169  proof of safety and progress is built upon this assumption that all quorums
  170  intersect.
  171  We observe that it is only necessary for phase 1 quorums (Q1 ) and phase
  172  2 quorums (Q2) to intersect. There is no need to require that Q1 's intersect
  173  with each other nor Q2's intersect with each other. We refer to this as Flexible
  174  Paxos (FPaxos) and it generalizes the Paxos algorithm. If we allow any set of at
  175  least bn/2c + 1 acceptors to form a Q1 or Q2 quorum in FPaxos, then FPaxos
  176  is equivalent to Paxos.
  177  Using this observation, we can make use of many non-intersecting quorum
  178  systems. In its most straight-forward application, we can simply decrease the
  179  size of Q2 at the cost of increasing the size of Q1 quorums.
  180  As we discussed earlier, the second phase of Paxos (replication) is far more
  181  frequent than the first phase (leader election) in Multi-Paxos. Therefore, reducing
  182  the size of Q2 decreases latency in the common case by reducing the number of
  183  acceptors required to participate in replication, improves system tolerance to slow
  184  acceptors and allows us to use disjoint sets of acceptors for higher throughput.
  185  The price we pay for this is requiring more acceptors to participate when we
  186  need to establish a new leader. Whilst electing a new leader is a rare event in
  187  a stable system, if sufficient failures occur that we cannot form a Q1 quorum,
  188  then we cannot make progress until some of the acceptors recover.
  189  Like Paxos, the system is able to make progress provided that at least enough
  190  acceptors are up and able to communicate to form both Q1 and Q2 quorums.
  191  Unlike Paxos, we are able to make progress within a given phase, provided we are
  192  able to form quorums corresponding to that phase. More concretely, if sufficient
  193  failures have occurred such that a proposer can no longer form Q1 quorums
  194  but is able to form the smaller Q2 quorums, the system can continue to safely
  195  make progress until a new leader is required. If the acceptors recover before the
  196  current leader fails, then the system suffers no loss in availability as a result.

  197  4 Implications

  198  We will now consider the practical implication of observing that quorums intersection is required only between the two phases of Paxos. There already exists
  199  an extensive literature on quorum systems from the fields of databases and data
  200  replication, which can now be more efficiently applied to the field of consensus.
  201  Interesting example systems include weighted voting [9], hierarchies [15] and

  203  crumbling walls [34]. For now however, we will illustrate the utility of FPaxos
  204  by considering three na¨ıve example quorum systems: (1) majority quorums; (2)
  205  simple quorums and (3) grid quorums.

  206  4.1 Majority quorums
  207  Currently, Paxos requires us to use quorums of size n/2 + 1 when the number
  208  of acceptors n is even1. Using our observation, we can safely reduce the size
  209  of Q2 by one from n/2 + 1 to n/2 and keep Q1 the same. Such a change
  210  would be trivial to implement and by reducing the number of acceptors required
  211  to participate in replication, we can reduce latency and improve throughput.
  212  Furthermore, we have also improved the fault tolerance of the system. As with
  213  Paxos, if at most n/2 − 1 failures occur then we are guaranteed to be able to
  214  make progress. However unlike with Paxos, if exactly n/2 acceptors fail and the
  215  leader is still up then we are able to continue to make progress and suffer no
  216  loss of availability.
  217  Figure 2 shows two example traces of FPaxos with majority quorums in
  218  practice. As the system is comprised of four acceptors, FPaxos uses a majority
  219  (3 acceptors) for Q1 but requires only two acceptors for Q2. In the examples, the
  220  two proposers wish to commit conflicting proposals. In figure 2a, proposer one is
  221  first to execute FPaxos and its value a is committed. Later, proposer two executes
  222  a round of Paxos and learns the value. In figure 2b, both proposers successfully
  223  execute the first phase of FPaxos and simultaneously submit conflicting proposed
  224  values to the disjoint sets of acceptors. Both Q2s will intersect with the two
  225  Q1s, so only one of them will be successful. The unsuccessful proposer can retry
  226  with a higher proposal number and learn the chosen value.

  227  4.2 Simple quorums
  228  We will use the term simple quorums to refer to a quorum systems where any
  229  acceptor is able to participate in a quorum and each acceptor's participation is
  230  counted equally. Simple quorums are a straightforward generalization of majority
  231  quorums. Paxos requires that all quorums intersect, and therefore, as we have
  232  previously discussed, each quorum must contain at least a strict majority of
  233  acceptors to meet this requirement.
  234  In contrast, FPaxos requires only that quorums from different phases intersect.
  235  Therefore, FPaxos with simple quorums must require that |Q1| + |Q2| > N. We
  236  know that in practice the second phase is much more common than the first
  237  phase so we allow |Q2| < N/2 and increase the size of Q1 accordingly. For a
  238  given size of Q2 and number of acceptors N, then minimum size of our first
  239  phase quorum is |Q1| = N − |Q2| + 1. FPaxos will always be able to handle up
  240  to |Q2| −1 failures. However, if between |Q2| to N − |Q2| failures occur, we can
  241  continue replication until a new leader is required.

  271  As has been previously observed [25], we do not need to send prepare and

  276  the fastest acceptors and must retransmit when failures occur.

  277  4.3 Grid quorums
  278  The key limitation of simple quorums is that reducing the size of the Q2 requires
  279  a corresponding increase in the size of Q1 to continue to ensure intersection.
  280  Grid quorums are an example of an alternative quorum system. Grid quorums
  281  can reduce the size of Q1 by offering a different trade off between quorum
  282  sizes, flexibility when choosing quorums and failure tolerance. Grid quorum
  283  schemes arrange the N nodes into a matrix of N1 columns by N2 rows, where
  284  N1 × N2 = N and quorums are composed of rows and columns. As with many
  285  other quorum systems, grid quorums restrict which combinations of acceptors
  286  can form valid quorums. This restriction allows us to reduce the size of quorums
  287  whilst still ensuring that they intersect.
  288  Paxos requires that all quorums intersect thus one suitable grid scheme would
  289  require one row and one column to form a quorum2. Figure 3a shows an example
  290  Q1 quorum and Q2 quorum using this scheme. This would reduce the size of a
  291  quorum from the majority of N to N1 + N2 − 1. The number of failures which
  292  could be tolerated range from M IN(N1, N2), where one node from every row or
  293  every column fails to (N1 − 1) × (N2 −1), leaving only one row and one column
  294  remaining.
  295  In FPaxos, we can safely reduce our quorums to one row of size N1 for Q1
  296  and one column of size N2 for Q2, examples are shown in Figure 3b. This
  297  construction is interesting as quorums from the same phase will never intersect,

  299  below it. The average quorum size would become N1 + (1/2)N2 , although the worst case is
  300  still N1 + N2 − 1.

  302  and may be useful in practice for evenly distributing the load of FPaxos across
  303  a group of acceptors. With simple quorums, a system cannot recover from
  304  leader failure whilst any set of |Q2| = N/2 acceptors have failed. Now with
  305  grid quorums, we are no longer treating all failures equally, it matters which of
  306  the acceptors have failed, not just how many have failed. Recall, that we are
  307  able to make progress in a given phase, provided we can still form a quorum
  308  for that phase. For example, let us consider if four acceptors in either of grids
  309  from Figure 3 were to fail. If these failures occur across two columns then both
  310  systems will make progress. If all the failed nodes are within one column then
  311  no progress will be made by Paxos but FPaxos will continue until a new leader
  312  is needed. Likewise, if all the nodes in a given row where to fail, FPaxos would
  313  be able to complete Q1 and thus recover all past decisions, it can then safely
  314  fall back to a reconfiguration protocol to remove or replace the failed acceptors
  315  and continue to make progress. In practice, failures are not independent and so
  316  we can distribute acceptors across the machines, racks or even data centers to
  317  minimize the likelihood of simultaneous failure.
  318  By way of a thought experiment, let us consider setting N1 = 1 and N2 = N
  319  when using grid quorums or equivalently setting |Q1| = N and |Q2| = 1 with
  320  simple quorums. Any single acceptor will be sufficient to form a Q2, however
  321  every acceptor must participate in Q1. In practice, this would allow all acceptors
  322  to learn the decided value in a single hop, however we would be unable to recover
  323  from leader failure until every acceptor is up.
  324  Alternatively, let us consider setting N1 = N and N2 = 1 when using grid
  325  quorums or equivalently setting |Q1| = 1 and |Q2| = N with simple quorums.
  326  This would require every acceptor to participate in Q2 but only a single acceptor
  327  is needed for Q1. If any acceptors are still up, then we can complete Q1 and learn
  328  past decisions. As it has been previously observed [25, 23], such a construction
  329  allows us to tolerate f failures with only f + 1 acceptors instead of 2f + 1.

  330  5 Safety

  331  Lamport's proof of safety for Paxos does not use the full strength of the assumptions made, namely that all quorums will intersect. For the sake of completeness,
  332  in this section we outline the proof of safety for FPaxos.
  333  For FPaxos to be safe, every decision that is reached must be final. In other
  334  words, once a value has been decided, no different value can be decided. This
  335  can be formally expressed as the following requirement:

  336  Theorem 1. If value v is decided with proposal number p and v0 is decided with
  337  proposal number p0 then v = v0
  338  For a given value v to be decided, it must first have been be proposed. Thus
  339  the following requirement is strictly stronger:
  340  Theorem 2. If value v is decided with proposal number p then for any message
  341  propose(p',v') where p0 > p then v = v0

  410  6 Prototype

  411  We implemented a na¨ıve FPaxos by modifying LibPaxos3 3, a commonly benchmarked Multi-Paxos implementation. Our modification simply generalized over
  412  the size of Q1 and Q2. The simple quorums were na¨ıvely chosen at random and
  413  messages were sent only to a quorum of nodes.
  414  LibPaxos3 is Multi-Paxos implementation in C which uses TCP/IP for
  415  transport. For each experiment, we tested N replicas, where each replica is a
  416  leader, an acceptor and a proposer. We used request sizes of 64 bytes with 10
  417  requests in progress at any given time. Our experiments were ran within a single
  418  linux VM with a single core and 1GB of RAM, we used mininet 4 to simulate a
  419  10 Mbps network with 20 ms round trip time. Each test was run for 120 seconds,
  420  we discard the first and last 10 second to measure the system during its steady
  421  state.

  423  Q2 quorum sizes. These results are as we would expect: by reducing the size
  424  of the Q2 quorum, we send fewer messages and thus increase throughput and

  440  We show that even a very naive implementation improves performance and
  441  we believe that systems designed for FPaxos will see far greater performance,
  442  particularly by taking advantage of using disjoint set of acceptors and smarter
  443  quorum construction techniques to improve failure tolerance. Our prototype
  444  source code and associated materials are available online5.

  445  7 Enhancements

  446  We observe that the safety of FPaxos relies only on the assumption that a given
  447  Q1 will intersect with all Q2s with lower proposal numbers. Therefore, we could
  448  further weaken the quorum requirements if a proposer was able to learn which
  449  Q2s have been used with smaller proposal numbers. We would then require only
  450  that a proposer's Q1 intersect with these instead of all possible Q2s.
  451  In order to take advantage of this, we can enhance FPaxos with a mechanism
  452  for leaders to select quorum(s) and to announce their selection. There are many
  453  ways this could be implemented, but for safety the mechanism for a leader
  454  to make its quorum selection known must be weaved carefully into the leader
  455  election protocol. Details are left out of the scope of this paper. Briefly, it would
  456  be akin to Paxos reconfiguration and achieved by adopting the principles of
  457  Vertical Paxos [23].
  458  The implications of this enhancement can be far reaching. For example, in a
  459  system of N = 100f nodes, a leader may start by announcing a fixed Q2 of size
  460  f + 1 and all higher proposal numbers (and readers) will need to intersect with
  461  only this Q2. This allow us to tolerate N − f failures. Likewise, a leader may
  462  choose a small set of Q2's and announce all of them, allowing more flexibility in
  463  phase 2 at the cost of less availability in phase 1. A leader may also change its
  464  quorum selection over time using the dynamic selection mechanism.
  465  We expect that these enhancements and others may open many new possibilities for practical system designs in the future.

  468  8 Related Works

  469  The insightful State Machine Replication (SMR) paradigm [17, 36] underlies
  470  many reliable systems, including pioneering works in distributed systems field
  471  like Viewstamped Replication [32] and Isis [3]. The Paxos algorithm provides
  472  the algorithmic solution for many production systems architected as replicated
  473  state machines. SMR must solve a core ingredient, agreement, which Dwork
  474  et al.[6] solved under minimal synchrony assumptions, and which is the basis
  475  for the single position agreement protocol (called Synod) in Paxos [18]. In
  476  the decades following its invention, the Paxos algorithm has been extensively
  477  researched: it has been explained in simpler terms [19, 39], optimized for practical
  478  systems [4, 11, 13, 33] and extended to handle reconfiguration [23] and arbitrary
  479  failures [5].
  480  Many variants of Paxos were proposed. Cheap Paxos [25] fixes a single phase
  481  2 quorum until a leader replacement occurs. Fast Paxos [21] has a leaderless
  482  fast-path protocol which utilizes fast-track phase 2 quorums of size f + df+1 e.
  483  2
  484  Mencius [26] uses a revolving leader regime. Ring-Paxos [28, 27] applies the
  485  idea in Cheap Paxos [25] to a ring overlay using network-level multicast. Chain
  486  Replication [41] daisy-chains acceptors and collapses the two phases into one
  487  chain sweep. Generalized Paxos [22] extends state-machine replication with
  488  commutative commands, and Egalitarian Paxos [30] extends Generalized Paxos
  489  with fast-track quorums whose size is f + bf+1c. EVE [14] optimistically
  490  2
  491  concurrently agrees on commands and later resolves conflicts in case they do
  492  not commute. Corfu [2] lets the leader delegate its exclusive authority to any
  493  proposer in order to yield better parallelism. There are many other variants; a
  494  comprehensive taxonomy of Paxos variants is given in [40]. These previous works
  495  were built on the foundations presented in the pioneering protocols [32, 3, 6, 18],
  496  and focused on enhancing them in order to achieve better performance. Our
  497  new observation revisited the foundations and generalized them; it is completely
  498  orthogonal and can be integrated into previous protocols as well as to real
  499  production systems in order to further improve performance.
  500  The SMR reconfiguration problem was addressed in several previous works.
  501  Some use consensus commands to agree on next configurations [32, 24, 23],
  502  whereas others use the first phase to determine which quorum (out of a fix set
  503  of quorums) will be used in the second phase [25, 28]. A general framework for
  504  reconfiguration that separates the steady state agreement mechanism from the
  505  reconfiguration event appears in [23]. Reconfiguration for other fault tolerant
  506  services was also previously investigated, e.g., in [10, 1, 12, 38, 8]. As discussed
  507  in Section 7, the ideas in these works can be adopted in order to enhance FPaxos
  508  into a reconfigurable and dynamic system.
  509  To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to prove and implement this generalization of Paxos. During the preparation of this publication, Sougoumarane
  510  independently made the same observation on which this work is based and
  511  released a blog post summarizing [37] it for the systems community.

  513  9 Conclusion

  514  In this paper we have described FPaxos, a generalization of the widely adopted
  515  Paxos algorithm, which no longer requires that quorums from the same Paxos
  516  phase intersect. We believe this result has wide ranging consequences.
  517  Firstly, over the last two decades Multi-Paxos has been widely studied,
  518  deployed and extended. Generalizing existing systems to use FPaxos should be
  519  quite straightforward. Exposing replication (phase 2) quorum size to developers
  520  would allow them to choose their own trade off between failure tolerance and
  521  steady state latency.
  522  Secondly, by no longer requiring replication quorums to intersect, we have
  523  removed an important limit on scalability. Through smart quorum construction
  524  and pragmatic system design, we believe a new breed of scalable, resilient and
  525  performant consensus algorithms is now possible.
