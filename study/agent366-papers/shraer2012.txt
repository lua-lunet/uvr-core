    5  Abstract
    6  Dynamically changing (reconfiguring) the membership of a replicated distributed system while preserving
    7  data consistency and system availability is a challenging
    8  problem. In this paper, we show that reconfiguration can
    9  be simplified by taking advantage of certain properties
   10  commonly provided by Primary/Backup systems. We
   11  describe a new reconfiguration protocol, recently implemented in Apache Zookeeper. It fully automates configuration changes and minimizes any interruption in service
   12  to clients while maintaining data consistency. By leveraging the properties already provided by Zookeeper our
   13  protocol is considerably simpler than state of the art.

   14  1 Introduction

   15  The ability to reconfigure systems is critical to cope with
   16  the dynamics of deployed applications. Servers permanently crash or become obsolete, user load fluctuates over time, new features impose different constraints;
   17  these are all reasons to reconfigure an application to use
   18  a different group of servers, and to shift roles and balance within a service. We refer to this ability of a system
   19  to dynamically adapt to a changing set of machines or
   20  processes as elasticity.
   21  Cloud computing has intensified the need for elastic
   22  long lived distributed systems. For example, some applications such as sports and shopping are seasonal with
   23  heavy workload bursts during championship games or
   24  peak shopping days. Such workloads mean that elasticity is not a matter of slowly growing a cluster; it may
   25  mean that a cluster grows by an order of magnitude only
   26  to shrink by the same order of magnitude shortly after.
   27  Unfortunately, at the back-end of today's cloud services, one frequently finds a coordination service which
   28  itself is not elastic, such as ZooKeeper [12]. Companies
   29  such as Facebook, LinkedIn, Netflix, Twitter, Yahoo!,
   30  and many others, use Zookeeper to track failures and
   31  configuration changes of distributed applications; application developers just need to react to events sent to
   32  them by the coordination service. However, Zookeeper
   33  users have been asking repeatedly since 2008 to facilitate reconfiguration of the service itself, and thus far,

   37  the road to elasticity has been error prone and hazardous:
   38  Presently, servers cannot be added to or removed from
   39  a running ZooKeeper cluster and similarly no other configuration parameter (such as server roles, network addresses and ports, or the quorum system) can be changed
   40  dynamically. A cluster can be taken down, reconfigured,
   41  and restarted, but (as we explain further in Section 2) this
   42  process is manually intensive, error prone and hard to execute correctly even for expert ZooKeeper users. Data
   43  corruption and split-brain1 caused by misconfiguration
   44  of Zookeeper has happened in production2. In fact, configuration errors are a primary cause of failures in production systems [22]. Furthermore, service interruptions
   45  are currently inevitable during reconfigurations. These
   46  negative side-effects cause operators to avoid reconfigurations as much as possible. In fact, operators often prefer to over-provision a Zookeeper cluster than to reconfigure it with changing load. Over-provisioning (such as
   47  adding many more replicas) wastes resources and adds
   48  to the management overhead.
   49  Our work provides a reconfiguration capability using
   50  ZooKeeper as our primary case-study. Our experience
   51  with ZooKeeper in production over the past years has
   52  lead us to the following requirements: first, ZooKeeper
   53  is a mature product that we do not want to destabilize; a
   54  solution to the dynamic reconfiguration problem should
   55  not require major changes, such as limiting concurrency
   56  or introducing additional system components. Second, as
   57  many Zookeeper-based systems are online, service disruptions during a reconfiguration should be minimized
   58  and happen only in rare circumstances. Third, even if
   59  there are failures during reconfiguration, data integrity,
   60  consistency or service availability must not be compromised, for instance, split-brain or loss of service due to
   61  partial configuration propagation should never be possible. Finally, we must support a vast number of clients
   62  who seamlessly migrate between configurations.
   63  We use the Zookeeper service itself for reconfiguration, but we ruled out several straw-man approaches.

   80  Indeed, dynamically reconfiguring a replicated distributed system while preserving data consistency and

   93  order we are able to implement reconfiguration without
   94  using an external service and with minimal changes to
   95  ZooKeeper (in fact, reconfigurations are pipelined with
   96  other operations and treated similarly) while guaranteeing minimal disruption to the operation of a running system. We believe that our methods may be applied to efficiently reconfigure any Primary/Backup system satisfying primary order.
   97  Previous reconfiguration approaches, such as the one
   98  proposed by Lamport [15], may violate primary order,
   99  cause service disruption during reconfiguration, as well
  100  as impose a bound on the concurrent processing of all
  101  operations due to uncertainty created by the ability to
  102  reconfigure (see Section 2). Similar to our approach,
  103  FRAPPE [4] imposes no such bounds, but requires rollback support and complex management of speculative
  104  execution paths, not needed in our solution.
  105  Our reconfiguration protocol also encompasses the

  106  clients. As the service configuration changes, clients
  107  should stay connected to the service. Literature rarely
  108  mentions the client side of reconfiguration, usually stating the need for a name-service (such as DNS), which
  109  is of course necessary. However, its also crucial to
  110  re-balance client connections across new configuration
  111  servers and at the same time prevent unnecessary client
  112  migration which may overload servers, severely degrading performance. We propose a probabilistic loadbalancing scheme to move as few clients as possible
  113  and still maintain an even distribution of clients across
  114  servers. When clients detect a change, they each apply a migration policy in a distributed fashion to decide
  115  whether to move to a new server, and if so, which server
  116  they should move to.
  117  In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:
  118  • An observation that primary order allows for simple
  119  and efficient dynamic reconfiguration.
  120  • A new reconfiguration protocol for Primary/Backup
  121  replication systems preserving primary order. Unlike all previous reconfiguration protocols, our new
  122  algorithm does not limit concurrency, does not require client operations to be stopped during reconfigurations, and does not incur a complicated management overhead or any added complexity to normal client operation.
  123  • A decentralized, client-driven protocol that rebalances client connections across servers in the
  124  presence of service reconfiguration. The protocol
  125  achieves a proven uniform distribution of clients
  126  across servers while minimizing client migration.
  127  • Implementation of our reconfiguration and loadbalancing protocols in Zookeeper (being contributed to Zookeeper codebase) and analysis of
  128  their performance.

  192  Primary order. Because the primary server broadcasts
  193  state changes, Zab must ensure that they are received
  194  in order. Specifically, if state change c is received by
  195  a backup from a primary, all changes that precede c from
  196  that primary must also have been received by the backup.
  197  Zab refers to this ordering guarantee as local primary order. The local primary order property, however, is not
  198  sufficient to guarantee order when primaries can crash.
  199  It is also necessary that a new primary replacing a previous primary guarantees that once it broadcasts new updates, it has received all changes of previous primaries
  200  that have been delivered or that will be delivered. The
  201  new primary must guarantee that no state changes from
  202  previous primaries succeed its own state changes in the
  203  order of delivered state changes. Zab refers to this ordering guarantee as global primary order.
  204  The term primary order refers to an ordering that satisfies both local and global primary orders. While the discussion above has been in the context of ZooKeeper and
  205  Zab, any primary/backup system in which a primary executes operations and broadcasts state changes to backups
  206  will need primary order. The importance of this property
  207  has already been highlighted in [13, 3]. Here, we further
  208  exploit this property to simplify system reconfiguration.
  209  Configurations in Zookeeper. A ZooKeeper deployment currently uses a static configuration S for both
  210  clients and servers, which comprises a set of servers,
  211  with network address information, and a quorum system.
  212  Each server can be defined as a participant, in which case
  213  it participates in Zab as a primary or as backup, or an observer, which means that it does not participate in Zab
  214  and only learns of state updates once they are committed. For consistent operation each server needs to have
  215  the same configuration S, and clients need to have a configuration that includes some subset of S.
  216  Performing changes to a ZooKeeper configuration is
  217  currently a tricky task. Suppose, for example, that we are
  218  to add three new servers to a cluster of two servers. The
=== PAGE 4 ===

  219  two original members of the cluster hold the latest state,
  220  so we want one of them to be elected leader of the new
  221  cluster. If one of the three new servers is elected leader,
  222  the data stored by the two original members will be lost.
  223  (This could happen if the three new servers start up, form
  224  a quorum, and elect a leader before the two older servers
  225  start up.) Currently, membership changes are done using
  226  a "rolling restart" – a procedure whereby servers are shut
  227  down and restarted in a particular order so that any quorum of the currently running servers includes at least one
  228  server with the latest state. To preserve this invariant,
  229  some reconfigurations (in particular, the ones in which
  230  quorums from the old and the new configurations do not
  231  intersect) require restarting servers multiple times. Service interruptions are unavoidable, as all servers must be
  232  restarted at least once. Rolling restart is manually intensive, error prone, and hard to execute correctly even
  233  for expert ZooKeeper users (especially if failures happen during reconfiguration). Furthermore, this procedure
  234  gives no insight on how clients can discover or react to
  235  membership changes.
  236  The protocol we propose in this paper overcomes such
  237  problems and enables dynamic changes to the configuration without restarting servers or interrupting the service.
  238  Reconfiguring a state-machine. Primary/backup replication is a special instance of a more general problem,
  239  state-machine replication (SMR). With SMR, all replicas start from the same state and process the same sequence of operations. Agreement on each operation in
  240  the sequence is reached using a consensus protocol such
  241  as Paxos [15]. Similarly to our algorithm, most existing
  242  SMR approaches use the state-machine itself to change
  243  system configuration, that is, the reconfiguration is interjected as any other operation in the sequence of statemachine commands [16]. The details of implementing
  244  this in a real system are complex, as pointed out in a
  245  keynote describing the implementation of Paxos developed at Google [6]. One of the core difficulties is that
  246  a reconfiguration is very different from other SMR commands, in that it changes the consensus algorithm used
  247  to agree on the subsequent operations in the sequence.
  248  To better understand the issue, notice that in SMR
  249  there is no dependency among operations and thus separate consensus decisions are made for the different
  250  "slots" in the history sequence. Thus, if operations 1
  251  through 100 are proposed by some server, it is possible that first operation 1 is committed, then 80, then 20,
  252  and so on. It is also possible that an operation proposed
  253  by a different server is chosen for slot number 2. Suppose now that a server proposes reconfiguration for slot
  254  50. If the proposal achieves a consensus decision, it is
  255  most natural to expect that it changes the set of servers
  256  that need to execute the consensus algorithm on subsequent slots (51 and onward). Unfortunately, above we

  257  stated that we already committed slot number 80 using
  258  the current configuration; this could lead to inconsistency
  259  (a split brain scenario). We must therefore delay the consensus decisions on a slot until we know the configuration in which it should be executed, i.e., after all previous
  260  slots have been decided. As a remedy, Lamport proposed
  261  to execute the configuration change α slots in the future,
  262  which then allows the consensus algorithms on slots n
  263  through n + α − 1 to execute simultaneously with slot
  264  n. In this manner, we can maintain a 'pipeline' of operations, albeit bounded by α.
  265  Thus, standard SMR reconfiguration approaches limit
  266  the concurrent processing of all operations, because of
  267  the uncertainty introduced by the ability to reconfigure.
  268  We use a different approach that overcomes this limitation by exploiting primary order. Our reconfiguration algorithm speculatively executes any number of operations
  269  concurrently.

  270  3 Primary/Backup Reconfiguration

  271  We start with a high level description of our reconfiguration protocol. In general, in order for the system to
  272  correctly move from a configuration S to a configuration
  273  S0 we must take the following steps [3], illustrated in

  280  committed) state from S to S0, persisting it on stable
  281  storage at a quorum of S0 (a consensus decision in
  282  S0 regarding its initial state).
  283  4. activate S0, so that it can independently operate and
  284  process client operations.

  299  Note that steps 1 and 2 are necessary to avoid split
  300  brain. Steps 3 and 4 make sure that no state is lost when
  301  moving to S0. The division into four steps is logical and
  302  somewhat arbitrary – some of these steps are often executed together.
  303  In a primary/backup system many of the steps above
  304  can be simplified by taking advantage of properties already provided by the system. In such systems, the primary is the only one executing operations, producing
  305  state-updates which are relative to its current state. Thus,
  306  each state-update only makes sense in the context of all
  307  previous updates. For this reason, such systems reach
  308  agreement on the prefix of updates and not on individual
  309  operations. In other words, a new update can be committed only after all previous updates commit. This does
  310  not, however, limit concurrency: a primary can execute
  311  and send out any number of state-updates speculatively
  312  to the backups, however updates are always committed in
  313  order and an uncommitted suffix of updates may later be
  314  revoked from a backup's log if the primary fails without
  315  persisting the update to a sufficient number of replicas
  316  (a quorum). Reconfiguration fits this framework well –
  317  we interject a configuration update operation, cop, in the
  318  stream of normal state-updates, which causes a reconfiguration after previously scheduled updates are committed
  319  (in state-machine terminology, α = 1). Thus, a reconfiguration is persisted to stable storage in the old configuration S just like any other operation inS (this corresponds
  320  to step 1 above). At the same time, there is no need to
  321  explicitly deactivate S – step 2 follows from the speculative nature of the execution. Just like with any other
  322  state-update, the primary may execute any number of
  323  subsequent operations, speculatively assuming that cop
  324  commits. Primary order then makes sure that such operations are committed only after the entire prefix up to
  325  the operation (including the configuration change cop) is
  326  committed, i.e., they can only be committed in the new
  327  configuration as required by step 2.
  328  Since the primary is the only one executing operations,
  329  its local log includes all state changes that may have been
  330  committed; hence, in step 3 there is no need to copy
  331  state from other servers. Moreover, we start state transfer
  332  ahead of time, to avoid delaying the primary's pipeline.
  333  When processing the reconfiguration operation cop, the
  334  primary only makes sure that state transfer is complete,
  335  namely that a quorum of S0 has persisted all operations
  336  scheduled up to and including cop. Finally, in step 4, the
  337  primary activates S0 .
  338  If the primary of S fails during reconfiguration, a candidate primary in S must discover possible decisions
  339  made in step 1. If a new configuration S0 is discovered
  340  at this stage, the candidate primary must first take steps
  341  to commit the stream of commands up to (and including) the operation proposing S0, and then it must repeat

  342  steps 2–4 in order to transition to S0. Unlike the original primary, the new candidate primary needs to perform
  343  a startup-phase in S0 and discover the potential actions
  344  of a previous primary in S0 as well. This presented an
  345  interesting challenge in the Zab realm, since a primary
  346  in Zab usually has the most up-to-date prefix of commands, and enforces it on the backups. However, a new
  347  primary elected from S might have a staler state compared to servers in S0. We must therefore make sure that
  348  no committed updates are lost without introducing significant changes to Zab. Below (in Section 3.1), we describe the solution we chose for this pragmatic issue and
  349  the Activation Property it induces.
  350  We now dive into the details of our protocol. Due to
  351  space limitations, we omit the formal proofs here and
  352  focus on the intuition behind our algorithm.

  353  3.1 Stable primary

  354  We start by discussing the simpler case, where the primary P of the current configuration S does not fail and
  355  continues to lead the next configuration. Figure 3 depicts
  356  the flow of the protocol.
  357  pre-step: In order to overlap state-transfer with normal
  358  activity, backups in S0 connect to the current primary,
  359  who initializes their state by transferring its currently
  360  committed prefix of updates H. With Zab, such statetransfer happens automatically once backups connect to
  361  the primary, and they continue receiving from P all subsequent commands (e.g., op1 and op2 in Figure 3), making the transition to S0 smooth.
  362  step 1: The primary p schedules cop, the reconfiguration command, at the tail of the normal stream of updates. It sends an ACCEPT message containing cop to all
  363  the backups connected to it (a backup may belong to S

  365  on the next configuration is reached once a quorum of S
  366  acknowledges cop.
  367  step 2: The primary does not stall operations it receives

  369  scheduled after cop. In principle, all updates following

  463  3.2 Primary failure or replacement
  464  Until now, we assumed that the primary does not fail during the transition to S0 and continues as the primary of
  465  S0. It remains to ensure that when it is removed or fails,
  466  safety is still guaranteed. First, consider the case that
=== PAGE 7 ===

  467  the current primary in S needs to be replaced. There are
  468  many reasons why we may want to replace a primary,
  469  e.g., the current primary may not be in S0, its new role
  470  in S0 might not allow it to continue leading, or even if
  471  the IP address or port it uses for communication with the
  472  backups needs to change as part of the reconfiguration.
  473  Our framework easily accommodates this variation:
  474  The old primary can still execute operations scheduled
  475  after cop and send them out to connected backups but
  476  it does not commit these operations, as these logically
  477  belong in S0. It is the responsibility of a new primary
  478  elected in S0 to commit these operations. As an optimization, we explicitly include in an ACTIVATE message
  479  the identity of a designated, initial primary for S0 (this is
  480  one of the backups in S0, which has acknowledged the
  481  longest prefix of operations, including at least cop). As
  482  before, this primary does not need to execute the startupphase in S0 since we know that no primary previously
  483  existed in S0. Obviously, if that default primary fails to
  484  form a quorum, we fall-back to the normal primary election in S0 .
  485  Likewise, the case of a primary failure after S0 has
  486  been activated is handled as a normal Zab leader reelection.
  487  An attempted reconfiguration might not even reach a
  488  quorum of backups in S, in which case it may disappear
  489  from the system like any other failed command.
  490  We are left with the interesting case when a primarycandidate b in S discovers a pending attempt for a consensus on cop(S0) by the previous primary. This can
  491  mean either that cop was already decided, or simply that
  492  some backup in the quorum of b heard cop from p. As
  493  for any other command in the prefix b learns, it must first
  494  commit cop in S (achieving the consensus decision required in step 1). However, executing cop requires additional work, and b must follow the reconfiguration steps
  495  to implement it.
  496  The only deviation from the original primary's protocol is that b must follow the startup-phase of a new primary (Section 2) in both S and S0. In order to do so,
  497  b connects to the servers in S0 . When connecting to a
  498  server b0 in S0 , b finds out whether b0 knows of the activation of S0 (or a later configuration). If S0 has been activated, servers in S0 may know of newer updates unknown
  499  to b, hence b should not attempt to perform state transfer
  500  (otherwise it may cause newer updates to be truncated).
  501  Instead, b restarts primary re-election in S0 (and in particular connects to an already elected primary in S0 if such
  502  primary exists). Otherwise, b implicitly initiates statetransfer to b0 (much like its predecessor did). This includes at least all updates up to cop but may also include
  503  updates scheduled by the previous primary after cop.
  504  This leads us to a subtle issue resulting from our desire to introduce as few changes as possible to the existing implementation of leader recovery in Zookeeper.
  505  Recall that the stream of updates by the previous primary
  506  may continue past cop, and so backups in S0 may have a
  507  longer history of commands than b. In Zookeeper, connecting to b would cause them to truncate their history.
  508  This is exactly why we chose to preserve the Activation
  509  Property. If b succeeds to connect to a quorum of S0
  510  without learning of the activation of S0, we know that
  511  all updates that may have been committed are stored at
  512  a quorum of S. Thus, b will find all such updates once
  513  completing the startup-phase in S; in fact, in Zookeeper
  514  the candidate b is chosen (by preliminary selection) as
  515  the most up-to-date backup in S (that can communicate
  516  with a quorum of S), so it will already have the full prefix and no actual transfer of updates is needed during the
  517  startup-phase.
  518  Finally, note that b might discover more than a single
  519  future reconfiguration while performing its startup-phase
  520  in S. For example, it may see that both S0 and S00 were
  521  proposed. b may in this case skip S0 and run the startupphase in S and S00, after which it activates S00.

  522  3.3 Progress guarantees
  523  As in [2], the fault model represents a dynamic interplay between the execution of reconfiguration operations
  524  and the "adversary": The triggering of a reconfiguration
  525  event from S to S0 marks a first transition. Until this
  526  event, a quorum of S is required to remain alive in order
  527  for progress to be guaranteed. After it, both a quorum of
  528  S and of S0 are required to remain alive. The completion
  529  of a reconfiguration is generally not known to the participants in the system. In our protocol, it occurs when the
  530  following conditions are met: (a) a quorum of S0 receives
  531  and processes the ACTIVATE message for S0 , and (b) all
  532  operations scheduled before S0 is activated by a primary
  533  are committed. The former condition indicates that S0
  534  can independently process new operations, while the latter indicates that all previous operations, including those
  535  scheduled while the reconfiguration was in progress, are
  536  committed (it is required due to the Activation Property
  537  and step 2'). Neither conditions are externally visible to
  538  a client or operator submitting the reconfiguration command. However, there is an easy way to make sure that
  539  both condition are met: after the reconfiguration completes at the client, it can submit a no-op update operation; once it commits, we know that both conditions (a)
  540  and (b) are satisfied (the no-op update can be automatically submitted by the client-side library). An alternative
  541  way to achieve this is to introduce another round to the
  542  reconfiguration protocol (which, for simplicity and compatibility with Zab, we decided to avoid). Either way,
  543  once (a) and (b) are satisfied, the fault model transitions
  544  for the second time: only a quorum of S0 is required to
  545  survive from now on.

  550  4 Reconfiguring the Clients

  551  Once servers are able to reconfigure themselves we are
  552  left with two problems at the client. First, clients need
  553  to learn about new servers to be able to connect to
  554  them. This is especially important if servers that a client
  555  was using have been removed from the configuration or
  556  failed. Second, we need to rebalance the load on the
  557  servers. ZooKeeper clients use long-lived connections
  558  and only change the server they are connected to if it has
  559  failed. This means that new servers added to a configuration will not take on new load until new clients start
  560  or other servers fail. We can solve the first problem using DNS and by having clients subscribe to configuration
  561  changes (see Section 5) in Zookeeper. For lack of space
  562  here we concentrate on the second problem.
  563  Figure 5 shows a balanced service with configuration
  564  S that is about to move to S0. There are 30 clients in
  565  the system and each of the three servers in S serves 10
  566  of the clients. When we change to S0 we would like
  567  to make sure the new system is also load-balanced. In
  568  this example this means that each server should service 6
  569  clients. We would also like to move as few clients as possible since session reestablishment puts load on both the
  570  clients and the servers and increases latency for client requests issued while the reestablishment is in process. A
  571  final goal is to accomplish the load balance using only
  572  logic at the clients so as not to burden the servers.

  631  5 Implementation and Evaluation

  632  We implemented our server and client-side protocols in
  633  Apache Zookeeper. To this end we updated the serverside library of ZooKeeper (written in Java) as well as
  634  the two client libraries (written in Java and in C). We
=== PAGE 9 ===

  635  added a reconfig command to the API that changes the
  636  configuration, a config command that retrieves the current configuration and additionally allows users to subscribe for configuration changes and finally the updateserver-list command that triggers the client migration algorithm described in Section 4. We support two reconfiguration modes. The first is incremental – it allows
  637  adding and removing servers to the current configuration.
  638  The second type of reconfiguration is non-incremental,
  639  which means that the user specifies the new configuration. This method allows changing the quorum system
  640  dynamically. We allow adding and removing servers as
  641  well as changing server roles. We also support dynamically changing the different network addresses and ports
  642  used by the system.
  643  In the remainder of this section we evaluate the impact

  645  the effect on throughput and latency of normal operations

  650  hard drives. The servers run RHEL 5.3 using the ext3 file

  867  To mitigate the problem illustrated in Figure 9 we
  868  could of course disconnect all clients and re-connect

  869  them to randomly chosen servers in the new configuration. This, however, creates excessive migration and
  870  unnecessary loss of throughput. Ideally, we would like
  871  the number of migrating clients to be proportional to the
  872  change in membership. If only a single server is removed
  873  (or added), only clients that were (or should be) connected to that server should need to migrate.

  874  Consistent Hashing. A natural way to achieve such
  875  limited migration, which we use as a second baseline,
  876  is to associate each client with a server using consistent hashing [14]. Client and server identifiers are randomly mapped to points in an m-bit space, which can
  877  be seen as circular (i.e., 0 follows 2m − 1). Each client
  878  is then associated with the server that immediately follows it in the circle. If a server is removed, only the
  879  clients that are associated with it will need to migrate by
  880  connecting to the next server on the circle. Similarly,
  881  if a new server is added a client migrates to it only if
  882  the new server was inserted between the client and the
  883  server to which it is currently connected. In order to improve load balancing, each server is sometimes hashed k
  884  times (usually k is chosen to be in the order of log(N),
  885  where N is the number of servers). To evaluated the
  886  approach, we implemented it in Zookeeper. Figure 10
  887  shows measurements for k = 1, k = 5 and k = 20.
  888  We used MD5 hashing to create random identifiers for
  889  clients and servers (m = 128). We can see that higher
  890  values of k achieve better load balancing. Note, however,
  891  that load-balancing in consistent hashing is uniform only
  892  with "high probability", which depends on N and k. In
  893  the case of Zookeeper, where 3-7 servers (N) are usually used, the values of N and k are not high enough to
  894  achieve reasonable load balancing.

  895  Probabilistic Load Balancing. Finally, Figure 11
  896  shows measurements of load-balancing with the approach we have implemented in Zookeeper as outlined
  897  in Section 4. Unlike consistent hashing, in this approach
  898  every client makes a probabilistic decision whether and
  899  where to migrate, such that the expected number of
  900  clients per server is the same for every server. As we can
  901  see from the figure the difference in number of clients
  902  between the server with the most clients and the least
  903  clients is very small. Using our simple case-based probabilistic load balancing we are able to achieve very close
  904  to optimal load-balance using logic entirely at the client.

  983  and we are replacing C with D, SMART runs two replicas of A and two of B, one in the new configuration
  984  and one in the old, each running its own instance of the
  985  replication protocol. An important design consideration
  986  in our work has been to introduce minimal changes to
  987  Zookeeper, as it is used in production by many commercial companies. Dynamically creating additional
  988  Zookeeper replicas just for the purpose of reconfiguration adds an implementation and management overhead
  989  that would not be acceptable to Zookeeper users. Unlike SMART, we do not limit concurrency or require any
  990  additional resources to reconfigure.
  991  FRAPPE [4] proposes a different solution. Each server
  992  in FRAPPE works with a set of possible configurations,
  993  similarly to RAMBO. If a reconfiguration is proposed for
  994  history slot n, any number of operations can be proposed
  995  after n, however their completion is speculative – users
  996  are aware that even though these operations commit they
  997  may later be rolled back if a different operation is chosen
  998  for slot n. This requires servers to maintain a speculative
  999  execution tree, each branch corresponding to an assumption on the decision on some reconfiguration for a particular history slot. In case the reconfiguration is chosen
 1000  for slot n and once state transfer is complete, the speculative operations become permanently committed and
 1001  the corresponding tree-branch is merged into the "trunk".
 1002  Otherwise, the branch is simply abandoned. Similarly to
 1003  SMART and FRAPPE, we do not require any intersection between the memberships of consecutive configurations. The algorithm presented in this paper processes
 1004  updates speculatively, similar to FRAPPE. However, our
 1005  algorithm does not require servers to work with or explicitly manage multiple configurations and it does not
 1006  expose speculative operation completions to the clients.
 1007  Group communication systems that provide virtual
 1008  synchrony [7, 3] are perhaps closer to Zookeeper than
 1009  Paxos-style replicated state machines. In such systems, a
 1010  group of processes may exchange messages with others
 1011  in the group, and the membership of the group (called a
 1012  view) may change. Virtual synchrony guarantees that all
 1013  processes transferring from one view to the next agree on
 1014  the set of messages received in the previous view. Note
=== PAGE 13 ===

 1015  that they do not necessarily agree on the order of messages, and processes that did not participate in the previous view do not have to deliver these messages. Still,
 1016  virtual synchrony is similar to primary order in the sense
 1017  that it does not allow messages sent in different configurations to interleave just as primary order does not
 1018  allow messages sent by different leaders to interleave.
 1019  Unlike state-machine replication systems, which remain
 1020  available as long as a quorum of the processes are alive,
 1021  group communication systems must react to every failure by removing the faulty process from the view. While
 1022  this reconfiguration is in progress, client operations are
 1023  not processed. Other systems, such as Harp [17] and
 1024  Echo [11] follow similar methodology, stopping all client
 1025  operations during reconfigurations. Conversely, our design (similarly to state-machine replication systems) tolerates failures as long as a quorum of the replicas remains
 1026  available, and allows executing client operations while
 1027  reconfiguration and state-transfer are in progress.

 1028  7 Conclusions

 1029  Reconfiguration is hard in general. It becomes especially hard when reconfiguring the configuration service.
 1030  While intuitively it seems simple, care must be taken to
 1031  address all failure cases and execution orderings.
 1032  Our reconfiguration protocol builds on properties of
 1033  Primary/Backup systems to achieve high performance
 1034  reconfigurations without imposing a bound on concurrent processing of operations or stalling them, and without the high management price of previous proposals.
 1035  The load balancing algorithm for distributing clients
 1036  across servers in a new configuration involves decisions
 1037  made locally at the client in a completely distributed
 1038  fashion. We guarantee uniform expected load while
 1039  moving a minimum number of clients between servers.
 1040  We implemented our protocols in an existing opensource primary/backup system, and are currently working on integrating it into production. This involved simple changes, mostly to the commit and recovery operations of Zookeeper. Our evaluation shows that there are
 1041  minimal disruptions in both throughput and latency using
 1042  our approach.
 1043  While the methods described in this paper were implemented in the context of ZooKeeper, the primary order property we have taken advantage of is commonly
 1044  provided by Primary/Backup systems.
