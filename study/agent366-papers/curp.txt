   18  Abstract
   19  Traditional approaches to replication require client requests
   20  to be ordered before making them durable by copying them to
   21  replicas. As a result, clients must wait for two round-trip times
   22  (RTTs) before updates complete. In this paper, we show that
   23  this entanglement of ordering and durability is unnecessary
   24  for strong consistency. The Consistent Unordered Replication Protocol (CURP) allows clients to replicate requests that
   25  have not yet been ordered, as long as they are commutative.
   26  This strategy allows most operations to complete in 1 RTT
   27  (the same as an unreplicated system). We implemented
   28  CURP in the Redis and RAMCloud storage systems. In
   29  RAMCloud, CURP improved write latency by ∼2x (14 µs
   30  → 7.1 µs) and write throughput by 4x. Compared to unreplicated RAMCloud, CURP's latency overhead for 3-way
   31  replication is just 1 µs (6.1 µs vs 7.1 µs). CURP transformed
   32  a non-durable Redis cache into a consistent and durable
   33  storage system with only a small performance overhead.

   34  1 Introduction
   35  Fault-tolerant systems rely on replication to mask individual failures. To ensure that an operation is durable, it cannot
   36  be considered complete until it has been properly replicated.
   37  Replication introduces a significant overhead because it
   38  requires round-trip communication to one or more additional
   39  servers. Within a datacenter, replication can easily double
   40  the latency for operations in comparison to an unreplicated
   41  system; in geo-replicated environments the cost of replication
   42  can be even greater.
   43  In principle, the cost of replication could be reduced or
   44  eliminated if replication could be overlapped with the execution of the operation. In practice, however, this is difficult to
   45  do. Executing an operation typically establishes an ordering
   46  between that operation and other concurrent operations, and
   47  the order must survive crashes if the system is to provide
   48  consistent behavior. If replication happens in parallel with
   49  execution, different replicas may record different orders for
   50  the operations, which can result in inconsistent behavior
   51  after crashes. As a result, most systems perform ordering
   52  before replication: a client first sends an operation to a server
   53  that orders the operation (and usually executes it as well);
   54  then that server issues replication requests to other servers,
   55  ensuring a consistent ordering among replicas. As a result,
   56  the minimum latency for an operation is two round-trip
   57  times (RTTs). This problem affects all systems that provide
   58  consistency and replication, including both primary-backup
   59  approaches and consensus approaches.

   63  Consistent Unordered Replication Protocol (CURP) reduces the overhead for replication by taking advantage of the
   64  fact that most operations are commutative, so their order of execution doesn't matter. CURP supplements a system's existing replication mechanism with a lightweight form of replication without ordering based on witnesses. A client replicates
   65  each operation to one or more witnesses in parallel with sending the request to the primary server; the primary can then execute the operation and return to the client without waiting for
   66  normal replication, which happens asynchronously. This allows operations to complete in 1 RTT, as long as all witnessedbut-not-yet-replicated operations are commutative. Noncommutative operations still require 2 RTTs. If the primary
   67  crashes, information from witnesses is combined with that
   68  from the normal replicas to re-create a consistent server state.
   69  CURP can be easily applied to most existing systems
   70  using primary-backup replication. Changes required by
   71  CURP are not intrusive, and it works with any kind of backup
   72  mechanism (e.g. state machine replication [31], file writes to
   73  network replicated drives [1], or scattered replication [26]).
   74  This is important since most high-performance systems
   75  optimize their backup mechanisms, and we don't want to lose
   76  those optimizations (e.g. CURP can be used with RAMCloud
   77  without sacrificing its fast crash recovery [26]).
   78  To show its performance benefits and applicability, we
   79  implemented CURP in two NoSQL storage systems: Redis [30] and RAMCloud [27]. Redis is generally used as
   80  a non-durable cache due to its very expensive durability
   81  mechanism. By applying CURP to Redis, we were able to
   82  provide durability and consistency with similar performance
   83  to the non-durable Redis. For RAMCloud, CURP reduced
   84  write latency by half (only a 1 µs penalty relative to RAM-
   85  Cloud without replication) and increased throughput by 3.8x
   86  without compromising consistency.
   87  Overall, CURP is the first replication protocol that completes linearizable deterministic update operations within
   88  1 RTT without special networking. Instead of relying
   89  on special network devices or properties for fast replication [21, 28, 22, 12, 3], CURP exploits commutativity, and it
   90  can be used for any system where commutativity of client requests can be checked just from operation parameters (CURP
   91  cannot use state-dependent commutativity). Even when
   92  compared to Speculative Paxos or NOPaxos (which require
   93  a special network topology and special network switches),
   94  CURP is faster since client request packets do not need to detour to get ordered by a networking device (NOPaxos has an
   95  overhead of 16 µs, but CURP only increased latency by 1 µs).

   97  2 Separating Durability from Ordering
   98  Replication protocols supporting concurrent clients have
   99  combined the job of ordering client requests consistently
  100  among replicas and the job of ensuring the durability of
  101  operations. This entanglement causes update operations to
  102  take 2 RTTs.
  103  Replication protocols must typically guarantee the
  104  following two properties:
  105  • Consistent Ordering: if a replica completes operation a
  106  before b, no client in the system should see the effects of
  107  b without the effects of a.
  108  • Durability: once its completion has been externalized
  109  to an application, an executed operation must survive
  110  crashes.
  111  To achieve both consistent ordering and durability, current
  112  replication protocols need 2 RTTs. For example, in masterbackup (a.k.a. primary-backup) replication, client requests
  113  are always routed to a master replica, which serializes
  114  requests from different clients. As part of executing an
  115  operation, the master replicates either the client request itself
  116  or the result of the execution to backup replicas; then the
  117  master responds back to clients. This entire process takes 2
  118  RTTs total: 1 from clients to masters and another RTT for
  119  masters to replicate data to backups in parallel.
  120  Consensus protocols with strong leaders (e.g. Multi-
  121  Paxos [17] or Raft [25]) also require 2 RTTs for update
  122  operations. Clients route their requests to the current leader
  123  replica, which serializes the requests into its operation log.
  124  To ensure durability and consistent ordering of the client
  125  requests, the leader replicates its operation log to a majority
  126  of replicas, and then it executes the operation and replies
  127  back to clients with the results. In consequence, consensus
  128  protocols with strong leaders also require 2 RTTs for updates:
  129  1 RTT from clients to leaders and another RTT for leaders to
  130  replicate the operation log to other replicas.
  131  Fast Paxos [19] and Generalized Paxos [18] reduced the
  132  latency of replicated updates from 2 RTTs to 1.5 RTT by
  133  allowing clients to optimistically replicate requests with
  134  presumed ordering. Although their leaders don't serialize
  135  client requests by themselves, leaders must still wait for a
  136  majority of replicas to durably agree on the ordering of the
  137  requests before executing them. This extra waiting adds 0.5
  138  RTT overhead. (See §B.3 for a detailed explanation on why
  139  they cannot achieve 1 RTT.)
  140  Network-Ordered Paxos [21] and Speculative Paxos [28]
  141  achieve near 1 RTT latency for updates by using special networking to ensure that all replicas receive requests in the same
  142  order. However, since they require special networking hardware, it is difficult to deploy them in practice. Also, they can't
  143  achieve the minimum possible latency since client requests
  144  detour to a common root-layer switch (or a middlebox).
  145  The key idea of CURP is to separate durability and
  146  consistent ordering, so update operations can be done in 1
  147  RTT in the normal case. Instead of replicating totally ordered

  148  Figure 1: CURP clients directly replicate to witnesses. Witnesses

  152  operations in 2 RTTs, CURP achieves durability without
  153  ordering and uses the commutativity of operations to defer
  154  agreement on operation order.
  155  To achieve durability in 1 RTT, CURP clients directly
  156  record their requests in temporary storage, called a witness,
  157  without serializing them through masters. As shown in Figure 1, witnesses do not carry ordering information, so clients
  158  can directly record operations into witnesses in parallel with
  159  sending operations to masters so that all requests will finish in
  160  1 RTT. In addition to the unordered replication to witnesses,
  161  masters still replicate ordered data to backups, but do so
  162  asynchronously after sending the execution results back to the
  163  clients. Since clients directly make their operations durable
  164  through witnesses, masters can reply to clients as soon as
  165  they execute the operations without waiting for permanent
  166  replication to backups. If a master crashes, the client requests
  167  recorded in witnesses are replayed to recover any operations
  168  that were not replicated to backups. A client can then
  169  complete an update operation and reveal the result returned
  170  from the master if it successfully recorded the request in
  171  witnesses (optimistic fast path: 1 RTT), or after waiting for
  172  the master to replicate to backups (slow path: 2 RTT).
  173  CURP's approach introduces two threats to consistency:
  174  ordering and duplication. The first problem is that the order
  175  in which requests are replayed after a server crash may not
  176  match the order in which the master processed those requests.
  177  CURP uses commutativity to solve this problem: all of the
  178  unsynced requests (those that a client considers complete,
  179  but which have not been replicated to backups) must be commutative. Given this restriction, the order of replay will have
  180  no visible impact on system behavior. Specifically, a witness
  181  only accepts and saves an operation if it is commutative with
  182  every other operation currently stored by that witness (e.g.,
  183  writes to different objects). In addition, a master will only
  184  execute client operations speculatively (by responding before
  185  replication is complete), if that operation is commutative with
  186  every other unsynced operation. If either a witness or master
  187  finds that a new operation is not commutative, the client must
  188  ask the master to sync with backups. This adds an extra RTT
  189  of latency, but it flushes all of the speculative operations.
  190  The second problem introduced by CURP is duplication.

  193  When a master crashes, it may have completed the replication
  194  of one or more operations that are recorded by witnesses. Any
  195  completed operations will be re-executed during replay from
  196  witnesses. Thus there must be a mechanism to detect and
  197  filter out these re-executions. The problem of re-executions is
  198  not unique to CURP, and it can happen in distributed systems
  199  for a variety of other reasons. There exist mechanisms to
  200  filter out duplicate executions, such as RIFL [20], and they
  201  can be applied to CURP as well.
  202  We can apply the idea of separating ordering and durability
  203  to both consensus-based replicated state machines (RSM) and
  204  primary-backup, but this paper focuses on primary-backup
  205  since it is more critical for application performance. Faulttolerant large-scale high-performance systems are mostly
  206  configured with a single cluster coordinator replicated by
  207  consensus and many data servers using primary-backup (e.g.
  208  Chubby [6], ZooKeeper [15], Raft [25] are used for cluster
  209  coordinators in GFS [13], HDFS [32], and RAMCloud [27]).
  210  The cluster coordinators are used to prevent split-brains for
  211  data servers, and operations to the cluster coordinators (e.g.
  212  change of master node during recovery) are infrequent and
  213  less latency sensitive. On the other hand, operations to data
  214  servers (e.g. insert, replace, etc) directly impact application
  215  performance, so the rest of this paper will focus on the CURP
  216  protocol for primary-backup, which is the main replication
  217  technique for data servers. In §B.2, we sketch how the same
  218  technique can be applied for consensus.

  219  3 CURP Protocol
  220  CURP is a new replication protocol that allows clients
  221  to complete linearizable updates within 1 RTT. Masters in
  222  CURP speculatively execute and respond to clients before
  223  the replication to backups has completed. To ensure the
  224  durability of the speculatively completed updates, clients
  225  multicast update operations to witnesses. To preserve
  226  linearizability, witnesses and masters enforce commutativity
  227  among operations that are not fully replicated to backups.

  228  3.1 Architecture and Model
  229  CURP provides the same guarantee as current primarybackup protocols: it provides linearizability to client requests
  230  in spite of failures. CURP assumes a fail-stop model and does
  231  not handle byzantine faults. As in typical primary-backup
  232  replications, it uses a total of f +1 replicas composed of 1
  233  master and f backups, where f is the number of replicas that
  234  can fail without loss of availability. In addition to that, it
  235  uses f witnesses to ensure durability of updates even before
  236  replications to backups are completed. As shown in Figure 2,
  237  witnesses may fail independently and may be co-hosted
  238  with backups. CURP remains available (i.e. immediately
  239  recoverable) despite up to f failures, but will still be strongly
  240  consistent even if all replicas fail.
  241  Throughout the paper, we assume that witnesses are
  242  separate from backups. This allows CURP to be applied to
  243  a wide range of existing replicated systems without modi-

  244  USENIX Association

  245  Figure 2: CURP architecture for f = 3 fault tolerance.
  246  fying their specialized backup mechanisms. For example,
  247  CURP can be applied to a system which uses file writes to
  248  network replicated drives as a backup mechanism, where
  249  the use of witnesses will improve latency while retaining its
  250  special backup mechanism. However, when designing new
  251  systems, witnesses may be combined with backups for extra
  252  performance benefits. (See §B.1 for details.)
  253  CURP makes no assumptions about the network. It
  254  operates correctly even with networks that are asynchronous
  255  (no bound on message delay) and unreliable (messages
  256  can be dropped). Thus, it can achieve 1 RTT updates on
  257  replicated systems in any environment, unlike other alternative solutions. (For example, Speculative Paxos [28] and
  258  Network-Ordered Paxos [21] require special networking
  259  hardware and cannot be used for geo-replication.)

  260  3.2 Normal Operation

  261  3.2.1 Client
  262  Client interaction with masters is generally the same as it
  263  would be without CURP. Clients send update RPC requests
  264  to masters. If a client cannot receive a response, it retries the
  265  update RPC. If the master crashes, the client may retry the
  266  RPC with a different server.
  267  For 1 RTT updates, masters return to clients before replication to backups. To ensure durability, clients directly record
  268  their requests to witnesses concurrently while waiting for
  269  responses from masters. Once all f witnesses have accepted
  270  the requests, clients are assured that the requests will survive
  271  master crashes, so clients complete the operations with the
  272  results returned from masters.
  273  If a client cannot record in all f witnesses (due to failures or
  274  rejections by witnesses), the client cannot complete an update
  275  operation in 1 RTT. To ensure the durability of the operation,
  276  the client must wait for replication to backups by sending
  277  a sync RPC to the master. Upon receiving sync RPCs, the
  278  master ensures the operation is replicated to backups before
  279  returning to the client. This waiting for sync increases the
  280  operation latency to 2 RTTs in most cases and up to 3 RTT in
  281  the worst case where the master hasn't started syncing until it
  282  receives a sync RPC from a client. If there is no response to
  283  the sync RPC (indicating the master might have crashed), the
  284  client restarts the entire process; it resends the update RPC to
  285  a new master and tries to record the RPC request in witnesses
  286  of the new master.

  288  3.2.2 Witness
  289  Witnesses support 3 basic operations: they record operations in response to client requests, hold the operations until
  290  explicitly told to drop by masters, and provide the saved
  291  operations during recovery.
  292  Once a witness accepts a record RPC for an operation, it
  293  guarantees the durability of the operation until told that the
  294  operation is safe to drop. To be safe from power failures,
  295  witnesses store their data in non-volatile memory (such as
  296  flash-backed DRAM). This is feasible since a witness needs
  297  only a small amount of space to temporarily hold recent client
  298  requests. Similar techniques are used in strongly-consistent
  299  low-latency storage systems, such as RAMCloud [27].
  300  A witness accepts a new record RPC from a client only
  301  if the new operation is commutative with all operations that
  302  are currently saved in the witness. If the new request doesn't
  303  commute with one of the existing requests, the witness must
  304  reject the record RPC since the witness has no way to order
  305  the two noncommutative operations consistent with the
  306  execution order in masters. For example, if a witness already
  307  accepted "x←1", it cannot accept "x←5".
  308  Witnesses must be able to determine whether operations are
  309  commutative or not just from the operation parameters. For
  310  example, in key-value stores, witnesses can exploit the fact
  311  that operations on different keys are commutative. In some
  312  cases, it is difficult to determine whether two operations commute each other. SQL UPDATE is an example; it is impossible to determine the commutativity of "UPDATE T SET
  313  rate = 40 WHERE level = 3" and "UPDATE T SET
  314  rate = rate + 10 WHERE dept = SDE" just from
  315  the requests themselves. To determine the commutativity of
  316  the two updates, we must run them with real data. Thus, witnesses cannot be used for operations whose commutativity
  317  depends on the system state. In addition to the case explained,
  318  determining commutativity can be more subtle for complex
  319  systems, such as DBMS with triggers and views.
  320  Each of f witnesses operates independently; witnesses
  321  need not agree on either ordering or durability of operations.
  322  In an asynchronous network, record RPCs may arrive at
  323  witnesses in different order, which can cause witnesses to
  324  accept and reject different sets of operations. However, this
  325  does not endanger consistency. First, as mentioned in §3.2.1,
  326  a client can proceed without waiting for sync to backups
  327  only if all f witnesses accepted its record RPCs. Second,
  328  requests in each witness are required to be commutative
  329  independently, and only one witness is selected and used
  330  during recovery (described in §3.3).

  331  3.2.3 Master
  332  The role of masters in CURP is similar to their role in
  333  traditional primary-backup replications. Masters in CURP
  334  receive, serialize, and execute all update RPC requests from
  335  clients. If an executed operation updates the system state, the
  336  master synchronizes (syncs) its current state with backups by
  337  replicating the updated value or the log of ordered operations.

  338  Figure 3: Sequence of executed operations in the crashed master.
  339  Unlike traditional primary-backup replication, masters
  340  in CURP generally respond back to clients before syncing
  341  to backups, so that clients can receive the results of update
  342  RPCs within 1 RTT. We call this speculative execution since
  343  the execution may be lost if masters crash. Also, we call
  344  the operations that were speculatively executed but not yet
  345  replicated to backups unsynced operations. As shown in
  346  Figure 3, all unsynced operations are contiguous at the tail of
  347  the masters' execution history.
  348  To prevent inconsistency, a master must sync before
  349  responding if the operation is not commutative with any
  350  existing unsynced operations. If a master responds for a noncommutative operation before syncing, the result returned to
  351  the client may become inconsistent if the master crashes. This
  352  is because the later operation might complete and its result
  353  could be externalized (because it was recorded to witnesses)
  354  while the earlier operation might not survive the crash
  355  (because, for example, its client crashed before recording it
  356  to witnesses). For example, if a master speculatively executes
  357  "x ← 2" and "read x", the returned read value, 2, will not be
  358  valid if the master crashes and loses "x←2". To prevent such
  359  unsafe dependencies, masters enforce commutativity among
  360  unsynced operations; this ensures that all results returned to
  361  clients will be valid as long as they are recorded in witnesses.
  362  If an operation is synced because of a conflict, the master
  363  tags its result as "synced" in the response; so, even if the
  364  witnesses rejected the operation, the client doesn't need to
  365  send a sync RPC and can complete the operation in 2 RTTs.

  366  3.3 Recovery
  367  CURP recovers from a master's crash in two phases: (1)
  368  restoration from backups and (2) replay from witnesses.
  369  First, the new master restores data from one of the backups,
  370  using the same mechanism it would have used in the absence
  371  of CURP.
  372  Once all data from backups have been restored, the new
  373  master replays the requests recorded in witnesses. The new
  374  master picks any available witness. If none of the f witnesses
  375  are reachable, the new master must wait. After picking
  376  the witness to recover from, the new master first asks it to
  377  stop accepting more operations; this prevents clients from
  378  erroneously completing update operations after recording
  379  them in a stale witness whose requests will not be retried
  380  anymore. After making the selected witness immutable, the
  381  new master retrieves the requests recorded in the witness.
  382  Since all requests in a single witness are guaranteed to be
  383  commutative, the new master can execute them in any order.
  384  After replaying all requests recorded in the selected witness,
  385  the new master finalizes the recovery by syncing to backups
  386  and resetting witnesses for the new master (or assigning a new
  387  set of witnesses). Then the new master can start accepting

  390  client requests again.
  391  Some of the requests in the selected witness may have been
  392  executed and replicated to backups before the master crashed,
  393  so the replay of such requests will result in re-execution of
  394  already executed operations. Duplicate executions of the
  395  requests can violate linearizability [20].
  396  To avoid duplicate executions of the requests that are
  397  already replicated to backups, CURP relies on exactly-once
  398  semantics provided by RIFL [20], which detects already
  399  executed client requests and avoids their re-execution. Such
  400  mechanisms for exactly-once semantics are already necessary to achieve linearizability for distributed systems [20],
  401  so CURP does not introduce a new requirement. In RIFL,
  402  clients assign a unique ID to each RPC; servers save the IDs
  403  and results of completed requests and use them to detect and
  404  answer duplicate requests. The IDs and results are durably
  405  preserved with updated objects in an atomic fashion. (If a
  406  system replicates client requests to backups instead of just
  407  updated values, providing atomic durability becomes trivial
  408  since each request already contains its ID and its result can be
  409  obtained from its replay during recovery.)
  410  This recovery protocol together with the normal operation
  411  protocol described in §3.2 guarantee linearizability of client
  412  operations even with server failures. An informal proof of
  413  correctness can be found in appendix §A.

  414  3.4 Garbage Collection
  415  To limit memory usage in witnesses and reduce possible
  416  rejections due to commutativity violations, witnesses must
  417  discard requests as soon as possible. Witnesses can drop the
  418  recorded client requests after masters make their outcomes
  419  durable in backups. In CURP, masters send garbage collection RPCs for the synced updates to their witnesses. The
  420  garbage collection RPCs are batched: each RPC lists several
  421  operations that are now durable (using RPC IDs provided by
  422  RIFL [20]).

  423  3.5 Reconfigurations
  424  This section discusses three cases of reconfiguration:
  425  recovery of a crashed backup, recovery of a crashed witness,
  426  and data migration for load balancing. First, CURP doesn't
  427  change the way to handle backup failures, so a system can
  428  just recover a failed backup as it would without CURP.
  429  Second, if a witness crashes or becomes non-responsive,
  430  the system configuration manager (the owner of all cluster
  431  configurations) decommissions the crashed witness and
  432  assigns a new witness for the master; then it notifies the
  433  master of the new witness list. When the master receives the
  434  notification, it syncs to backups to ensure f-fault tolerance
  435  and responds back to the configuration manager that it is now
  436  safe to recover from the new witness. After this point, clients
  437  can use f witnesses again to record operations. However,
  438  CURP does not push the new list of witnesses to clients. Since
  439  clients cache the list of witnesses, clients may still use the
  440  decommissioned witness (if it was temporarily disconnected,
  441  the witness will continue to accept record RPCs from clients).

  443  This endangers consistency since requests recorded in the old
  444  witnesses will not be replayed during recovery.
  445  To prevent clients from completing an unsynced update operation with just recording to old witnesses, CURP maintains
  446  a monotonically increasing integer, WitnessListVersion, for
  447  each master. A master's WitnessListVersion is incremented
  448  every time the witness configuration for the master is updated,
  449  and the master is notified of the new version along with the
  450  new witness list. Clients obtain the WitnessListVersion when
  451  they fetch the witness list from the configuration manager. On
  452  all update requests, clients include the WitnessListVersion,
  453  so that masters can detect and return errors if the clients used
  454  wrong witnesses; if they receive errors, the clients fetch new
  455  witness lists and retry the updates. This ensures that clients'
  456  update operations can never complete without syncing to
  457  backups or recording to current witnesses.
  458  Third, for load balancing, a master can split its data into
  459  two partitions and migrate a partition to a different master.
  460  Migrations usually happen in two steps: a prepare step
  461  of copying data while servicing requests and a final step
  462  which stops servicing (to ensure that all recent operations are
  463  copied) and changes configuration. To simplify the protocol
  464  changes from the base primary-backup protocol, CURP
  465  masters sync to backups and reset witnesses before the final
  466  step of migration, so witnesses are completely ruled out of
  467  migration protocols. After the migration is completed, some
  468  clients may send updates on the migrated partition to the old
  469  master and old witnesses; the old master will reject and tell
  470  the client to fetch the new master information (this is the same
  471  as without CURP); then the client will fetch the new master
  472  and its witness information and retry the update. Meanwhile,
  473  the requests on the migrated partition can be accidentally
  474  recorded in the old witness, but this does not cause safety
  475  issues; masters will ignore such requests during the replay
  476  phase of recovery by the filtering mechanism used to reject
  477  requests on not owned partitions during normal operations.

  478  3.6 Read Operations
  479  CURP handles read operations in a fashion similar to that
  480  of primary-backup replication. Since such operations don't
  481  modify system state, clients can directly read from masters,
  482  and neither clients nor masters replicate read-only operations
  483  to witnesses or backups.
  484  However, even for read operations, a master must check
  485  whether a read operation commutes with all currently
  486  unsynced operations as discussed in §3.2.3. If the read
  487  operation conflicts with some unsynced update operations,
  488  the master must sync the unsynced updates to backups before
  489  responding for the read.

  493  allow reading from backups because it reduces the load on
  494  masters and can provide better latency in a geo-replicated

  522  replication mechanism prevents backups from returning new
  523  values that are not yet fully synced to all backups. Such mechanism is neccessary even before applying CURP since returning a new value prematurely can cause inconsistency; even if
  524  a value is replicated to some of backups, the value may get lost
  525  if the master crashes and a new master recovers from a backup
  526  that didn't receive the new value. A simple solution for this
  527  problem is that backups don't allow reading values that are not
  528  yet fully replicated to all backups. For backups to track which
  529  values are fully replicated and ok to be read, a master can piggyback the acknowlegements for successful previous syncs

  531  to read a value that is not known to be yet fully replicated, the
  532  backup can wait for full replication or ask the client to retry.

  534  still guarantees linearizability. With a concurrent update,
  535  reading from backups could violate linearizability in two
  536  ways: (1) a read sees the old value after the completion
  537  of the update operation and (2) a read sees the old value

  538  after another read returned the new value. The first issue
  539  is prevented by checking a witness before reading from a
  540  backup. Since clients can complete an update operation only
  541  if it is synced to all backups or recorded in all witnesses, a
  542  reader will either see a noncommutative update request in the
  543  witness being checked or find the new value from the backup;
  544  thus, it is impossible for a read after an update to return the
  545  old value. For the second issue, since both a master and
  546  backups delay reads of a new value until it is fully replicated
  547  to all backups, it is impossible to read an older value after
  548  another client reads the new value.

  549  4 Implementation on NoSQL Storage
  550  This section describes how to implement CURP on lowlatency NoSQL storage systems that use primary-backup
  551  replications. With the emergence of large-scale Web services, NoSQL storage systems became very popular (e.g.
  552  Redis [30], RAMCloud [27], DynamoDB [33] and MongoDB [7]), and they range from simple key-value stores to
  553  more fully featured stores supporting secondary indexing and
  554  multi-object transactions; so, improving their performance
  555  using CURP is an important problem with a broad impact.
  556  The most important piece missing from §3 to implement
  557  CURP is how to efficiently detect commutativity violations.
  558  Fortunately for NoSQL systems, CURP can use primary
  559  keys to efficiently check the commutativity of operations.
  560  NoSQL systems store data as a collection of objects, which
  561  are identified by primary keys. Most update operations in
  562  NoSQL specify the affected object with its primary key (or a
  563  list of primary keys), and update operations are commutative
  564  if they modify disjoint sets of objects. The rest of this section
  565  describes an implementation of CURP that exploits this
  566  efficient commutativity check.

  567  4.1 Life of A Witness
  568  Witnesses have two modes of operation: normal and
  569  recovery. In each mode, witnesses service a subset of
  570  operations listed in Figure 5. When it receives a start RPC,
  571  a witness starts its life for a master in normal mode, in
  572  which the witness is allowed to mutate its collection of saved
  573  requests. In normal mode, the witness services record RPCs
  574  for client requests targeted to the master for which the witness
  575  was configured by start; by accepting only requests for the
  576  correct master, CURP prevents clients from recording to
  577  incorrect witnesses. Also, witnesses drop their saved client
  578  requests as they receive gc RPCs from masters.
  579  A witness irreversibly switches to a recovery mode once
  580  it receives a getRecoveryData RPC. In recovery mode,
  581  mutations on the saved requests are prohibited; witnesses
  582  reject all record RPCs and only service getRecoveryData
  583  or end. As a recovery is completed and the witness becomes
  584  useless, the cluster coordinator may send end to free up the
  585  resources, so that the witness server can start another life for
  586  a different master.

  607  4.2 Data Structure of Witnesses
  608  Witnesses are designed to minimize the CPU cycles spent
  609  for handling record RPCs. For client requests mutating a
  610  single object, recording to a witness is similar to inserting
  611  in a set-associative cache; a record operation finds a set of
  612  slots using a hash of the object's primary key and writes
  613  the given request to an available slot in the set. To enforce
  614  commutativity, the witness searches the occupied slots in
  615  the set and rejects if there is another request with the same
  616  primary key (for performance, we compare 64-bit hashes of
  617  primary keys instead of full keys). If there is no slot available
  618  in the set for the key, the record operation is rejected as well.
  619  For client requests mutating multiple objects, witnesses
  620  perform the commutativity and space check for every affected
  621  object; to accept an update affecting n objects, a witness must
  622  ensure that (1) no existing client request mutates any of the
  623  n objects and (2) there is an available slot in each set for all n
  624  objects. If the update is commutative and space is available,
  625  the witness writes the update request n times as if recording
  626  n different requests on each object.

  627  4.3 Commutativity Checks in Masters
  628  Every NoSQL update operation changes the values of one
  629  or more objects. To enforce commutativity, a master can
  630  check if the objects touched (either updated or just read) by
  631  an operation are unsynced at the time of its execution. If an
  632  operation touches any unsynced value, it is not commutative
  633  and the master must sync all unsynced operations to backups
  634  before responding back to the client.
  635  If the object values are stored in a log, masters can
  636  determine if an object value is synced or not by comparing its
  637  position in the log against the last synced position.
  638  If the object values are not stored in a log, masters can use
  639  monotonically increasing timestamps. Whenever a master
  640  updates the value of an object, it tags the new value with a
  641  current timestamp. Also, the master keeps the timestamp of
  642  when last backup sync started. By comparing the timestamp
  643  of an object against the timestamp of the last backup sync,
  644  a master can tell whether the value of the object has been
  645  synced to backups.

  647  4.4 Improving Throughput of Masters
  648  Masters in primary-backup replication are usually the bottlenecks of systems since they drive replication to backups.
  649  Since masters in CURP can respond to clients before syncing
  650  to backups, they can delay syncs until the next batch without
  651  impacting latency. This batching of syncs improves masters'
  652  throughput in two ways.
  653  First, by batching replication RPCs, CURP reduces the
  654  number of RPCs a master must handle per client request.
  655  With 3-way primary-backup replication, a master must
  656  process 4 RPCs per client request (1 update RPC and 3
  657  replication RPCs). If the master batches replication and syncs
  658  every 10 client requests, it handles 1.3 RPCs on average. On
  659  NoSQL storage systems, sending and receiving RPCs takes a
  660  significant portion of the total processing time since NoSQL
  661  operations are not compute-heavy.
  662  Second, CURP eliminates wasted resources and other inefficiencies that arise when masters wait for syncs. For example,
  663  in the RAMCloud [27] storage system, request handlers use
  664  a polling loop to wait for completion of backup syncs. The
  665  syncs complete too quickly to context-switch to a different
  666  activity, but the polling still wastes more than half of the CPU
  667  cycles of the polling thread. With CURP, a master can complete a request without waiting for syncing and move on to the
  668  next request immediately, which results in higher throughput.
  669  The batch size of syncs is limited in CURP to reduce
  670  witness rejections. Delaying syncs increases the chance of
  671  finding non-commutative operations in witnesses and masters, causing extra rejections in witnesses and more blocking
  672  syncs in masters. A simple way to limit the batching would be
  673  for masters to issue a sync immediately after responding to a
  674  client if there is no outstanding sync; this strategy gives a reasonable throughput improvement since at most one CPU core
  675  will be used for syncing, and it also reduces witness rejections
  676  by syncing aggresively. However, to find the optimal batch
  677  size, an experiment with a system and real workload is necessary since each workload has a different sensitivity to larger
  678  batch sizes. For example, workloads which randomly access
  679  large numbers of keys uniformly can use a very large batch
  680  size without increasing the chance of commutativity conflicts.

  681  4.5 Garbage Collection
  682  As discussed in§3.4, masters send garbage collection RPCs
  683  for synced updates to their witnesses. Right after syncing to
  684  backups, masters sendgc RPCs (in Figure5), so the witnesses
  685  can discard data for the operations that were just synced.
  686  To identify client requests for removal, CURP uses 64-bit
  687  key hashes and RPC IDs assigned by RIFL [20]. Upon
  688  receiving a gc RPC, a witness locates the sets of slots using
  689  the keyHashes and resets the slots whose occupying requests
  690  have the matching RPC IDs. Witnesses ignore keyHashes
  691  and rpcIds that are not found since the record RPCs might
  692  have been rejected. For client requests that mutate multiple
  693  objects, gc RPCs include multiple ⟨keyHash, rpcIds⟩ pairs
  694  for all affected objects, so that witnesses can clear all slots

  696  occupied by the request.
  697  Although the described garbage collection can clean up
  698  most records, some slots may be left uncollected: if a client
  699  crashes before sending the update request to the master,
  700  or if the record RPC is delayed significantly and arrives
  701  after the master finished garbage collection for the update.
  702  Uncollected garbage will cause witnesses to indefinitely
  703  reject requests with the same keys.
  704  Witnesses detect such uncollected records and ask masters
  705  to retry garbage collection for them. When it rejects arecord,
  706  a witness recognizes the existing record as uncollected
  707  garbage if there have been many garbage collections since
  708  the record was written (three is a good number if a master
  709  performs only one gc RPC at a time). Witnesses notify
  710  masters of the requests that are suspected as uncollected
  711  garbage through the response messages of gc RPCs; then the
  712  masters retry the requests (most likely filtered by RIFL), sync
  713  to backups, and thus include them in the next gc requests.

  714  4.6 Recovery Steps
  715  To recover a crashed master, CURP first restores data
  716  from backups and then replays requests from a witness.
  717  To fetch the requests to replay, the new master sends a
  718  getRecoveryData RPC (in Figure 5), which has two effects:
  719  (1) it irreversibly sets the witness into recovery mode, so that
  720  the data in the witness will never change, (2) it provides the
  721  entire list of client requests saved in the witness.
  722  With the provided requests, the new master replays all of
  723  them. Since operations already recovered from backups will
  724  be filtered out by RIFL [20], the replay step finishes very
  725  quickly. In total, CURP increases recovery time by the execution time for a few requests plus 2 RTT (1 RTT for getRecoveryData and another RTT for backup sync after replay).

  726  4.7 Zombies
  727  For a fault-tolerant system to be consistent, it must neutralize zombies. A zombie is a server that has been determined
  728  to have crashed, so some other server has taken over its
  729  functions, but the server has not actually crashed (e.g., it may
  730  have suffered temporary network connectivity problems).
  731  Clients may continue to communicate with zombies; reads or
  732  updates accepted by a zombie may be inconsistent with the
  733  state of the replacement server.
  734  CURP assumes that the underlying system already has
  735  mechanisms to neutralize zombies (e.g., by asking backups
  736  to reject replication requests from a crashed master [27]).
  737  The witness mechanism provides additional safeguards.
  738  If a zombie responds to a client request without waiting
  739  for replication, then the client must communicate with all
  740  witnesses before completing the request. If it succeeds before
  741  the witness data has been replayed during recovery, then
  742  the update will be reflected in the new master. If the client
  743  contacts a witness after its data has been replayed, the witness
  744  will reject the request; the client will then discover that the
  745  old master has crashed and reissue its request to the new
  746  master. Thus, the witness mechanism does not create new

  763  We evaluated CURP by implementing it in the RAMCloud
  764  and Redis storage systems, which have very different backup

  766  show that CURP improves the performance of consistently
  767  replicated systems. Second, with the Redis implementation,
  768  we demonstrate that CURP can make strong consistency

  889  5.2 Resource Consumption by Witness Servers
  890  Each witness server implemented in RAMCloud can
  891  handle 1270k record requests per second with occasional
  892  garbage collection requests (1 every 50 writes) from master
  893  servers. A witness server runs on a single thread and consumes 1 hyper-thread core at max throughput. Considering
  894  that each RAMCloud master server uses 8 hyper-thread
  895  cores to achieve 728k writes per second, adding 1 witness
  896  increases the total CPU resources consumed by RAMCloud
  897  by 7%. However, CURP reduces the number of distinct
  898  backup operations performed by masters, because it enables
  899  batching; this offsets most of the cost of the witness requests
  900  (both backup and witness operations are so simple that most
  901  of their cost is the fixed cost of handling an RPC; a batched
  902  replication request costs about the same as a simple one).
  903  The second resource overhead is memory usage. Each
  904  witness server allocates 4096 request storage slots for each associated master, and each storage slot is 2KB. With additional
  905  metadata, the total memory overhead per master-witness pair
  906  is around 9MB.
  907  The third issue is network traffic amplification. In CURP,
  908  each update request is replicated both to witnesses and
  909  backups. With 3-way replication, CURP increases network
  910  bandwidth use for update operations by 75% (in the original
  911  RAMCloud, a client request is transferred over the network
  912  to a master and 3 backups).

  981  5.4 Making Redis Consistent and Durable
  982  Redis [30] is another low-latency in-memory key-value
  983  store, where values are data structures, such as lists, sets, etc.
  984  For Redis, the only way to achieve durability and consistency
  985  after crashes is to log client requests to an append-only file
  986  and invoke fsync before responding to clients. However,
  987  fsyncs can take several milliseconds, which is a 10–100x
  988  performance penalty. As a result, most Redis applications do
  989  not use synchronous mode; they use Redis as a cache with no
  990  durability guarantees. Redis also offers replication to multiple servers, but the replication mechanism is asynchronous,
  991  so updates can be lost after crashes; as a result, this feature is
  992  not widely used either.
  993  For this experiment, we used CURP to hide the cost of
  994  Redis' logging mechanism: we modified Redis to record
  995  operations on witnesses, so that operations can return
  996  without waiting for log syncs. Log data is then written
  997  asynchronously in the background. The result is a system
  998  with durability and consistency, but with performance
  999  equivalent to a system lacking both of these properties. In
 1000  this experiment the log data is not replicated, but the same
 1001  mechanism could be used to replicate the log data as well.
 1002  With the Redis implementation of CURP, we answered the
 1003  following questions:
 1004  • Can CURP transform a fast in-memory cache into a
 1005  strongly-consistent durable storage system without
 1006  degrading performance?
 1007  • How wide a range of operations can CURP support?
 1008  Measurements of the Redis implementation were conducted on a cluster of machines in CloudLab [29], whose
 1009  specifications are in Table 1. All measurements were collected using 10 Gbps networking and NVMe SSDs for Redis
 1010  backup files. Linux fsync on the NVMe SSDs takes around
 1011  50–100 µs; systems with SATA3 SSDs will perform worse
 1012  with the fsync-always option.
 1013  For the Redis implementation, we used Redis 3.2.8 for
 1014  servers and "C++ Client" [34] for clients. We modified "C++
 1015  Client" to construct Redis requests more quickly.

 1129  Egalitarian Paxos (EPaxos) [22] relies on commutativity
 1130  to allow multiple leaders to propose and execute operations
 1131  concurrently. This approach improves throughput. In georeplicated environments, EPaxos allows clients to choose a
 1132  nearby replica as leader, so operations can complete in 1 widearea RTT. However, in LAN environments, EPaxos clients
 1133  cannot hide the message delay to a leader, so operations take
 1134  2 RTT. Also, since EPaxos does not have a strong leader, read
 1135  operations must run through full consensus and be written to
 1136  replicated command logs; for read-heavy workloads, EPaxos
 1137  will perform worse than traditional 2 RTT protocols with
 1138  read leases, such as Raft [25]. On the other hand, CURP can
 1139  directly execute read operations in masters or even in backups
 1140  with the help of witnesses. Another limitation of EPaxos is
 1141  that clients in a datacenter that doesn't host a replica must use
 1142  a remote leader, increasing its latency to 2 wide-area RTTs.
 1143  Speculative Paxos [28] and Network-Ordered Paxos
 1144  (NOPaxos) [21] reduce latency almost to 1 RTT by serializing client requests within network. Both protocols use
 1145  SDNs to detour requests from all clients through a single
 1146  network device (a root layer switch or middlebox); so, they
 1147  can be deployed only in specialized environments (e.g. a
 1148  privately-owned datacenter). Also, due to detouring of
 1149  packets, they actually add latency overhead over unreplicated
 1150  systems; Speculative Paxos (∼25 µs) or NOPaxos(∼16 µs)
 1151  have higher latency overhead compared to CURP (∼1 µs).
 1152  TAPIR [37] and Janus [23] commit distributed transactions
 1153  in 1 wide-area RTT; before them, transaction commits took
 1154  2 RTTs: 1 for transaction prepares and 1 for geo-replicating
 1155  the data of prepare. They flattened out these serial steps by
 1156  replicating data before the prepare is executed. They modified concurrency control protocols to fix inconsistencies in
 1157  replications. They also require commutativity of workloads
 1158  for 1 RTT commits.
 1159  To avoid the performance penalty of consistent replications, eventual consistency [36] has been widely adopted in

 1160  industry [10, 8, 5]. Systems using eventual consistency return
 1161  from updates before replication is complete, and replications
 1162  happen asynchronously; since nearby replicas are stale,
 1163  clients must read from far-away masters for consistency.
 1164  Pileus [35] and Tuba [2] allowed applications to declare
 1165  their consistency and latency priorities, and they dynamically
 1166  select replicas to read from.
 1167  Broadcast-broadcast (BB) protocols [4, 3, 12, 16] for total
 1168  order broadcasts [11] have similarities to CURP. Senders in
 1169  BB protocols broadcast a message to all destinations (replicated processes) plus a sequencer before ordering, followed
 1170  by a second broadcast from the sequencer about the ordering
 1171  information. Some variants of BB protocols [3, 12] exploit
 1172  the fact that broadcasts are mostly delivered in-order in small
 1173  LAN environments and let processes optimistically consume
 1174  messages without waiting for the ordering information from
 1175  the sequencer. If the suspected order turned out to be different
 1176  from the order determined by the sequencer, the process must
 1177  rollback to correct the inconsistency. On the other hand, in
 1178  CURP, replicas wait for the ordered replication from a master
 1179  instead of executing operations with a presumed ordering, so
 1180  CURP doesn't require rollbacks, which is expensive and difficult to implement. Furthermore, even if client requests arrive
 1181  in a master and witnesses out of order, CURP still achieves 1
 1182  RTT as long as the reordered requests are commutative.

 1183  7 Conclusion
 1184  In this paper we have uncovered an opportunity for introducing concurrency into mechanisms for consistent replication. By exploiting the commutativity of operations, replication without ordering can be performed in parallel with sending requests to an execution server. This general approach can
 1185  be applied to improve a variety of replication mechanisms,
 1186  including primary-backup approaches and consensus protocols with strong leaders. We presented Consistent Unordered
 1187  Replication Protocol (CURP), which supplements standard
 1188  primary-backup replication mechanisms. CURP reduces the
 1189  latency to complete operations from 2 RTTs to 1 RTT while
 1190  retaining strong consistency. We implemented CURP in
 1191  RAMCloud and Redis to demonstrate its benefits.

 1329  A Informal Proof of Correctness
 1330  With the normal operation behaviors described in §3.2,
 1331  the recovery protocol in §3.3 guarantees the following
 1332  correctness properties.
 1333  • Durability: if a client completes an operation, it survives
 1334  server crashes.
 1335  • Consistency: if a client completes an operation, its result
 1336  returned to an application remains consistent after server
 1337  crash recoveries.
 1338  • Linearizability: an operation appears to be executed
 1339  exactly once between start and completion.
 1340  Before presenting proofs, we reiterate some key behaviors
 1341  of the CURP protocol.
 1342  (Rule 1) from §3.2.1, a client only completes an update
 1343  operation if (1) it is recorded in all f witnesses or (2) it is
 1344  replicated to f backups.
 1345  (Rule 2) a completed unsynced operation must be individually commutative with all preceding operations that are not
 1346  synced yet. This is the behavior described in §3.2.3; a master
 1347  must sync before responding if the current operation is not
 1348  commutative with any other existing (preceding) unsynced
 1349  operations.
 1350  Now, we present proof sketches for the properties.
 1351  Durability: recovery of a master only completes after
 1352  recovery from 1 backup and 1 witness, and the completed
 1353  operation must exist in the backup or the witness by (Rule 1);
 1354  thus, the completed operation must be recovered when the
 1355  recovery is completed.
 1356  Consistency: Consider an individual completed operation
 1357  α and its consistency. To prove thatα's result doesn't change
 1358  even after crash recovery, we will think about the operation
 1359  execution sequence before α, which we will call history of α
 1360  (or H ).
 1361  α
 1362  Case 1: the operation α has been synced to the backup
 1363  used for recovery. This operation will be recovered from
 1364  the backup (phase 1) and any replay from witnesses (phase
 1365  2) will be ignored (by RIFL). Since backup syncs preserve
 1366  the execution order of operations, the H didn't change; so
 1367  α
 1368  the post-recovery execution sequence should regenerate the
 1369  original execution result of α.
 1370  Case 2: the operation α has not been synced to the backup
 1371  used for recovery. α must have been recorded in all witnesses
 1372  by (Rule 1) and will be recovered during phase 2. We can
 1373  split the original execution history of α into two parts as in
 1374  Figure 3: ⟨synced⟩ followed by ⟨unsynced⟩. The 1st phase
 1375  of recovery will recover the exactly same execution history
 1376  for the ⟨synced⟩ part. By (Rule 2), we know that losing any
 1377  ⟨unsynced⟩ part of history after crash will not change the
 1378  execution result of α. During phase 2 of recovery (from
 1379  a witness), we may replay some other operations before
 1380  replaying α, but the result of α doesn't change since all
 1381  operations recorded in the witness must be commutative.
 1382  Linearizability: we assume that the underlying system
 1383  before applying CURP guarantees linearizability for op-

 1384  USENIX Association

 1385  erations that are replicated to backups. CURP may break
 1386  the linearizability of the underlying system since masters in
 1387  CURP return before syncing to backups. So, we will reason
 1388  about how CURP recovers from master crashes without
 1389  breaking linearizability.
 1390  The definition of linearizability can be reworded as
 1391  following: if the execution of an operation is observed by
 1392  the issuing client or other clients, no contrary observation
 1393  can occur afterwards (i.e. it should not appear to revert or
 1394  be reordered). Since we only care about what happens after
 1395  recovery, we prove the following proposition: if the execution
 1396  of an individual operation α is observed before crash, no
 1397  contrary observation can occur after recovery.
 1398  Case 1: the execution of α was observed by other dependent operations (e.g. reads). By (Rule 2), the master must
 1399  have synced α to backups since dependent operations don't
 1400  commute with α. Since it was replicated to backups, α will
 1401  be linearizable as long as the underlying system is.
 1402  Case 2: the execution was observed only by the completion
 1403  ofα. α must be recovered because of the Durability property.
 1404  The only observation about α before crash was the returned
 1405  execution result, and it must be still consistent even after
 1406  recovery because of the Consistency property.
 1407  Case 3: no observation was made before crash. α may
 1408  be lost if it didn't reach to either the backup or witness used
 1409  for recovery. In CURP, the client keeps retrying until it can
 1410  complete α. Regardless of whether α was recovered or not,
 1411  RIFL ensures the retry will only execute α at-most once and
 1412  return the result of the sole execution.

 1413  B Extra Discussions

 1414  B.1 Why Are Witnesses Separate from Backups?
 1415  By having witnesses separated from backups, CURP
 1416  requires fewer changes to the existing systems and is more
 1417  applicable to many wildly different backup mechanisms.
 1418  Both of our two implementations leveraged this flexibility:
 1419  in RAMCloud, a master keeps changing backups to which
 1420  it replicates (to spread data over the entire cluster), so
 1421  clients don't know which backups are currently used by the
 1422  master; in Redis, operation logs are stored in local disks to
 1423  ensure durability, so there are no separate backup servers
 1424  to which CURP clients can record inputs. Thus, separating
 1425  witnesses from backups improves CURPs applicability to
 1426  many existing primary-backup systems.
 1427  On the other hand, when designing a new storage system,
 1428  combining witnesses and backups can bring extra performance benefits. When they are combined, clients directly
 1429  send requests to a master and backups, which now also serve
 1430  as witnesses. The key change is masters now sync operation
 1431  orders (by listing IDs as in witness gc RPCs) instead of full
 1432  client requests; then backups lookup the matching requests
 1433  from their witness storage and move them to backup logs.
 1434  This approach will lower network bandwidth consumption.
 1435  Also, most witness gc RPCs can be eliminated; immediately
=== PAGE 17 ===

 1436  62 16th USENIX Symposium on Networked Systems Design and Implementation

 1437  after handling the sync, the requests in the witness storage
 1438  can be deleted as they are now safe in the backup log. (For
 1439  safety, the recovery protocol must pick 1 witness/backup
 1440  combo and must not mix.) This saving of gc RPCs will
 1441  improve masters' throughput and will reduce the chance of
 1442  commutativity conflicts.

 1443  B.2 Extending CURP to Consensus Protocols
 1444  This section illustrates how CURP can be extended to
 1445  reduce the latency of consensus protocols. CURP can be integrated in most consensus protocols with strong leaders (e.g.
 1446  Raft [25], Viewstamped Replication [24]). In such protocols,
 1447  clients send requests to the current leader, which serializes
 1448  the requests into its command log. The leader then replicates
 1449  its command log to a majority of replicas before executing
 1450  the requests and replying back to clients with the results. This
 1451  process takes 2 RTTs, and CURP can reduce it to 1 RTT.
 1452  As in primary-backup replication, CURP on consensus
 1453  allows clients to replicate requests to witnesses in parallel
 1454  with sending requests to the leader; the leader then speculatively executes the requests and responds to clients before
 1455  replicating the requests to a quorum of replicas. A client can
 1456  complete an operation if it is accepted by a superquorum of
 1457  witnesses or committed in a quorum of replicas.
 1458  To mask f failures, consensus protocols use 2f +1 replicas, and systems stay available with f failed replicas. For the
 1459  same guarantee, CURP also uses 2f + 1 replicas, but each
 1460  replica also has a witness component in addition to existing
 1461  components for consensus. Although CURP can proceed
 1462  with f +1 available replicas, it needs f +df /2e+1 replicas
 1463  (for superquorum of witnesses) to use 1 RTT operations.
 1464  With less than f +df /2e+1 replicas, clients must ask masters
 1465  to commit operations in f +1 replicas before returning result
 1466  (2 RTTs).
 1467  Like masters in regular CURP, leader replicas execute operations speculatively if they are commutative with existing unsynced operations; for an incoming client request, the leader
 1468  serializes it into its command log, executes it, and responds
 1469  to the client before committing it in a majority of replicas.
 1470  For clients to complete an operation in 1 RTT, it must be
 1471  recorded in a superquorum of f +df/2e+1 witnesses. The
 1472  reason why CURP needs a superquorum instead of a simple
 1473  majority is to ensure commutativity of replays from witnesses
 1474  during recovery. During recovery, only f + 1 out of 2f + 1
 1475  replicas (each of which embeds a witness) might be available.
 1476  If a client could complete an operation after recording to
 1477  f +1 witnesses, the completed operation may exist in only
 1478  1 witness out of available f + 1 witnesses during recovery
 1479  (since intersection of two quorums is 1 replica). If the other f
 1480  witnesses accepted other operations that are not commutative
 1481  with the completed operation (since each witness enforces
 1482  commutativity individually), recovery cannot distinguish
 1483  which one is the completed one; executing all appearing
 1484  in any f + 1 witnesses is also not safe since they are not
 1485  commutative, so they must be replayed in a correct order.

 1486  For correctness, the client requests replayed from witnesses
 1487  during recovery must be commutative and inclusive of all
 1488  completed operations that are not yet committed in a majority of replicas. By recording to a superquorum, all completed
 1489  operations (but not yet committed) are guaranteed to exist in
 1490  a majority (df /2e+1) of any quorum of f +1 witnesses, and
 1491  any operations that don't commute with the completed operations cannot exist in more than bf/2c (less than majority of
 1492  any quorum). Thus, during recovery, all requests that appear
 1493  in a majority (df/2e+1) from any quorum of f +1 witnesses
 1494  are guaranteed to be commutative and include all completed
 1495  operations; so, recovery can replay requests that appear in
 1496  more than df/2e+1 witnesses out of any f +1 witnesses.
 1497  When leadership changes (e.g. leader election in Raft [25]
 1498  or view change in Viewstamped Replication [24]), the new
 1499  leader must recover from witnesses before accepting new operations. To do so, the new leader must collect saved requests
 1500  from at least f +1 witnesses. This collection can be included
 1501  in the existing data collection (e.g. Raft votes) that is required
 1502  by most leadership change protocols. As mentioned in the
 1503  previous paragraph, the new leader should only replay client
 1504  requests that are recorded in at least df /2e +1 witnesses to
 1505  ensure commutativity.
 1506  After leadership changes, the state machine of the old
 1507  leader could have diverged from other replicas due to
 1508  speculatively executed operations that were not recovered
 1509  from witnesses. To fix this, the old leader must reload from
 1510  a checkpoint that does not have speculative executions.
 1511  However, we can avoid reloading from checkpoints if the
 1512  leadership change was not because of a crash or disconnect of
 1513  the old leader; instead of requring old leader to reload from a
 1514  checkpoint, we can require the new leader to fetch and commit
 1515  all uncommitted operations in the old leader's command log.
 1516  The last problem introduced by speculative execution is
 1517  that clients may use old zombie leaders (which believe they
 1518  are current leaders). Zombie leaders were not possible before
 1519  CURP since an operation must be committed in a majority
 1520  before being executed and at least one replica would reject
 1521  the operation. To prevent clients from completing operations
 1522  with an old (possibly disconnected) leader, they tag record
 1523  RPCs with a term number (e.g. a Raft term or a view-number
 1524  in Viewstamped Replication), which increments every time
 1525  when leadership changes. A witness checks the term number
 1526  against the term used by its replica (recall that a witness is a
 1527  part of a consensus replica); if the record RPC has an old term
 1528  number, the witness rejects the request and tells the client to
 1529  fetch new leader information.
 1530  CURP can use read leases like many consensus protocols
 1531  so that read operations can be executed solely by leaders
 1532  within 1 RTT without recording to witnesses. Optimizing
 1533  read operations using read leases is common for consensus
 1534  protocols with strong leaders. A leader replica with a
 1535  valid read lease can safely execute read operations without
 1536  committing the read operations through consensus. For the

 1537  USENIX Association
=== PAGE 18 ===

 1538  16th USENIX Symposium on Networked Systems Design and Implementation 63

 1539  optimization, each replica grants the read lease to the current
 1540  leader, promising not to agree on a leader change for a lease
 1541  period. With valid leases from a majority of replicas, the
 1542  leader knows that no operations can be committed from other
 1543  replicas, so it can safely execute read operations without
 1544  consulting with other replicas. CURP does not interfere with
 1545  this read lease mechanism.

 1546  B.3 Why Do Fast / Generalized Paxos require 1.5 RTTs?
 1547  There is a widespread misunderstanding that both Fast
 1548  Paxos and Generalized Paxos already achieve 1 RTT operations. The confusion probably stems from the fact that both
 1549  Fast and Generalized Paxos allow Paxos learners to know
 1550  about acceptance of an operation in 1 RTT.
 1551  However, 1 RTT is sufficient to know only that an operation
 1552  is committed but not enough to know the result: that requires
 1553  another 0.5 RTT. The abstract for Generalized Paxos says that
 1554  a server can execute the command in two message delays;
 1555  however, it take an additional message delay for the result to
 1556  reach a client, for a total of three message delays (1.5 RTT). It
 1557  doesn't help for the client to be a Paxos learner, because even
 1558  learners don't know the result after 1 RTT.
 1559  For most operations, results are not trivial and clients must
 1560  wait for the results from real executions before completing
 1561  operations. Many writes, such as conditional writes or readmodify-writes, have results that clients cannot know before
 1562  executions. Blind writes (those that don't return results)
 1563  could potentially complete in 1 RTT. However, truly blind
 1564  writes are rarely feasible because they can return exceptions,
 1565  such as "table no longer on this server" or "permission
 1566  denied"; clients must be aware of these exceptions.
 1567  As a result, Fast/Generalized Paxos are generally considered to have 1.5 RTT latency for clients to complete
 1568  operations. [21, 28,38]

 1569  C Implementation Details

 1570  C.1 Modifications to RIFL
 1571  RIFL [20] is a mechanism for detecting duplicate invocations of RPCs. With RIFL, masters make a durable completion record of each RPC that updates state, which includes
 1572  the RPC result. The completion record survives crashes and
 1573  can be used to detect duplicate invocations of the RPC. When
 1574  a duplicate is detected, the master skips the execution of the
 1575  RPC and returns the result from the completion record.
 1576  RIFL has two mechanisms for garbage collecting completion records: (1) on RPC requests, clients piggyback
 1577  acknowledgments of the results of their previous requests (so
 1578  servers can safely delete these completion records), and (2)
 1579  clients maintain leases in a central server; if a client's lease
 1580  expires, masters can delete all completion records for that
 1581  client. Both of these must be modified to work with CURP.
 1582  Since both garbage collection mechanisms assume that
 1583  retries always come from the same client that made the
 1584  original request, RIFL must be modified to accommodate
 1585  retries from witnesses. Firstly, once clients acknowledge

 1620  C.2 Why Use Set-associative Cache for Witnesses?
 1621  We initially used a direct-mapped cache instead of setassociative cache, but this resulted in a high rate of rejections
 1622  because of conflicts (i.e. no slot is available for the mapped
 1623  set). Figure 14 shows the expected number of recordings
 1624  before a conflict occurs on a witness slot. Using a direct
 1625  mapping and 4096 total slots, it is expected to have a false
 1626  conflict after about 80 insertions. Thus, we switched to
 1627  4-way associative cache, to reduce witness rejections. We
 1628  didn't need 8-way associativity (a bit slower than 4-way)
 1629  since the number of requests in witnesses is already limited
 1630  by commutativity. (Once a master hits a non-commutative
 1631  operation and syncs to backups, all saved requests in the
 1632  witness are garbage collected.)
