  This paper provides a realization of distributed leader election without having any eventual timely links. Progress is guaranteed in the following weak setting: Eventually one process can send messages such that every message obtains f timely responses, where f is a resilience bound.    21  1 Introduction

   22  A fundamental design guideline pioneered in the Paxos protocol [1] and later
   23  employed in numerous coordination protocols is to separate safety properties
   24  from liveness properties. Safety must be preserved at all times, and hence, its
   25  implementation must not rely on synchrony assumptions. Liveness, on the other
   26  hand, may be hampered during periods of instability, but eventually, when the
   27  system resumes normal behavior, progress should be guaranteed. In various coordination protocols such as Paxos, liveness hinges on a separate leader election
   28  algorithm, with the problem of finding a good leader election algorithm left open.
   29  It is well known in the theory of distributed computing that liveness of consensus cannot be guaranteed in a purely asynchronous system with no timing
   30  assumptions [2]. Ω is known to be the weakest failure detector [3,4] that is
   31  sufficient for consensus, hence provides the liveness properties of consensus. Ω

   36  essentially implements an eventual leader election, where all non-faulty processes
   37  eventually trust the same non-faulty process as the leader.
   38  While Ω captures the abstract properties needed to provide liveness, it does
   39  not say under which pragmatic system conditions is progress guaranteed. It
   40  leaves open the interesting questions of what synchrony conditions should be
   41  assumed when implementing Ω and what additional properties would yield an
   42  ideal leader election algorithm for practical coordination schemes such as Paxos.

   43  A revisit of Paxos. In this paper, rather than cooking up arbitrary synchrony
   44  assumptions and additional properties, we derive the desired features of our
   45  protocols from Paxos, a cornerstone coordination scheme employed in various
   46  reliable storage systems such as Petal [5], Frangipani [6], Chain Replication [7],
   47  and Boxwood [8].
   48  At a high level, Paxos is a protocol for a set of processes to reach consensus
   49  on a series of proposals. With a leader election algorithm, a process p that is
   50  elected leader first carries out the prepare phase of the protocol. In this phase,
   51  p sends a prepare message to all processes to declare the ballot number it uses
   52  for its proposals, learns about all the existing proposals, and requests promises
   53  that no smaller ballot numbers be accepted afterwards. The prepare phase is
   54  completed once p receives acknowledgments from n − f processes. Once the
   55  prepare phase is completed, to have a proposal committed, leader p initiates the
   56  accept phase by sending an accept message to all processes with the proposal and
   57  the ballot number it declares in the prepare phase. The proposal is committed
   58  when p receives acknowledgments from f + 1 processes. Whenever a higher
   59  ballot number is encountered in the prepare phase or the accept phase, the
   60  leader has to initiate a new prepare phase with an even higher ballot number.
   61  This could happen if there are other processes acting as leaders, unavoidable in
   62  an asynchronous system.
   63  To implement a replicated state machine, Paxos streamlines a series of consensus decisions. A new leaderpcarries out the preparephase once for all its proposals.
   64  After the completion of the prepare phase,p carries out only the accept phase for
   65  each proposal until a new leader emerges by initiating a new prepare phase.
   66  Our goal is to distill the conditions under which Paxos can have new proposals
   67  committed in a timely fashion and to provide a leader election protocol exactly
   68  under those conditions. Therefore, we make the following observations:
   69  – After an initial prepare phase, in order for a leader p to make timely
   70  progress, it suffices for p to obtain timely responses for its accept message
   71  from any set of f + 1 processes (or f processes besides itself). The set could
   72  change for different accept messages.
   73  – Any leader change incurs the cost of an extra round of communication for
   74  the prepare phase.

   80  First, the algorithm guarantees to elect a leader without having any eventual
   81  timely links. Progress is guaranteed in the following surprisingly weak setting:
   82  Eventually one process can send messages such that every message obtains f
   83  timely responses, where f is a resilience bound. We name such a process 3faccessible. A crucial facet of this property is that the f responders need not be
   84  fixed, and may change from one message to another. We emphasize that this
   85  condition stems from the workings of Paxos, whose safety does not necessitate
   86  that the f processes with which a leader interacts be fixed.
   87  Our solution bears the following ramification on the foundations of distributed computing. It implies that the FLP [2] impossibility result on consensus
   88  with one failure (f = 1) is circumvented if one process can at any time interact
   89  in a timely manner with one other process in the system.
   90  No previous leader election protocol provides any guarantee in these settings.
   91  In fact, the approach taken in most previous protocols is fundamentally incompatible with this condition, because previous protocols gossip about suspicions
   92  until the system converges. This does not allow for a leader to communicate at
   93  different times with different subsets of the system, as the leader will constantly
   94  be under suspicion of some part of the system. Thus, no easy "engineering" of
   95  previous protocols can provide progress under the 3f-accessibility condition.
   96  The second contribution provided by our algorithm is leader stability. This
   97  is based on the observation that a leader change necessitates an execution of
   98  a prepare phase by the new leader, an often costly operation. We therefore
   99  embrace the notion of stability that a qualified leader not be demoted, where a
  100  leader is considered qualified if it remains capable of having proposals committed
  101  in a timely fashion. For Paxos, when n = 2f + 1 holds, a leader is qualified if it is
  102  non-faulty and maintains timely communication with a set of f other processes
  103  at all times, with the set possibly changing over time.
  104  An important practical measure for a leader election protocol is its communication complexity [9]. It is desirable that under a steady state, where a qualified
  105  leader operates without being suspected by any other process, only the leader
  106  incurs periodic communication with the rest of the system (hence achieving O(n)
  107  steady-state link-utilization communication complexity.) In Section 6, we sketch
  108  an extension of our protocol that achieves this ideal. This extension works only
  109  with the basic version of our protocol for Ω, which does not uphold stability. It
  110  is left as an open question whether a stable leader election algorithm with O(n)
  111  steady-state message complexity exists under the condition of 3f accessibility.

  191  3 Informal Model

  192  The system consists of a set P of n processes, each pair of which can directly
  193  communicate by sending and receiving messages over a bi-directional link. Each
  194  process is equipped with a drift-free local clock. Clocks of different processes need
  195  not be synchronized. When we reason about the system, we often use a global
  196  wall-clock t, which is not known or used by the processes within the system.
  197  Each process executes a sequence of steps triggered either by message reception or timer expiration. In a step, a process may perform any number of local
  198  computations, send messages, and set timers. For simplicity, we denote the time
  199  it takes to perform a step as zero.

  209  Communication Synchrony. The conditions regarding timeliness of links are at
  210  the heart of our investigation. There is a known upper bound δ on the round-trip
  211  delay of messages, but it does not hold on all pairs of processes at all times. What
  212  is known is that eventually there is one process that is able to exchange messages
  213  within the δ delay with f other processes. We will now make this notion precise.
  214  Definition 1. Let (p, q) denote the communication link between p and q. We say
  215  that (p, q) is timely at time t if any message sent by p to q at time t receives a
  216  response within δ time. Note that if q becomes faulty before handling p's message,
  217  or q is slow to respond, then by definition the link is not timely.
  218  Definition 2. A process p ∈ P is said to be f-accessible at time t if there exist
  219  f other processes q such that the links (p, q) are timely at t.
  220  Our synchrony requirement is the following.
  221  Definition 3. (3f-accessibility) There is a time t and a process p such that for
  222  all t ≥ t, p is f-accessible at t .

  254  4 Ω with 3f-Accessibility

  255  Our first protocol implements Ω under the 3f-accessibility condition. The protocol for process p appears in Figure 1. It works as follows.
  256  Each process maintains for itself a non-decreasing epoch number, as well as an
  257  epoch freshness counter. Epochs are implemented using the following data types
  258  and variables. An epoch number is a pair that consists of an integer field named
  259  serialNum and another field named processId, which stores either a process id
  260  or null. We assume a total ordering on process ids with null smaller than any
  261  process id. Epoch numbers are ordered lexicographically, first by serialNum and
  262  then by processId.
  263  We define a state to be a pair consisting of an epoch-number field named
  264  epochNum and an integer field named freshness. States are ordered lexicographically, first by epochNum and then by freshness.
  265  A process refreshes its epoch number in fixed periodicity of length ∆, by
  266  incrementing the epoch freshness counter and writing it to its registry, which is
  267  replicated on all processes in the system. If the refresh fails to complete updating
  268  the registry at f + 1 processes within the known δ round-trip bound, the process
  269  increases its own epoch number. The vector registry[] records locally at each
  270  process the latest state it received from others: registry[q] is updated upon receipt
  271  of a refresh message from q.
  272  Process p records the states it reads of all other processes in a vector named
  273  views[]. A process updates its view by periodically reading the entire registry
  274  vector from n − f processes. Each entry views[q] has two fields. One is a state
  275  field, and the other is a bit called expired indicating whether q's state has been
  276  continuously refreshed or not. Initially, all serialNum and freshness fields are
  277  zeroed, and expired field set to true.
  278  The idea is to select as a leader the process with the lowest non-expired epoch
  279  number (breaking ties using process ids). To assess whether an epoch number
  280  has expired or not, every process reads the registry of all processes from n − f
  281  processes periodically. The exact period between the completion of a previous
  282  read and the start of the next must be at least ∆ + δ to guarantee that every

  308  5 Stability

  309  Driven by our need to employ Ω within repeated consensus instances of the
  310  Paxos protocol, we now introduce a crucial addition to Ω.
  311  The definition of Ω mandates that eventually a single leader stabilizes and
  312  is never replaced. However, it allows many leaders to be replaced many times
  313  until that time arrives. This is undesirable in many respects. In Paxos, replacing
  314  a leader is a costly operation. The new leader needs to perform an extra round
  315  of communication in order to collect information about the latest actions of the
  316  previous leader. In many other settings, electing a new leader involves heavy
  317  re-configuration procedures, which should be avoided if possible.
  318  We therefore would like to require that a qualified leader (e.g., a 3f-accessible
  319  leader) never be demoted. To this end, we first need to define precisely what it
  320  means for a process to be a leader. Our definition is simple and is grounded in
  321  practice: A process p is a leader at time t if it considers itself a leader at time t.
  322  More precisely, we have the following simple definition:

  371  Ω with Stability

  372  In this section, we introduce changes to the above protocol in order to provide
  373  for leader stability. In order for these changes to work, however, we require
  374  n = 2f + 1.3
  375  In the protocol of Figure 1, p considers itself a leader immediately when p sets
  376  leaderp to p; that is, when p's current epoch number is the lowest non-expired
  377  epoch number in p's view. This is insufficient; the scenario that disrupts stability
  378  is as follows. Suppose a process p becomes a leader at time t because its current
  379  epoch number ep is the lowest non-expired epoch number in its view at t. In the
  380  meantime, another process q times out on an epoch number eq < ep − 1 and
  381  advances to a new epoch number eq + 1 < ep. If q now becomes f-accessible,
  382  eq + 1 will eventually become the lowest epoch number, demoting leader p even
  383  if p has been f-accessible; leader stability is thus violated.
  384  To achieve stability, for a process p to become a leader, we not only require
  385  that p's epoch number be the lowest non-expired epoch number in p's view, but
  386  further require that p declare itself a leader only after making sure that no nonexpired lower epoch number will cause other processes to claim leadership. This
  387  can be achieved by the following two extensions to the first protocol:

  388  1. Whenever a process initiates a new epoch number, rather than incrementing
  389  the epoch number by 1, it learns the highest existing epoch number through
  390  a timely communication (with bound δ) with n−f processes and then picks
  391  an epoch number that is higher than any existing epoch number.
  392  2. Process p not only checks whether its current epoch number is the lowest in
  393  its current view, but also waits for sufficiently long to ensure that all nonexpiring epoch numbers that can be lower than ep must have been reflected
  394  in p's view.

  396  a process p has to wait until the completion of a collect/status round that

  398  and f-accessible p will start its first refresh for ep at t + ∆ and receive f + 1

  462  6 Reducing Message Complexity

  463  As suggested in [9], a crucial measure of communication complexity is the number
  464  of links that are utilized infinitely often in the protocol. Our protocols use all-toall communication infinitely often to keep leader information up to date, hence
  465  employ O(n2) infinite-utilization links.
  466  For the protocol in Figure 1, the steady-state communication complexity can
  467  be reduced to O(n), where in a steady state there exists a unique f-accessible
  468  leader that is never suspected by any non-faulty process. We briefly sketch the
  469  required changes here. The full paper [19] contains a precise protocol description
  470  and its correctness proof.
  471  The first change is related to the refreshing of epoch numbers. A process
  472  p that is not currently the leader need not refresh its own epoch number; it
  473  can simply let it become inactive, since it is not contending for the leadership.
  474  Therefore, we disable the periodic refresh at p when it is not a leader. A process increments its epoch number only when it experiences a roundTripTimer
  475  timeout, as in the original protocol, and may "revive" an inactive epoch number
  476  when becoming a leader.
  477  The second change is related to the monitoring of epoch numbers in the
  478  system. In a steady state, there is no reason for a process p to monitor the states
  479  of all other processes. Therefore, we disable periodic collect altogether.
  480  A process p that does not obtain any refresh message carrying the current
  481  presumed leader's epoch number for some timeout period suspects that the current leader has failed. Likewise, a process p that hears a refresh message carrying
=== PAGE 13 ===

  482  Leader Election and Stability Without Eventual Timely Links 211

  483  a lower epoch number than the current presumed leader's epoch number assumes
  484  that it does not have up-to-date information about the current leader .
  485  In these two cases (only), a process activates the collect procedure twice,
  486  where the second one is activated at least ∆ + δ time units after the first one
  487  completes, as in the original protocol. Process p then determines the lowest active
  488  epoch number and compares it with its current epoch number. If p's current
  489  epoch number is no higher than the lowest active epoch number, p becomes a
  490  leader and activates refresh periodically as in the original protocol. Otherwise,
  491  p will consider the process owning the lowest epoch number as the leader and
  492  expect to receive refresh messages from that process periodically.
  493  The intuition behind the success of the modified protocol is somewhat similar
  494  to our original protocol, but with crucial differences. As before, consider a process
  495  p that, after a certain time t, always manages to write its registry to f other
  496  processes within δ. It follows that eventually p stops increasing its epoch number.
  497  Note that this is true for any 3f-accessible process.
  498  Now, consider a non-crashed process q with the lowest current epoch number
  499  in the system. If q is not the leader yet, then q believes that there exists a lower
  500  active epoch number than its own. Because such an epoch number no longer
  501  exists, eventually q times out on that epoch number and performs two collects.
  502  Because its epoch number is the lowest among the non-crashed processes, it will
  503  learn that its epoch number is no higher than the lowest active epoch number
  504  in the system and become a leader. If q is not 3f-accessible, eventually it will
  505  fail updating its own freshness counter and will increase its epoch number.
  506  Together, we have that, on the one hand, the 3f-accessible processes stop
  507  increasing their epoch numbers. On the other hand, any non 3f-accessible process either crashes or increases its own epoch number to be higher than the
  508  lowest epoch number in the system. As before, the process p whose epoch stops
  509  increasing at the lowest value in the system becomes a permanent leader.
  510  In terms of message complexity, once an f-accessible leader is elected and all
  511  processes receive its refresh messages without suspecting the leader, eventually
  512  all non-leader processes stop refreshing their epochs and stop reading, hence the
  513  communication complexity drops to O(n).

  514  7 Discussion

  515  The condition we introduced to uphold stability in this paper, namelyn = 2f +1,
  516  is stronger than what is required in practice. It is worth noting that, for both
  517  Paxos and our stable leader election protocol, it suffices for a leader p to interact
  518  in a timely fashion once with n −f processes. Subsequently, p can maintain its
  519  leadership and proceed with consensus decisions, provided that it can interact
  520  at any time with f + 1 processes.
  521  Stability also appears to be in conflict with the ability to reduce the steadystate message complexity to O(n). Intuitively, the reduced message complexity
  522  forces a process to decide whether to become a leader based on less accurate information, thereby creating opportunities for unnecessary demotion. For example,
=== PAGE 14 ===

  523  212 D. Malkhi, F. Oprea, and L. Zhou

  524  in our protocol, to ensure stability, a process becomes a leader only when it is certain that no process can have a lower active epoch number. This is hard because
  525  epoch numbers can remain inactive (and unknown to other processes) before they
  526  are revived. It is left as an open question whether a stable leader protocol exists
  527  under 3f-accessibility with O(n) steady-state message complexity.

  528  8 Conclusion

  529  It is our firm belief that leader election algorithms that implement Ω should be
  530  studied in the context of practical coordination schemes that realize consensus.
  531  This paper makes two contributions toward this goal.
  532  First, it contributes to the study of weak synchrony conditions that enable
  533  leader election. 3f-accessibility, the synchrony condition we require, is new and
  534  surprisingly weak, in that it requires no eventual timely links. It is incomparable to (but also not stronger than) previously known conditions for leader
  535  election. The condition is derived by our observations on Paxos, leading to an
  536  implementation of Ω under f-accessibility.
  537  Second, it provides practical and stable leader election protocol that eliminates unnecessary and potentially expensive leader changes. The paper therefore
  538  provides Paxos with a "good" leader election protocol; this was left as an open
  539  problem in Lamport's original Paxos paper [1].
