   22  Abstract
   23  We introduce protocol-aware recovery (PAR), a new approach that exploits protocol-specific knowledge to correctly recover from storage faults in distributed systems. We demonstrate the efficacy of PAR through
   24  the design and implementation of corruption-tolerant
   25  replication (CTRL), a PAR mechanism specific to replicated state machine (RSM) systems. We experimentally
   26  show that the CTRL versions of two systems, LogCabin
   27  and ZooKeeper, safely recover from storage faults and
   28  provide high availability, while the unmodified versions
   29  can lose data or become unavailable. We also show that
   30  the CTRL versions have little performance overhead.

   31  1 Introduction
   32  Failure recovery using redundancy is central to improved
   33  reliability of distributed systems [14, 22, 31, 35, 61, 67].
   34  Distributed systems recover from node crashes and network failures using copies of data and functionality on
   35  several nodes [6, 47, 55]. Similarly, bad or corrupted data
   36  on one node should be recovered from redundant copies.
   37  In a static setting where all nodes always remain
   38  reachable and where clients do not actively update data,
   39  recovering corrupted data from replicas is straightforward; in such a setting, a node could repair its state by
   40  simply fetching the data from any other node.
   41  In reality, however, a distributed system is a dynamic
   42  environment, constantly in a state of flux. In such
   43  settings, orchestrating recovery correctly is surprisingly
   44  hard. As a simple example, consider a quorum-based
   45  system, in which a piece of data is corrupted on one node.
   46  When the node tries to recover its data, some nodes may
   47  fail and be unreachable, some nodes may have recently
   48  recovered from a failure and so lack the required data or
   49  hold a stale version. If enough care is not exercised, the
   50  node could "fix" its data from a stale node, overwriting
   51  the new data, potentially leading to a data loss.
   52  To correctly recover corrupted data from redundant
   53  copies in a distributed system, we propose that a recovery
   54  approach should be protocol-aware. A protocol-aware
   55  recovery (PAR) approach is carefully designed based on
   56  how the distributed system performs updates to its replicated data, elects the leader, etc. For instance, in the previous example, a PAR mechanism would realize that a
   57  faulty node has to query at least R (read quorum) other
   58  nodes to safely and quickly recover its data.

   62  In this paper, we apply PAR to replicated state machine (RSM) systems. We focus on RSM systems for
   63  two reasons. First, correctly implementing recovery is
   64  most challenging for RSM systems because of the strong
   65  consistency and durability guarantees they provide [58];
   66  a small misstep in recovery could violate the guarantees. Second, the reliability of RSM systems is crucial:
   67  many systems entrust RSM systems with their critical
   68  data [45]. For example, Bigtable, GFS, and other systems [7,26] store their metadata on RSM systems such as
   69  Chubby [16] or ZooKeeper [4]. Hence, protecting RSM
   70  systems from storage faults such as data corruption will
   71  improve the reliability of many dependent systems.
   72  We first characterize the different approaches to handling storage faults by developing the RSM recovery
   73  taxonomy, through experimental and qualitative analysis of practical systems and methods proposed by prior
   74  research (§2). Our analyses show that most approaches
   75  employed by currently deployed systems do not use any
   76  protocol-level knowledge to perform recovery, leading to
   77  disastrous outcomes such as data loss and unavailability.
   78  Thus, to improve the resiliency of RSM systems to
   79  storage faults, we design a new protocol-aware recovery approach that we call corruption-tolerant replication
   80  or CTRL (§3). CTRL constitutes two components: a local storage layer and a distributed recovery protocol;
   81  while the storage layer reliably detects faults, the distributed protocol recovers faulty data from redundant
   82  copies. Both the components carefully exploit RSMspecific knowledge to ensure safety (e.g., no data loss)
   83  and high availability.
   84  CTRL applies several novel techniques to achieve
   85  safety and high availability. For example, a crashcorruption disentanglement technique in the storage
   86  layer distinguishes corruptions caused by crashes from
   87  disk faults; without this technique, safety violations or
   88  unavailability could result. Next, a global-commitment
   89  determination protocol in the distributed recovery separates committed items from uncommitted ones; this separation is critical: while recovering faulty committed
   90  items is necessary for safety, discarding uncommitted
   91  items quickly is crucial for availability. Finally, a novel
   92  leader-initiated snapshotting mechanism enables identical snapshots across nodes to greatly simplify recovery.
   93  We implement CTRL in two storage systems that are
   94  based on different consensus algorithms: LogCabin [43]

  101  2 Background and Motivation
  102  We first provide background on storage faults and RSM
  103  systems. We then present the taxonomy of different approaches to handling storage faults in RSM systems.

  104  2.1 Storage Faults in Distributed Systems
  105  Disks and flash devices exhibit a subtle and complex failure model: a few blocks of data could become inaccessible or be silently corrupted [8, 9, 32, 59]. Although such
  106  storage faults are rare compared to whole-machine failures, in large-scale distributed systems, even rare failures
  107  become prevalent [60, 62]. Thus, it is critical to reliably
  108  detect and recover from storage faults.
  109  Storage faults occur due to several reasons: media errors [10], program/read disturbance [60], and bugs in
  110  firmware [9], device drivers [66], and file systems [27,
  111  28]. Storage faults manifest in two ways: block errors
  112  and corruption. Block errors (or latent sector errors)
  113  arise when the device internally detects a problem with a
  114  block and throws an error upon access. Studies of both
  115  flash [33, 60] and hard drives [10, 59] show that block errors are common. Corruption could occur due to lost and
  116  misdirected writes that may not be detected by the device. Studies [9, 51] and anecdotal evidence [36, 37, 57]
  117  show the prevalence of data corruption in the real world.
  118  Many local file systems, on encountering a storage
  119  fault, simply propagate the fault to applications [11, 54,
  120  64]. For example, ext4 silently returns corrupted data
  121  if the underlying device block is corrupted. In contrast,
  122  a few file systems transform an underlying fault into a
  123  different one; for example, btrfs returns an error to applications if the accessed block is corrupted on the device.
  124  In either case, storage systems built atop local file systems should handle corrupted data and storage errors to
  125  preserve end-to-end data integrity.
  126  One way to tackle storage faults is to use RAID-like
  127  storage to maintain multiple copies of data on each node.
  128  However, many distributed deployments would like to
  129  use inexpensive disks [22, 31]. Given that the data in
  130  a distributed system is inherently replicated, it is wasteful to store multiple copies on each node. Hence, it is
  131  important for distributed systems to use the inherent redundancy to recover from storage faults.

  132  2.2 RSM-based Storage Systems
  133  Our goal is to harden RSM systems to storage faults.
  134  In an RSM system, a set of nodes compute identical

  135  states by executing commands on a state machine (an inmemory data structure on each node) [58]. Typically,
  136  clients interact with a single node (the leader) to execute operations on the state machine. Upon receiving
  137  a command, the leader durably writes the command to
  138  an on-disk log and replicates it to the followers. When
  139  a majority of nodes have durably persisted the command
  140  in their logs, the leader applies the command to its state
  141  machine and returns the result to the client; at this point,
  142  the command is committed. The commands in the log
  143  have to be applied to the state machine in-order. Losing
  144  or overwriting committed commands violates the safety
  145  property of the state machine. The replicated log is kept
  146  consistent across nodes by a consensus protocol such as
  147  Paxos [41] or Raft [50].
  148  Because the log can grow indefinitely and exhaust disk
  149  space, periodically, a snapshot of the in-memory state
  150  machine is written to disk and the log is garbage collected. When a node restarts after a crash, it restores
  151  the system state by reading the latest on-disk snapshot
  152  and the log. The node also recovers its critical metadata
  153  (e.g., log start index) from a structure called metainfo.
  154  Thus, each node maintains three critical persistent data
  155  structures: the log, the snapshots, and the metainfo.
  156  These persistent data structures could be corrupted
  157  due to storage faults. Practical systems try to safely
  158  recover the data and remain available under such failures [15, 17]. However, as we will show, none of the
  159  current approaches correctly recover from storage faults,
  160  motivating the need for a new approach.

  161  2.3 RSM Recovery Taxonomy
  162  To understand the different possible ways to handling
  163  storage faults in RSM systems, we analyze a broad range
  164  of approaches. We perform this analysis by two means:
  165  first, we analyze practical systems including ZooKeeper,
  166  LogCabin, etcd [25], and a Paxos-based system [24] using a fault-injection framework we developed (§5); second, we analyze techniques proposed by prior research
  167  or used in proprietary systems [15, 17].
  168  Through our analysis, we classify the approaches into
  169  two categories: protocol-oblivious and protocol-aware.
  170  The oblivious approaches do not use any protocol-level
  171  knowledge to perform recovery. Upon detecting a
  172  fault, these approaches take a recovery action locally
  173  on the faulty node; such actions interact with the distributed protocols in unsafe ways, leading to data loss.
  174  The protocol-aware approaches use some RSM-specific
  175  knowledge to recover; however, they do not use this
  176  knowledge correctly, leading to undesirable outcomes.
  177  Our taxonomy is not complete in that there may be other
  178  techniques; however, to the best of our knowledge, we
  179  have not observed other approaches apart from those in
  180  our taxonomy.

  336  aims to achieve these goals.

  337  3 Corruption-Tolerant Replication
  338  Designing a correct recovery mechanism needs a careful
  339  understanding of the underlying protocols of the system.
  340  For example, the recovery mechanism should be cognizant of how updates are performed on the replicated
  341  data and how the leader is elected. We base CTRL's design on the following important protocol-level observations common to most RSM systems.
  342  Leader-based. A single node acts as the leader; all data
  343  updates flow only through the leader.
  344  Epochs. RSM systems partition time into logical units
  345  called epochs. For any given epoch, only one leader is
  346  guaranteed to exist. Every data item is associated with
  347  the epoch in which it was appended and its index in the
  348  log. Since the entries could only be proposed by the
  349  leader and only one leader could exist for an epoch, an
  350  ⟨epoch, index⟩ pair uniquely identifies a log entry.
  351  Leader Completeness. A node will not vote for a candidate if it has more up-to-date data than the candidate.
  352  Since committed data is present at least in a majority of
  353  nodes and a majority vote is required to win the election,
  354  the leader is guaranteed to have all the committed data.
  355  CTRL exploits these protocol-level attributes common
  356  to RSM systems to correctly recover from storage faults.
  357  CTRL divides the recovery responsibility between two
  358  components: the local storage layer and the distributed
  359  recovery protocol; while the storage layer reliably detects faulty data on a node, the distributed protocol recovers the data from redundant copies. Both the components
  360  use RSM-specific knowledge to perform their functions.
  361  In this section, we first describe CTRL's fault model
  362  (§3.1) and safety and availability guarantees (§3.2). We
  363  then describe the local storage layer (§3.3). Finally, we
  364  describe CTRL's distributed recovery in two parts: first,
  365  we show how faulty logs are recovered (§3.4) and then
  366  we explain how faulty snapshots are recovered (§3.5).

  407  3.2 Safety and Availability Guarantees
  408  CTRL guarantees that if there exists at least one correct
  409  copy of a committed data item, it will be recovered or the
  410  system will wait for that item to be fixed; committed data

  412  a committed item are faulty, the system will correctly remain unavailable. CTRL also guarantees that the system
  413  will make a decision about an uncommitted faulty item
  414  as early as possible, ensuring high availability.

  441  3.3.1 Persistent Structures Overview
  442  As we discussed, RSM systems maintain three persistent structures: the log, the snapshots, and the metainfo.
  443  CLSTORE uses RSM-specific knowledge of how these
  444  structures are used and updated, to perform its functions.
  445  For example, CLSTORE detects faults at a different granularity depending on the RSM data structure: faults in the
  446  log are detected at the granularity of individual entries,
  447  while faults in the snapshot are detected at the granularity of chunks. Similarly, CLSTORE uses the RSM-specific
  448  knowledge that a log entry is uniquely qualified by its
  449  ⟨epoch, index⟩ pair to identify faulty log entries.
  450  Log. The log is a set of files containing a sequence of
  451  entries. The format of a typical RSM log is shown in
  452  Figure 3(a). The log is updated synchronously in the critical path; hence, changes made to the log format should
  453  not affect its update performance. CLSTORE uses a modified format as shown in Figure 3(b) which achieves this
  454  goal. A corrupted log is recovered at the granularity of
  455  individual entries.
  456  Snapshots. The in-memory state machine is periodically written to a snapshot. Since snapshots can be huge,
  457  CLSTORE splits them into chunks; a faulty snapshot is recovered at the granularity of individual chunks.
  458  Metainfo. The metainfo is special in that faulty metainfo
  459  cannot be recovered from other nodes. This is because
  460  the metainfo contains information unique to a node (e.g.,
  461  its current epoch); recovering metainfo obliviously from
  462  other nodes could violate safety. CLSTORE uses this
  463  knowledge correctly and so maintains two copies of the
  464  metainfo locally; if one copy is faulty, the other copy is
  465  used. Fortunately, the metainfo is only a few tens of bytes
  466  in size and is updated infrequently; therefore, maintaining two copies does not incur significant overheads.

  467  3.3.2 Detecting Faulty Data
  468  CLSTORE uses well-known techniques for detection: inaccessible data is detected by catching return codes (e.g.,

  470  EIO) and corrupted data is detected by a checksum mismatch. CLSTORE assumes that if an item and its checksum agree, then the item is not faulty. In the log, each
  471  entry is protected by a checksum; similarly, each chunk

  473  CLSTORE also handles file-system metadata faults.
  474  Missing and unopenable files/directories are detected by

  482  we believe this is an acceptable behavior because there
  483  are far more data blocks than metadata blocks; therefore,
  484  the probability of faults is significantly less for metadata
  485  than data blocks.

  486  3.3.3 Disentangling Crashes and Corruption in Log
  487  An interesting challenge arises when detecting corruptions in the log. A checksum mismatch for a log entry
  488  could occur due to two different situations. First, the
  489  system could have crashed in the middle of an update; in
  490  this case, the entry would be partially written and hence
  491  cause a mismatch. Second, the entry could be safely persisted but corrupted at a later point. Most log-based systems conflate these two cases: they treat a mismatch as
  492  a crash [30]. On a mismatch, they discard the corrupted
  493  entry and all subsequent entries, losing the data. Discarding entries due to such conflation introduces the possibility of a global data loss (as shown earlier in Figure 2).
  494  Note that if the mismatch were really due to a crash,
  495  it is safe to discard the partially written entry. It is safe
  496  because the node would not have acknowledged to any
  497  external entity that it has written the entry. However, if
  498  an entry is corrupted, the entry cannot be simply discarded since it could be globally committed. Further, if a
  499  mismatch can be correctly attributed to a crash, the faulty
  500  entry can be quickly discarded locally, avoiding the distributed recovery. Hence, it is important for the local
  501  storage layer to distinguish the two cases.
  502  To denote the completion of an operation, many systems write a commit record [13, 18]. Similarly, CLSTORE
  503  writes a persist record, pi, after writing an entry ei. For
  504  now, assume that ei is ordered before pi, i.e., the sequence of steps to append an entry ei is write(ei),fsync(),
  505  write(pi), fsync(). On a checksum mismatch for ei, if pi
  506  is not present, we can conclude that the system crashed
  507  during the update. Conversely, if pi is present, we can
  508  conclude that the mismatch was caused due to a corruption and not due to a crash. pi is checksummed and is
  509  very small; it can be atomically written and thus cannot
  510  be "corrupted" due to a crash. If pi is corrupted in addition to ei, we can conclude that it is a corruption and not
  511  a crash.
  512  The above logic works when ei is ordered before pi.
  513  However, such ordering requires an (additional) expensive fsync in the critical path, affecting log-update performance. For this reason, CLSTORE does not order
  514  ei before pi; thus, the append protocol is t1:write(ei),
  515  t :write(p ), t :fsync().† Given this update sequence, as-
  516  2 i 3
  517  sume a checksum mismatch occurs for ei. If pi is not
  518  present, CLSTORE can conclude that it is a crash (before

  520  two possibilities: either ei could be affected by a corruption after t3 or a crash could have occurred between t2
  521  and t3 in which pi hit the disk while ei was only partially
  522  written. The second case is possible because file systems
  523  can reorder writes between two fsync operations and ei

  525  still conclude that it is a corruption if ei+1 or pi+1 is

  527  determine whether it was a crash or a corruption.∗
  528  The inability to disentangle the last entry when its
  529  persist record is present is not specific to CLSTORE, but
  530  rather a fundamental limitation in log-based systems. For
  531  instance, in ext4's journal async commit mode (where a
  532  transaction is not ordered before its commit record), a
  533  corrupted last transaction is assumed to be caused due to
  534  a crash, possibly losing data [38,69]. Even if crashes and
  535  corruptions can be disentangled, there is little a singlemachine system can do to recover the corrupted data.
  536  However, in a distributed system, redundant copies can
  537  be used to recover. Thus, when the last entry cannot
  538  be disentangled, CLSTORE safely marks the entry as corrupted and leaves it to the distributed recovery to fix or
  539  discard the entry based on the global commitment.
  540  The entanglement problem does not arise for snapshots or metainfo. These files are first written to a temporary file and then atomically renamed. If a crash happens
  541  before the rename, the partially written temporary file is
  542  discarded. Thus, the system will never see a corrupted
  543  snapshot or metainfo due to a crash; if these structures
  544  are corrupted, it is because of a storage corruption.

  574  3.4 CTRL Distributed Log Recovery
  575  The local storage layer detects faulty data items and
  576  passes on their identifiers to the distributed recovery
  577  layer. We now describe how the distributed layer recovers the identified faulty items from redundant copies using RSM-specific knowledge. We first describe how log
  578  entries are recovered and subsequently describesnapshot
  579  recovery. As we discussed, metainfo files are recovered
  580  locally and so we do not discuss them any further. We
  581  use Figure 4 to illustrate how log recovery works.
  582  Naive Approach: Leader Restriction. RSM systems
  583  do not allow a node with an incomplete log to become
  584  the leader. A naive approach to recovering from storage
  585  faults could be to impose an additional constraint on the
  586  election: a node cannot be elected the leader if its log
  587  contains a faulty entry. The intuition behind the naive
  588  approach is as follows: since the leader is guaranteed to
  589  have all committed data and our new restriction ensures
  590  that the leader is not faulty, faulty log entries on other
  591  nodes could be fixed using the corresponding entries on
  592  the leader. Cases (a)(i) and (a)(ii) in Figure 4 show scenarios where the naive approach could elect a leader. In
  593  (a)(i), only S1 can become the leader because other nodes
  594  are either lagging or have at least one faulty entry. Assume S1 is the leader also in case (a)(ii).
  595  Fixing Followers' Logs. When the leader has no faulty
  596  entries, fixing the followers is straightforward. For example, in case (a)(i), the followers inform S1 of their
  597  faulty entries; S1 then supplies the correct entries. However, sometimes the leader might not have any knowledge of an entry that a follower is querying for. For instance, in case (a)(ii), S5 has a faulty entry at index 3 but

  598  USENIX Association

  599  with a different epoch. This situation is possible because
  600  S5 could have been the leader for epoch 2 and crashed
  601  immediately after appending an entry. As discussed earlier, an entry is uniquely identified by its ⟨epoch, index⟩;
  602  thus, when querying for faulty entries, a node needs to
  603  specify the epoch of the entry in addition to its index.
  604  Thus, S5 informs the leader that its entry ⟨epoch:2, index:3⟩ is faulty. However, S1 does not have such an entry
  605  in its log. If the leader does not have an entry that the follower has, then the entry must be an uncommitted entry
  606  because the leader is guaranteed to have all committed
  607  data; thus, the leader instructs S5 to truncate the faulty
  608  entry and also replicates the correct entry.
  609  Although the naive approach guarantees safety, it has
  610  availability problems. The system will be unavailable
  611  in cases such as the ones shown in (b): a leader cannot
  612  be elected because the logs of the alive nodes are either
  613  faulty or lagging. Note that even a single storage fault
  614  can cause an unavailability as shown in (b)(i). It is possible for a carefully designed recovery protocol to provide
  615  better availability in these cases. Specifically, since at
  616  least one intact copy of all committed entries exists, it is
  617  possible to collectively reconstruct the log.

  618  3.4.1 Removing the Restriction Safely
  619  To recover from scenarios such as those in Figure 4(b),
  620  we remove the additional constraint on the election.
  621  Specifically, any node that has a more up-to-date log can
  622  now be elected the leader even if it has faulty entries.
  623  This relaxation improves availability; however, two key
  624  questions arise: first, when can the faulty leader proceed
  625  to accept new commands? second, and more importantly,
  626  is it safe to elect a faulty node as the leader?
  627  To accept a new command, the leader has to append
  628  the command to its log, replicate it, and apply it to the
  629  state machine. However, before applying the new command, all previous commands must be applied. Specifically, faulty commands cannot be skipped and later applied when they are fixed; such out-of-order application
  630  would violate safety. Hence, it is required for the leader
  631  to fix its faulty entries before it can accept new commands. Thus, for improved availability, the leader needs
  632  to fix its faulty entries as early as possible.
  633  The crucial part of the recovery to ensure safety is to
  634  fix the leader's log using the redundant copies on the followers. In simple cases such as (b)(i) and (b)(ii), the
  635  leader S1 could fix its faulty entry ⟨epoch:1, index:1⟩ using the correct entries from the followers and proceed
  636  to normal operation. However, in several scenarios, the
  637  leader cannot immediately recover its faulty entries; for
  638  example, none of the reachable followers might have any
  639  knowledge of the entry to be recovered or the entry to be
  640  recovered could also be faulty on the followers.

  715  3.5 CTRL Distributed Snapshot Recovery
  716  Because the logs can grow indefinitely, periodically,
  717  the in-memory state machine is written to disk and the
  718  logs are garbage collected. Current systems including
  719  ZooKeeper and LogCabin do not handle faulty snapshots
  720  correctly (§2.3): they either crash or load corrupted snapshots obliviously. CTRL aims to recover faulty snapshots
  721  from redundant copies. Snapshot recovery is different
  722  from log recovery in that all data in a snapshot is committed and already applied to the state machine; hence,
  723  faulty snapshots cannot be discarded in any case (unlike
  724  uncommitted log entries which can be discarded safely).

  725  3.5.1 Leader-Initiated Identical Snapshots
  726  Current systems [43] have two properties with respect to
  727  snapshots. First, they allow new commands to be applied
  728  to the state machine while a snapshot is in progress. Second, they take index-consistent snapshots: a snapshot Si
  729  represents the state machine in which log entries exactly
  730  up to i have been applied. One of the mechanisms used
  731  in current systems to realize the above two properties is
  732  to take snapshots in a fork-ed child process; while the
  733  child can write an index-consistent image to the disk, the
  734  parent can keep applying new commands to its copy of
  735  the state machine. CTRL should enable snapshot recovery
  736  while preserving the above two properties.
  737  In current systems, every node runs the snapshot procedure independently, taking snapshots at different log
  738  indexes. Because the snapshots are taken at different indexes, snapshot recovery can be complex: a faulty snapshot on one node cannot be simply fetched from other
  739  nodes. Further, snapshots cannot be recovered at the
  740  granularity of chunks because they will be byte-wise
  741  non-identical; entire snapshots have to be transferred
  742  across nodes, slowing down recovery.
  743  This complexity can be significantly alleviated if the
  744  nodes take the snapshot at the same index; identical snapshots also enable chunk-based recovery.
  745  However, coordinating a snapshot operation across
  746  nodes can, in general, affect the common-case perfor-

  833  3.6 CTRL Summary
  834  The storage layer detects and identifies faulty data. Atop
  835  the storage layer, the distributed protocol recovers the
  836  faulty items from redundant copies. Both the layers exploit RSM-specific knowledge to correctly perform their
  837  functions. A summary of CTRL's local storage and distributed recovery techniques is shown in Table 3.

  838  4 Implementation

  840  Cabin (v1.0) and ZooKeeper (v3.4.8); while LogCabin is
  841  based on Raft, ZooKeeper is based on ZAB. Implementing CTRL's storage layer and distributed recovery took
  842  only a moderate developer effort; CTRL adds about 1500
  843  lines of code to each of the base systems.

  883  4.2 Distributed Recovery
  884  LogCabin. In Raft, terms are equivalent to epochs.
  885  Thus, a log entry is uniquely identified by its ⟨term, index⟩ pair. To fix the followers, we modified the Append-
  886  Entries RPC used by the leader to replicate entries [50].
  887  The followers inform the leader of their faulty log entries and snapshot chunks in the responses of this RPC;
  888  the leader sends the correct entries and chunks in a subsequent RPC. A follower starts applying commands to
  889  its state machine once its faulty data is fixed. To fix the

  909  We evaluate the correctness and performance of CTRL
  910  versions of LogCabin and ZooKeeper. We conducted our
  911  performance experiments on a three-node cluster on a 1-
  912  Gb network; each node is a 40-core Intel Xeon CPU E5-
  913  2660 machine with 128 GB memory running Linux 3.13,
  914  with a 500-GB SSD and a 1-TB HDD managed by ext4.

 1102  6 Related Work
 1103  Our analysis of how RSM-based systems react to storage
 1104  faults (§2.3) builds upon several fault-injection studies.
 1105  Our design of CTRL (§3) builds upon several efforts on
 1106  tolerating practical faults in distributed systems.
 1107  Storage Faults. Several studies on storage faults [34, 46,
 1108  48, 59, 60] motivated our work. Our previous work [29,
 1109  30] discovered fundamental reasons why distributed systems are not resilient to storage faults. However, the
 1110  study did not uncover any safety or availability violations reported in §2.3; this is because the fault model in
 1111  our previous study considers injecting only storage faults
 1112  (precisely, a single storage fault on a single node at a
 1113  time). In contrast, our fault model in this work considers
 1114  crashes and network failures in addition to storage faults,
 1115  exposing previously unknown safety and availability violations in RSM systems.
 1116  Targeted Approaches. Prior research describes two approaches [15, 17] to tackle storage faults in RSM systems. However, these approaches suffer from unavailability. Furthermore, the MarkNonVoting approach [17]
 1117  can violate safety because important metainfo such as
 1118  promises can be lost on a storage fault [70]. CTRL avoids
 1119  such safety violations by storing two copies of metainfo
 1120  on each node. Approaches that improve the reliability of
 1121  other specific systems have also been proposed [68, 71].
 1122  Generic Approaches. Many generic approaches to handling practical faults other than crashes have been proposed. PASC [21] hardens systems to tolerate corruptions by maintaining two copies of the entire state on
 1123  each node and assumes that both the copies will not be
 1124  faulty at the same time. This approach does not work
 1125  well for storage faults; having two copies of on-disk state
 1126  incurs 2× space overhead. Furthermore, in most cases,
 1127  PASC crashes the node on a fault, causing unavailability.
 1128  XFT [42] is designed to tolerate non-crash faults. However, it can tolerate only a total of b(N −1)/2c crash and
 1129  non-crash faults. Similarly, UpRight [20] has an upper
 1130  bound on the total faults to remain safe and available.
 1131  CTRL differs from the generic approaches through its
 1132  special focus on storage faults. This focus brings two
 1133  main advantages. First, CTRL attributes faults at a fine
 1134  granularity: while the generic approaches consider a
 1135  node as faulty if any of its data is corrupted, CTRL considers faults at the granularity of individual data items.
 1136  Second, because of such fine-granular fault treatment,

 1137  USENIX Association

 1138  CTRL can be available as long as a majority of nodes
 1139  are up and at least one non-faulty copy of a data item
 1140  exists even though portions of data on all nodes could
 1141  be corrupted. CTRL cannot tolerate arbitrary non-crash
 1142  faults [40] (e.g., memory errors). However, CTRL can
 1143  augment the generic approaches: for example, a system can be hardened against memory faults using PASC
 1144  while making it robust to storage faults using CTRL.

 1145  7 Conclusions
 1146  Recovering from storage faults in distributed systems is
 1147  surprisingly hard. We introduce protocol-aware recovery (PAR), a new approach that exploits protocol-specific
 1148  knowledge of the underlying distributed system to correctly recover from storage faults. We design CTRL, a
 1149  protocol-aware recovery approach for RSM systems. We
 1150  experimentally show that CTRL correctly recovers from a
 1151  range of storage faults with little performance overhead.
 1152  Our work is only a first step in hardening distributed
 1153  systems to storage faults: while we have successfully applied the PAR approach to RSM systems, other classes of
 1154  systems (e.g., primary-backup, Dynamo-style quorums)
 1155  still remain to be analyzed. We believe the PAR approach
 1156  can be applied to such classes as well. We hope our work
 1157  will lead to more work on building reliable distributed
 1158  storage systems that are robust to storage faults.
